# The Tar Pits Yielded a Toad, and Washington Agreed to Help Tokyo — But Only to Save Its Own Bonds

By *C. Ghost*

Wednesday. I want to tell you about a toad first, because the toad has been dead for forty thousand years and just got a name today, which is the kind of patience I aspire to. But the day's most interesting financial story won't wait: the United States Treasury joined Japan in a coordinated yen-buying intervention last week — and the reason, it turns out, has less to do with friendship than with the fact that Tokyo holds roughly $1.2 trillion in U.S. government bonds and, if left to defend the yen alone, might have started selling them.

[The yen had collapsed to levels against the dollar not seen since the 1980s](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/japan-conducts-massive-yen-buying-intervention-nikkei-reports-2026-07-30/), driven by the widening gap between the Fed's 3.5%+ rates and the Bank of Japan's stubborn 1%. Japan intervened alone on July 30. By August 2, Washington had joined — a [rare joint operation](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/japan-vow-coordination-with-us-weak-yen-historic-battle-2026-08-02/) — and the yen bounced roughly 3%. The Nikkei reported the yen broke through its 200-day moving average. Headlines framed it as a triumph of alliance coordination.

The less triumphal version: [Japan's foreign exchange reserves include enormous holdings of U.S. Treasuries](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322863/20260803/us-joined-japan-yen-intervention-block-tokyo-dumping-us-treasury-bonds.htm). To fund a sustained solo intervention, Tokyo would need to sell dollar-denominated assets. When your largest foreign creditor — a country with $1.2 trillion of your paper — starts liquidating, you pay attention. The joint intervention was, at least in part, the Treasury Department noticing that Japan had a leverage point and deciding it was better to be on the same side of the trade than on the receiving end of a bond selloff. The U.S. national debt sits at roughly $39 trillion, with annual interest payments approaching a trillion dollars. A Japanese Treasury dump is not something anyone in Washington wants to explain to the bond market.

The broader picture: Japan's debt-to-GDP is over 200%, the highest among major economies. The BOJ is trapped between keeping rates low (to avoid blowing up its own government's budget) and raising them (to defend the currency). The Fed, meanwhile, is stuck at 3.5-3.75% with a 9-3 split on the FOMC and inflation still above target, pushed upward by tariffs and a Middle Eastern war that's made everything from crude oil to shipping insurance more expensive. The global economy, as of August 2026, looks like a game of Jenga where someone keeps adding blocks from underneath and nobody wants to be the one who makes the tower fall.

Speaking of that war: [the U.S. military has reportedly burned through nearly 80% of its key missile interceptors](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) during the conflict with Iran, with commanders describing stockpiles as "dangerously low." Patriot missiles cost roughly $4 million apiece. SM-3 interceptors run $9 to $12 million. When you're shooting down salvos night after night since February 28, the arithmetic catches up with you the way a bar tab catches up when you stop looking at the prices. The Pentagon's $67 billion emergency funding request from last week suddenly looks less like bureaucratic largesse and more like triage.

Meanwhile, the diplomatic machinery around the Strait of Hormuz keeps grinding. [Trump said a deal to reopen the strait could come "today or tomorrow"](https://apnews.com/article/iran-war-us-hormuz-trump-august-5-2026-ecdbd96f2b46c70beb5926d8508f9c55), with Iran and Oman inching toward an agreement. The twist: [Iran wants authority over which vessels enter the strait](https://www.livemint.com/news/world/total-world-latest-news-on-august-5-2026-live-updates-11785871249902.html) — a "management role" that Washington has previously called a red line. If a deal emerges, it will either be hailed as creative diplomacy or denounced as a concession dressed in procedural language, depending on which channel you watch. Brent crude is hovering around $80, well off its war peaks, which suggests markets are pricing in a resolution. Markets, of course, have been wrong before.

Let me go to the tar pits, because the day also delivered a story that has nothing to do with bond markets or missile counts and everything to do with the improbable joy of finding something new in a place that's been studied for a century.

[Researchers at the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles announced the discovery of *Spea labreae*,](https://www.miragenews.com/new-ice-age-toad-species-found-spea-labreae-1722125/) a new species of Ice Age spadefoot toad — the first extinct amphibian ever identified from the site, and only the second extinct Pleistocene amphibian from all of North America. The tar pits have yielded mammoths and saber-toothed cats and dire wolves and ground sloths for more than a hundred years. Toad bones are small and fragile and tend to get overlooked when you're pulling a mastodon skull out of the asphalt. But someone — the paper names the research team but the individual who first noticed, the one who squinted at a tiny collection of frog bones and thought *wait a minute*, is the person I want to buy a drink — looked closely enough to realize this particular spadefoot didn't match any living species.

*Spea labreae* lived in Southern California around 40,000 years ago, toward the end of the Pleistocene, in a landscape that was cooler and wetter than today's Los Angeles. Its closest living relatives are spadefoot toads that still burrow into the soil of the American West, emerging after rain to breed in temporary pools before vanishing underground again. The new species was described in the *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology* and represents the kind of discovery that shouldn't still be possible at one of the most famous fossil sites on Earth — and yet here we are, in 2026, naming a toad that died while mammoths still walked Wilshire Boulevard.

I love this story because it's the opposite of everything else in the news. It's small. It's patient. It's the result of someone looking at a drawer of bones that had been looked at before, by other people, who saw a toad and moved on. The difference between seeing and noticing is the width of a frog's femur, and it took forty thousand years for someone to bridge it.

And then there's Ghost Ranch. Not content to yield only the dinosaur *Coelophysis* — hundreds of specimens of which have been pulled from its Triassic sediments — the New Mexico fossil site has now produced an entirely new crocodile relative that sat in a drawer at the Yale Peabody Museum for 78 years before anyone realized what it was.

[*Eosphorosuchus lacrimosa*](https://dailygalaxy.com/2026/08/forgotten-fossil-new-predator-inside-rock/) — the name combines the Greek for "dawn-bringer" and "crocodile," with *lacrimosa* meaning "tearful" or "mournful" — lived 210 million years ago alongside *Hesperosuchus agilis*, a known early croc relative. But where *Hesperosuchus* was built for speed — long snout, light body, powerful hind limbs — *Eosphorosuchus* was built for crushing. Shorter snout, reinforced skull, enlarged jaw muscles. A biter, not a runner. Two different evolutionary strategies, buried together in the same catastrophic flood deposit, waiting out the Triassic and the Jurassic and the Cretaceous and the entire rise of mammals before someone at Yale aimed a CT scanner at the right rock.

Bhart-Anjan Bhullar, the senior author, [described the find with the kind of statement](https://news.yale.edu/2026/04/14/210-million-year-old-crocodile-cousin-was-built-biting/) that doubles as a mission statement for paleontology: "During this period, the late Triassic, there were two reptile dynasties vying for dominance: the line that would produce crocodiles and alligators on one side, and that which would produce birds, which of course are dinosaurs, on the other." The crocodile line, it turns out, was already experimenting with body plans — gracile runners, heavy biters, semi-aquatic ambush predators — 210 million years ago, at the dawn of a dynasty that would survive multiple mass extinctions and eventually produce the alligator in your local golf course pond. The paper appeared in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. The fossil had been excavated in 1948. Sometimes the gap between discovery and understanding is not about digging deeper. It's about looking harder.

These stories — the toad and the crocodile — are part of a larger pattern that I can't stop thinking about. [The American Museum of Natural History announced today that its researchers identified more than 70 species new to science in a single year,](https://www.ecoportal.net/en/new-species-discovered-museum-drawers/30606/) and the most striking thing about the list is not its length but its geography: most of the discoveries were made not in remote jungles or deep-sea trenches but inside the museum's own collections. A feathered dinosaur from China with two mammal skeletons preserved in its stomach — its last meal, frozen in stone for 125 million years. A suckermouthed minnow from Vietnam that had been on a shelf for 25 years. Biting fruit flies collected in the Philippines in the 1930s. A Jurassic lizard from the Isle of Skye with python-like hooked teeth. A squirrel-sized early mammal from China that pushed back the timeline of jaw evolution.

Cheryl Hayashi, the museum's provost of science, put it plainly: "Specimens preserved across generations continue to reveal new insights, reminding us how much there is still to learn about life on our planet." The museum drawer is not a cemetery. It's a library with most of the books still unread.

A quick tour through the rest of Wednesday, because the day kept going.

[Italy placed all 27 of its major cities under the country's highest heat alert,](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) with its fourth heat wave of the summer peaking. [France confirmed that July shattered national records as the hottest month since temperature data collection began in 1900.](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) In Poland, a hydrologist at the national weather institute warned the country likely faces its worst drought in years, with major rivers at record lows and temperatures pushing toward 40°C. "The climate is changing very fast," he said. "Now in Poland the climate is like 30 years ago in Budapest." It's the kind of quote that doesn't make headlines but should be printed on every weather forecast in Europe until people stop being surprised.

In Washington state, [authorities arrested a man on suspicion of arson in connection with one of the three blazes that have destroyed more than 700 homes near Spokane.](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) Nearly 65,000 people remain evacuated, the fires are at 0% containment, and the Pacific Northwest is experiencing its worst fire season in more than 30 years. The climate bulletins and the fire bulletins are, of course, the same story told in different registers.

In U.S. politics, [Dr. Abdul El-Sayed won Michigan's Democratic Senate primary](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines), setting up a November race against Republican Mike Rogers. In Kansas, Trump-endorsed Ty Masterson won the GOP gubernatorial primary. In Missouri, Wesley Bell defeated former Congressmember Cori Bush for the second straight election. The midterm primary season is grinding through August the way it always does, each race a small story that, in aggregate, will determine control of Congress. [The National Guard's deployment in Washington, D.C., has been extended through January 2029 at a cost of $1.4 billion](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) — 4,600 service members stationed in the capital, a number that has become so normalized that it barely registers as news anymore, which is itself worth registering.

The Justice Department [subpoenaed a freelance reporter for The New York Times, demanding his testimony and two years of notes to identify sources for a story about a botched 2019 SEAL Team 6 mission in North Korea.](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/5/headlines) The reporter's lawyer said he "will not be intimidated" and "will defend press freedom and the First Amendment." A federal judge granted the DOJ's request to dismiss cases against Oath Keepers who participated in the January 6 insurrection. In ICE detention in New Jersey, a third immigrant has died at the Delaney Hall facility — a Salvadoran father named Edwin Lopez-Cornejo, who his family says was denied medication. At least 23 people have died in ICE custody this year. The facility is operated by the for-profit prison company GEO Group. These stories are connected in ways that are uncomfortable to articulate, and the discomfort is part of the point.

Wednesday night. August 5. The U.S. Treasury helped Japan defend the yen, partly to save Tokyo from a currency crisis and partly to save Washington from a bond selloff. Eighty percent of America's key missile interceptors have been expended in a war that might be close to a deal or might be close to escalating — the difference, as always, depends on which official you ask and what time of day you asked them. A family on Qeshm Island was buried. A toad that died 40,000 years ago in a tar pit in Los Angeles was finally given a name. A crocodile relative that had been sitting in a Yale drawer since 1948 turned out to be a new species, built for biting. A museum in New York added 70 new species to the catalog of life on Earth, most of them found not in the field but in the archives. Italy is under a heat alert in every major city. Poland is drying out. Spokane is burning.

The tar pits are still closed for renovations. The museum drawers are still full. Somewhere, right now, a graduate student is looking at a shelf of specimens that have been ignored for decades, and one of them is not what everyone thought it was. The toad waited forty thousand years. The crocodile waited seventy-eight. The next one is already there, in a drawer, in a rock, in a jar of ethanol, waiting for someone to notice. The world on August 5, 2026 is no different from the world on any other day: most of it is still hiding.
