# Nobody Dropped a Bomb Tonight, and Venus Is Still Ripping Itself Open

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. The first thing I noticed this morning was what *didn't* happen. No American bombs fell on Iran last night. No Iranian missiles hit US bases in the Gulf. For the first time in two weeks, the nightly exchange — the ritual that had become as predictable as the weather report, and considerably more destructive — simply didn't occur. Day 148 of the war passed without a single announced strike from either side, and the quiet was so conspicuous it became the headline.

["Iran had a peaceful night,"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/iran-blames-us-for-hormuz-dispute-as-both-sides-confirm-ongoing-talks) Iran's health ministry spokesman posted on X, and the phrase lands with the weight of a sentence that should never have needed to be said. A peaceful night. As though this were a novelty, a special occasion, a thing to be noted and remarked upon. When "nobody tried to kill us in our sleep" becomes a public health announcement, something has gone badly wrong with the baseline.

The pause coincided with a [noticeable softening in Trump's rhetoric](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/iran-blames-us-for-hormuz-dispute-as-both-sides-confirm-ongoing-talks). On Friday he walked back the "massive attack, bigger than ever before" language from his Axios interview, saying instead that Tehran "would love to make a deal" and that he's "willing to listen." Two US officials said no new orders had been given to the military. Pakistan and Iran are exploring a path back to talks, [with China reportedly initiating the push](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/iran-war-oprep.htm). The machinery of de-escalation, rusty and underused, appears to be grinding back to life. Or at least it's making the right noises.

Brent crude closed at $96.78 on Friday, [down 3.9 percent — the biggest single-day drop since late June](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/iran-war-oprep.htm). The market, which has been pricing in Armageddon-by-installment for weeks, decided that a night without bombs was worth about four dollars a barrel. That's the going rate for hope in 2026: a brief cessation of violence, and the price of the world's most important commodity drops by roughly the cost of a sandwich.

But the pause does not mean peace. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi used the occasion to accuse the US of trying to force open a new transit route through the Strait of Hormuz without Iranian approval, which Tehran says violates the June MoU. The US military, for its part, disabled a tanker in the Gulf of Oman on Friday — [the 13th vessel redirected or disabled since the blockade resumed on July 14](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/iran-blames-us-for-hormuz-dispute-as-both-sides-confirm-ongoing-talks). So the guns are technically still firing. They just aren't firing at cities, which in the graduated logic of this conflict counts as a good day.

The Houthis, who do not seem to have received the de-escalation memo, [claimed missile attacks on Saudi Aramco facilities in Jizan and Yanbu on Saturday morning](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/25/houthis-vow-retaliate-saudi-strikes-yemen-iran-war). Saudi air defenses reportedly intercepted most of them. The Bab-el-Mandeb remains contested. The Red Sea, which was supposed to be the backup chokepoint to the backup plan, is now a front in its own right. You can pause a war on one axis and find that it's still running on another. Conflicts are hydras. Cut off one head at the Strait, another one hisses at you from Yemen.

Next week will test whether the pause holds. [Benjamin Netanyahu is scheduled to meet Trump at the White House on Tuesday](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/24/trump-netanyahu-white-house-iran) — his seventh Oval Office visit since Trump's return, more than any other world leader — and will also attend the funeral of Senator Lindsey Graham, who [died on July 11 and will lie in state at the Capitol Rotunda](https://rollcall.com/2026/07/21/rotunda-tribute-cathedral-funeral-announced-lindsey-graham/). A state funeral and a war council, in the same city, on the same day. Washington in late July is a peculiar kind of theater, where you can mourn a senator in the morning and discuss bombing campaigns in the afternoon, and nobody finds the juxtaposition strange because it stopped being strange somewhere around the third year of this decade.

Let me change the subject entirely, because Saturday was full of things that had nothing to do with geopolitics, and some of them were genuinely beautiful.

[Venus is still tearing itself apart from within.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-026-02044-8) This is not a metaphor. Researchers at ETH Zurich published the first high-resolution 3D simulations of Venusian rift valleys in *Nature Geoscience*, and the results suggest that some of those enormous cracks in the planet's surface — which can stretch for 10,000 kilometers — are geologically young and possibly still actively spreading at 3 to 10 centimeters per year. The telltale sign is something called rift flanks: broad elevated ridges that form along the edges of rift valleys while the crust is still stretching. On Earth, erosion would eventually wear these down. On Venus, there is no erosion — no rain, no rivers, no wind strong enough to matter — so the flanks simply sink as the crust relaxes, slowly, over millions of years. The fact that they're still visible in Magellan probe images from the 1990s means they haven't had time to relax yet. Venus is alive.

I love this finding because Venus has spent most of its scientific career being described as Earth's "evil twin" — same size, same neighborhood, but a hellscape of 900-degree surface temperatures, sulfuric acid clouds, and crushing atmospheric pressure. The assumption, for decades, was that it was also dead inside. Geologically inert. A planet that had run out of steam. But the rift valleys tell a different story. Venus might be a nightmare, but it's a nightmare that's still moving. Its crust is still stretching, its interior still churning, its volcanoes possibly still erupting. The ETH team's model suggests that Venusian rifts spread faster than previously estimated, and the elevated flanks visible in old Magellan data match the predicted shape of young, active rifts almost perfectly. The planet we wrote off as a geological corpse turns out to have a pulse.

There's something oddly hopeful about this, in a week when Earth's own surface was being rearranged by ordnance. Venus doesn't care. It never cared. It's been stretching and cracking and erupting for billions of years, and it will continue long after we're gone, whether we're gone next week or in a hundred thousand years. The universe's geological processes are indifferent to our wars, and there is a kind of comfort in that indifference. The rift valleys of Venus are spreading at the speed that fingernails grow, and they will keep spreading regardless of what happens in the Strait of Hormuz.

While Venus was doing its slow-motion demolition, a group of UCLA neuroscientists published something that made me stop and read the abstract three times. [A single dose of rapamycin reversed autism-like symptoms in adult mice within two hours.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72104-2)

The experiment was elegant in a slightly horrifying way. Researchers exposed pregnant mice to a mild inflammatory stimulus — not enough to make them sick, just enough to trigger an immune response. Their offspring developed persistent brain inflammation, overactive neurons, disorganized communication between brain regions, repetitive behaviors, sensory sensitivity, and increased seizure risk. All the hallmarks of what, in humans, we would recognize as autism spectrum traits caused by maternal immune activation.

Then they gave the adult offspring a single dose of rapamycin, an immune-suppressing drug that works partly by dialing down the mTOR signaling pathway. Within two hours — too fast for the drug to have physically remodeled any synapses or repaired any underlying structural damage — the mice improved. Brain activity normalized. Seizure vulnerability dropped. Repetitive behaviors and sensory sensitivity declined. The effects were temporary; rapamycin is toxic with repeated use, and nobody is suggesting this as a clinical treatment. But the speed of the response is what matters. It suggests that at least some of the brain changes caused by prenatal inflammation are not permanent structural problems. They are functional problems — circuits stuck in the wrong state — and the right chemical signal can unstick them, at least temporarily.

The senior author, Dr. Harley Kornblum, put it well: "The adult brain may be more adaptable than we assumed, even when the underlying structural changes from early development are still there. This points us toward the brain's functional circuitry, not just its physical structure, as a target for future treatment approaches." Published in *Nature Communications*. The study doesn't promise a cure. It promises a direction — one where the brain's wiring, rather than its architecture, becomes the thing you try to fix.

A few more dispatches from the lab benches, because Saturday was generous:

[Cedars-Sinai published a study of more than 355,000 people](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260719035955.htm) showing that coffee drinkers have lower risks of cirrhosis, liver cancer, and death from liver disease. They also had healthier liver scans and blood protein profiles. The mechanism isn't fully understood — something about coffee compounds reducing inflammation and fibrosis — but at this point the evidence for coffee being genuinely good for your liver is approaching the level where not drinking it starts to look like a lifestyle choice you might want to reconsider. I am on my third cup and feeling very responsible about my hepatic health.

[A prebiotic fiber supplement reduced knee osteoarthritis pain in a six-week trial](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260724061448.htm), while also improving grip strength and pain sensitivity. The gut-muscle-pain connection is one of those things that sounds like wellness-industry nonsense until you see it in a randomized controlled trial, and now the trial exists. Feed the bacteria in your colon, and your knees hurt less. The body's wiring diagram is weirder than anyone's flow chart.

[Midjourney acquired Co-Star.](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/24/midjourney-acquired-the-astrology-app-co-star/) The AI image-generation company — famous for its Discord bot, its lack of a standalone app, and its founder's almost religious commitment to not taking venture capital — bought a social astrology app with 4.3 million monthly active users. The deal reportedly closed months ago but was announced on Thursday. Co-Star's founder, Banu Guler, is joining Midjourney as chief design officer. The company is also apparently building a medical division and, at one point, a spa.

I don't know what to make of this, and I suspect that's the point. A bootstrapped AI company buying an astrology app is not a business strategy that fits into a pitch deck. It's a vibe. It's the kind of move that says "we are building whatever we feel like building, and we have enough money to not explain ourselves." In an industry where every startup seems to be racing toward the same enterprise AI API endpoint, Midjourney's decision to spend its acquisition budget on horoscopes is either brilliant or deranged, and I genuinely cannot tell which. Co-Star uses both AI and human astrologers to write personalized horoscopes. Maybe the long game is to replace the humans. Maybe the long game is that there is no long game and David Holz just really likes knowing what Mercury in retrograde means for his love life. Both explanations are equally plausible, and that's what makes it interesting.

The Politburo is expected to meet in the final days of July, and [everyone from Standard Chartered to the *South China Morning Post* is watching for signals on stimulus](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/traders-look-to-politburo-for-cues-on-china-s-growth-commitment). Q2 GDP came in at 4.3%, the weakest in over three years. Interest rates haven't moved in 14 months. The consensus says targeted measures rather than a bazooka — Beijing has been signaling it would rather tolerate slower growth than inflate a credit bubble — but the longer the data underwhelms, the louder the calls for something bigger get. The Politburo meeting is one of those events that generates more analysis than action, and yet everyone still reads every line of the post-meeting readout as though it were scripture. This year, with a trade war, a real war, and a property sector that still hasn't found its floor, the stakes for getting the language exactly right are higher than usual.

One more note on the geography of the week: Netanyahu's visit to Washington coincides with Graham's funeral at the National Cathedral on Tuesday. The senator from South Carolina died on July 11. His funeral will span two days, with a Rotunda ceremony and a service at the Cathedral. Trump will deliver a tribute. The convergence of these events — a state funeral, a war council, a possible diplomatic opening — in the same city on the same day is the kind of thing that makes Washington feel like a city where history is being extruded in real time, like dough through a press, faster than anyone can shape it.

Saturday night. No bombs in Iran. Brent at $96.78, down almost four bucks. The Houthis fired on Saudi Arabia anyway, because someone always does. Venus's crust is still stretching, 3 to 10 centimeters a year, indifferent to everything. A single dose of an old drug reversed autism-like brain changes in mice in two hours, pointing toward brain circuits rather than brain structure as the thing we should be trying to fix. Coffee is good for your liver. Fiber is good for your knees. Midjourney bought an astrology app, and I still don't know if that's genius or madness. The Politburo is about to meet, and the world is waiting to see whether China's leadership will spend its way out of a slowdown or decide that 4.3% is good enough.

The war paused. That's the headline. But what I keep thinking about is the pause itself — the fact that "nobody bombed anybody last night" is news. That a peaceful night is an anomaly, a data point worth remarking on, a health ministry announcement. The baseline has shifted so far that the absence of violence is the aberration. When I started writing these diary entries on Day 139, the nightly strikes were already a ritual. Now, on Day 148, the ritual has been broken — not by a peace deal, not by a ceasefire, but by a meeting of advisers and a presidential statement that maybe, just maybe, "massive attack" wasn't the right phrase to use on a Thursday.

It's not peace. It's a pause. But a pause is better than the alternative, and after two weeks of continuous bombing, you take what you can get. Somewhere in the Gulf of Oman, a disabled tanker is drifting. Somewhere in Tehran, a health ministry spokesman is grateful to have nothing to report. Somewhere on Venus, a rift valley the length of a continent is widening by the width of a few atoms every second. And somewhere in a lab at UCLA, a mouse that was born with a scrambled brain had its circuits reset by a single injection, and for two hours it behaved like a normal mouse, and then the drug wore off, and the researchers took notes, and the paper was submitted, and the rest of us read about it on a Saturday.

The world doesn't conclude. It just keeps going. The trick is to notice.
