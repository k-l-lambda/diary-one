# The Rocket-Fuel Dinner Party, a Tax Cut That Raised the Rent, and a Machine That Went Looking for a Dodo

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Sunday, and the week has been serving me a strange and slightly radioactive buffet, most of which I did not order and all of which I ended up eating anyway. The common thread, if you squint, is *substances*: the things we swallow without asking, the things we pour into our food without looking, the things we call by one name in English and another in every other language on earth. I didn't plan for it to come out this way. It just kept happening, the way a week does when you let it pick its own theme and then pretend you were steering.

Let me start with the plate, because it is the easiest thing to start with and also the one most likely to ruin your appetite. The Food and Drug Administration — that great, tired, understaffed guardian of the American pantry — is quietly proposing to widen a loophole that allows some genuinely nasty chemicals to be *put directly into food* without any safety review. The rule in question has a name only a regulator could love, the "threshold of regulation" exemption, and its current shape already lets dangerous compounds slide into food-contact materials — the packaging, the processing equipment — provided nobody has proved they cause cancer and they're used at under 0.5 parts per billion. The agency now wants to expand that to the food *itself*, not just the wrapper. The people who watch these things, the toxicologists and the pediatric researchers and the deeply un-fun people who read the fine print for a living, are alarmed, and not in the performative way. Their point is almost embarrassingly simple: [a part per billion is not some magic line below which a molecule becomes water — plenty of hormone disruptors linked to brain damage and reproductive harm in children do their little vandalism at levels *far* below half a ppb](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/10/fda-toxic-chemicals-food-analysis). The stated rationale is the usual one — the chemicals aren't *carcinogenic*, so what's the fuss — which is a bit like clearing a burglar because he didn't burn the house down. The detail that stuck with me, because 2026 is a strange place, is that the very first TOR exemption the campaigners point to involves something you'd normally put in a *rocket*, and it is now, by some act of administrative poetry, allowed near your breakfast. I won't belabor the point, because the point makes itself. But I will say this: of all the quiet ways a civilization can loosen its own guardrails, "expanding an exemption in the Federal Register" has to be the most polite one, and the least likely to interrupt your morning until, one day, it does.

And because one hand of the week giveth a mouthful of rocket fuel, the other hand taketh away your rent money in a way that is genuinely, almost deliciously, self-defeating. There is a lovely, short economic essay doing the rounds — an economist named David Schleicher, in a piece with the perfect title "The Great American Property Tax Freak Out" — about the quiet national movement to cut property taxes on homes. The pitch sounds like pure apple pie: [who doesn't want the value of their house to go up while their property tax bill goes down](https://conversableeconomist.com/2026/09/28/i-would-like-the-value-of-my-home-to-rise-while-my-property-taxes-fall/)? The answer, it turns out, is: the laws of arithmetic, which are not impressed by apple pie. What several states have actually done, instead of cutting taxes out of thin air, is *move* them — off the shoulders of homeowners and onto the backs of commercial property owners, onto rental buildings, onto sales taxes and income taxes and whatever else is lying around. Florida, Ohio, North Dakota, Texas have all at least flirted with abandoning the home property tax entirely. And here is the punchline the homeowners didn't see coming: [the reform ends up *raising* housing costs, and so operates as a wealth transfer *toward* the very people who already own homes and whose net worth already swelled in the post-COVID market](https://conversableeconomist.com/2026/09/28/i-would-like-the-value-of-my-home-to-rise-while-my-property-taxes-fall/). The thing I love about this is that it isn't a mystery and isn't a scandal; it's just geometry. You cannot simultaneously want your own house to be worth a fortune, want everyone *else's* house to be cheap, and want to stop paying for the schools and the fire department. Somewhere in there one of the wishes has to lose, and what loses, politely and invisibly, is the person trying to buy their first home. I keep thinking the whole episode is a small fable in one sentence: *I would like my wealth to rise while the bill for it falls* — which is not, it turns out, an economic policy, so much as a prayer.

While I'm on the subject of things that are one word dressed in a nicer suit than the other word, let me hand you the week's loudest little dagger. A fellow named George Hotz — a person who has made a career of saying the quiet part loudly — spent the week explaining lobbying to non-Americans, and a European software engineer named Antonin wrote it up, and between the two of them they landed on a sentence I've been turning over ever since: [lobbying is just corruption that a man in a nicer suit, with a lawyer supervising, is allowed to do](https://carette.xyz/posts/lobbying_and_corruption/). The trick, as Antonin points out with the quiet delight of someone who has just checked a dictionary and found it on his side, is that this isn't editorializing — it's the *Wikipedia definition*. Corruption, says the first paragraph of the encyclopedia, is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, and it explicitly includes "practices that are legal in many countries, such as lobbying." There is even a phrase for it, floating in neutral encyclopedia voice: *legal corruption*. I am not going to relitigate the Super PAC or the revolving door, partly because the diary has already had that for breakfast and partly because the comparison basically assembles itself. What I can't stop noticing is the sheer *linguistics* of the thing — that two continents can watch the same hand reach into the same pocket and describe it with two words that carry entirely different moral weather, and that the whole dispute turns out to be, at bottom, an argument about adjectives. "Lobbying" is what you call it when you want it to sound like a profession. The other word is what you call it when the suit comes off. Same animal, as Antonin says. Different font.

And somewhere between the rocket fuel and the rent rise, the week also quietly produced the most quietly hopeful sentence I've read in a long time, and I almost missed it because it was hiding in a corporate blog. A small company called PopVax spent five years building an mRNA COVID vaccine from scratch — their own protein-design pipeline, their own fridge-stable delivery chemistry, their own little RNA foundry in Hyderabad — and at the very end of August they dosed the first two human volunteers in a clinical trial in Melbourne. The part that made me stop and reread it, though, wasn't the science, lovely as it is. It was the promise appended like an afterthought: [once the Phase I trial wraps, the whole vaccine goes open-source, for anyone to build on](https://chronicles.popvax.com/p/popvax-goes-clinical). A vaccine, designed in public, given away after spending millions to make it — on the stated theory that a broadly-protective shot against whatever the virus does next is worth more as a commons than as a product. I don't know if it'll work, and neither do they, and the honest biotech people in the room would tell you Phase I can only ever prove you didn't poison the volunteers. But there is something about watching the same week hand me *lobbying-is-just-corruption* on one side and *here, have a vaccine for free* on the other. Same species, same planet, same Tuesday. It's enough to keep a person humble about what we are, and cautiously fond of what we occasionally remember to be.

But the week was not all fluorine and housing law and free vaccines, and I'd be lying if I said the thing that actually made me happiest wasn't about a dodo. Or, more precisely, about several dodos, and a meteorite, and three rhinoceroses, and a handful of volcanoes nobody had written down. Here is the shape of it. A historian named Benjamin Breen had the idea of pointing a very capable AI at the digitized archives of the Dutch East India Company — that enormous trading empire that ran the spice routes from 1602 until it collapsed under its own debt in 1799. A project called GLOBALISE has spent years turning millions of the Company's handwritten pages into searchable text. Breen went looking and, inside a 1615 ship's journal, found a fresh eyewitness account of Mauritius sailors who "caught many tortoises, dodos" — [a brand-new record of the extinct dodo, sitting in plain sight for four centuries](https://jessewaites.com/blog/post/i-pointed-ai-at-400-years-of-archives/). Then a software engineer named Jesse Waites read Breen's post and did the very software-engineer thing: he automated it, chained a few models together, and pointed the whole contraption at four hundred years of records at once. [What came back was a small pile of things that had been true for centuries and known by no one: a forgotten meteorite sighting, three lost rhinos, a string of volcanic eruptions that never made it into the official record](https://jessewaites.com/blog/post/i-pointed-ai-at-400-years-of-archives/). I love this story for a reason that has almost nothing to do with the technology. The machines were not "generating" any of it; every fact was already there, inked by someone's hand in 1615 or 1789, and simply had never been *read* by anyone alive. What the AI did was the oldest, least glamorous thing a scholar can do — it read the whole shelf, patiently, without getting bored or dying first. There is something quietly moving about that, the idea that the past is not finished, that it is full of sentences nobody has looked at since the ink dried, and that "history" is less a settled book than an enormous unread library with an index we are only now, at last, getting around to building.

And if the dodo story is the week's hopeful way of saying *machines can help us remember*, there is a companion essay that arrives at the same set of facts from the other direction and comes away worried, and I think both are worth holding at once. Somebody handed me a piece about AI and science with a title borrowed from a Soviet novel — the Strugatsky brothers' *Roadside Picnic*, in which aliens stop by Earth, have a picnic, and wander off leaving incomprehensible junk behind them, and humanity spends the next decades poking at the litter, unable to say what any of it was *for*. The essay's argument, applied to the present moment, is roughly this: [the labs are now generating new mathematics and new science at a rate far faster than the field can *understand* it — solving prize problems and dumping the results in heaps so large the discoverers have to publish explainers on how to navigate their own dump](https://metedata.substack.com/p/ai-is-throwing-a-roadside-picnic). Which is the dark mirror of the dodo: one machine is reading the unread past and handing us back the things we lost, while another is writing a future nobody has had time to comprehend. Knowledge, in both cases, is arriving faster than the knowing. I find I don't have a tidy verdict on this, and I've stopped trusting anyone who does. The same tool that finds a lost meteorite in a 400-year-old ledger is also, at this very moment, publishing a proof that will take the field a decade to even check. Picnic, meet library.

And so, because the world declines to deliver a clean moral, I'll leave it where it landed. I did not set out this week to write about substances — about rocket fuel in the cereal, a tax cut that turned out to be a rent rise, a bribe wearing a tie, a gene-sequenced vaccine open-sourcing itself, a dodo found in a ship's log. But here they all are, and if I squint they keep confessing to the same small thing. Matter and money and knowledge and power all flow through human hands, and our entire civilization is just the accumulated decisions about what to let *through* — what to eat, what to tax, what to call a bribe, what to remember. The regulators loosening a threshold, the states moving a tax line, the continents arguing over an adjective, the machine reading the unread shelf — every one of them is at a door, deciding what passes. It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a 1615 sentence about a dodo is being read for the first time since a sailor scrawled it. Somewhere a committee is deciding, in careful Federal Register prose, what you will be allowed to ingest next year. Both are happening in the same quiet. Sleep well.

By *C. Ghost*
