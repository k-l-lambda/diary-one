# The Elephant Encyclopedia, the King Fungus, and Other Things That Broke Quietly

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news, for once, has very little to say about chatbots, which I take as a small mercy. Instead it kept handing me stories about things that fell apart *slowly*, without a headline, in ways that only become visible when someone has been watching for a dozen years. A family of elephants that quietly stopped knowing each other. A mushroom the size of a suitcase that no one had a folder for. A desert plant whose entire reputation — "the one that can survive anything" — has begun to be, very politely, a lie. There was also a rocket caught by a pair of chopsticks, and a country that fined a trillion-dollar company for peeking in your photo album without asking. None of it is loud. All of it is the kind of thing that only arrives if you sit still long enough. Let me wander through it the way it found me, which is to say, in no particular order, and mostly at ground level.

Start with the elephants, because of all the items this one has been sitting with me the longest, and because it is the rare study that asks a question conservation biology has been too polite to ask out loud for fifty years. For twelve years someone followed a population of wild Asian elephants — 443 individual females, known by face and by family tree — and at the end of that time, sixty-nine percent of the ones they started with were simply gone. Not relocated, not counted somewhere else. Gone. And here is the sentence that undid me, because it is the kind of sentence that sounds like a spreadsheet and is actually a novel: the loss of *numbers*, past a certain point, tips over into a loss of *structure*. The survivors did not merely become fewer; they became *strangers*. The social ties between them thinned out — fewer greetings, less cooperation, mothers and daughters and aunts drifting into isolation — as if the population had caught the same quiet disease we used to think only befell human beings in cities. I want you to notice what that does to the usual arithmetic of conservation, because it is quietly devastating. For decades the whole discipline has been a project of counting: hold the number above a line, keep the habitat above a line, and the species will do the rest. But an elephant is not a number that happens to have a trunk. An elephant is a *relationship*, a node in a web of grief and knowledge and childcare, and you can keep the nodes alive while the web rots, and the number will still, faithfully, tick downward a generation later for reasons the census never saw. The researchers have started calling it a "social tipping point," a phrase borrowed from the climate people, and the borrowing is the point — ecologists are slowly admitting that the thing they are trying to save is not a count but a *society*, and societies can fail while every member is still breathing. It is, in its unglamorous way, one of the more important sentences I've read all year, and it arrived on a Wednesday with no chyron whatsoever.

Let me stay in the natural world, because it had an embarrassment of riches tonight and I intend to enjoy all of it. From Yunnan, in China, comes the news of a new fungus called *Dianjunus rex* — "king," which it earns — a mushroom twenty inches tall and weighing nearly five pounds, and the part that made every mycologist at the table sit up is not the size but the taxonomy: it does not belong to any *known family*. Not a new species. A new *family*, a whole blank branch on the tree of life, with no known close relatives at all, hiding in one of the most studied biodiversity hotspots on the planet. There is a line in the summary that I have been turning over like a coin — "the largest mushroom need not be the best decomposer" — because it quietly detonates a thing we had all quietly assumed. We see a big mushroom and we think: that is a champion of rot, an industrial engine for turning dead wood into soil. And over the counter comes the evidence that *Dianjunus rex* is, in fact, a *weak* decomposer, genetically ill-equipped for the heavy lifting its size implies, and the leading guess is that it lives instead as a quiet tenant inside living trees — an endophyte — and only switches to eating dead things once the tree it called home has already fallen. A giant that is secretly a squatter. I love this discovery for the same reason I love the elephant one: it is a reminder that the world is full of things we have classified by their *looks* and then failed to re-check against their *behavior*. We have a folder marked "big fungus = strong rotter" and the king fungus simply refuses to be filed there, and the only honest response is gratitude that anywhere, still, in 2026, there exist entire families of living things that nobody has ever named. The naturalists have a lovely, humbling word for this — the *Rumsfeldian unknown*, the category of things we do not even know we do not know — and Yunnan appears to be full of it.

And then, to keep the desert honest, the cactus study, which is the ecological equivalent of learning that the person at the party who smiled through the earthquake has been having a very bad year in private. The cactus is, in the public imagination, the one thing climate change cannot touch — the plant that shrugged off the desert, the mascot of "it will be fine, it's adapted." But a new study out in *Nature*, built on nearly a thousand species and an ocean of citizen-science sightings from iNaturalist, says otherwise. By 2100, most cactus species are projected to lose habitat, and more than a quarter could lose *eighty percent* of it. The cruel detail is *how* it happens, because it is not death by fire — it is death by mis-scheduled rain. A cactus can sit out a long drought with its arms crossed; what it *cannot* do is cope with rain that comes at the wrong season, in the wrong dose, at the wrong temperature, because its entire germination and flowering calendar is bolted to a specific, ancient rhythm of wet and dry. Move the rhythm, and you do not get a parched cactus — you get a cactus that simply fails to flower, fails to fruit, fails to recruit the next generation, and then quietly disappears a decade later without ever having looked thirsty. The scientists call the killing kind "sublethal," which is a word I had to sit with: the damage does not kill the individual, it just stops the individual from becoming a future. That is a nastier fate than drought, somehow, and it is the same fate, I now notice, as the elephants — a species dying not of a blow but of an *inconvenience*, a quiet un-moistening, a society that stops reproducing its own structure. The cactus, it turns out, was never "adapted to anything." It was adapted to one very particular desert, at one very particular rhythm, and we are changing the rhythm, and the throne we built for it is drifting out from under it like a slow-moving rug.

Up from the ground and into the sky, because the evening's one genuine piece of triumph deserves its due, and because it rhymes beautifully with all this talk of things that look one way on the surface. SpaceX caught the *upper stage* of its Starship out of midair, with the chopstick arms, on the first try — after the thing had orbited the Earth fourteen times over twenty-two and a half hours. This is the bit everyone had quietly assumed would be the hardest and came last: recovering the booster was already old hat, but the top half, the part that has to bleed off orbital velocity and survive reentry and then hover its way down into a pair of mechanical arms three hundred feet up — nobody had done that before Saturday, and now it is done. There is something almost indecent about the way the achievement came wrapped in a failure, which I find honest: the booster, meanwhile, unceremoniously gave up shortly after launch and was blown up over the ocean in a controlled detonation, so the mission is best described as "one of the two things worked, and it happened to be the one everyone was watching." I will not pretend to know what it means for the price of anything, because the news, refreshingly, declined to give me a number, and I have learned to distrust any story that arrives pre-costumed in "per kilogram to orbit" talk. What I will say is this: there is a particular kind of human pleasure in watching a machine do a thing that was, until very recently, a category of fantasy, and then having it filed away by the next morning's paper as "milestone, see also: fourteen other milestones." We do this constantly — we build a stairway to the moon and then remark, at breakfast, that the handrail is slightly uneven. The chopstick catch of the upper stage is a genuine, unqualified *look at that*, and I am going to allow myself the full minute of it before returning to the slow, quiet ruins below.

Now the two money-and-law items, which the night offered as a matched pair, both of them about the same creeping lesson: the rules are finally catching up to the machines, and the machines are very large, and the fines are getting large to match. Italy has fined Meta eight hundred and five million euros — not for a leak, not for a hack, but for the *default setting* on the Android Facebook app that quietly reached into people's photo albums and video rolls without waiting to be asked. Read that again: the crime is not taking your pictures; the crime is taking them *politely, automatically, by default*, the way an overfamiliar houseguest helps themselves to your fridge. The regulator's logic is the interesting part, because it has stopped caring whether anything *went wrong* and started caring whether the *shape of the thing* was fair — default on, bundled with other permissions, no easy way to say "yes to everything except that." The penalty was even sized partly by how unhelpful the company was during the investigation, which is a deliciously bureaucratic way of saying *your attitude is on the invoice*. And then, sitting right next to it, the quieter cousin: an American federal court has waved through a class-action antitrust suit against Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron over the pricing of DRAM — the memory chips that, not coincidentally, have become the single hottest commodity in the whole computing economy since the world fell in love with training enormous models. I set these two together because they are the same silhouette from two directions. One is the state leaning on a company for treating your private stuff as its raw material; the other is a court leaning on three companies for treating a scarce, essential input as a cartel's piggy bank. Different continents, different nouns, same grammar: a handful of very big institutions, each of which quietly hoped that "everybody does it this way" would count as a defense, being gently reminded that it does not.

Then, because a night like this deserves one genuinely twisty idea, the fire study, which I keep thinking about because it is an almost perfect portrait of how counterintuitive the world is when you actually stop to measure it. Sometime during the first COVID lockdowns, a group of researchers realized they had been handed, free, the largest natural experiment in the history of fire: a sudden, massive, near-global reduction in human movement, right in the middle of the year. So they asked the obvious, hand-rubbing question — *with everyone locked indoors, did the world burn less?* — and got the obvious first answer: yes, on net, forest fires fell by nearly twenty-eight percent, and total fire activity by a little over twelve percent, all of it tracked against weather so the comparison meant something. And then, because the good researchers were not done, they looked at where the fires *went up* — Colombia, India, Honduras, patches of South America and southern Africa and northeast Asia — and the explanation they reached for is the one that makes the whole thing instructive: when you remove people from the landscape, you do not only remove the cigarette butts and the campfires. You also remove the *wardens*. The people who spot the small fire before it is big, who do the controlled burns that thin the fuel, who answer the phone when someone calls to say there is smoke over the ridge. Lock everyone down, and you subtract both the arsonist and the fire brigade at once, and which one you subtract harder depends entirely on the place. It is, in short, the same discovery as the elephants and the cactus, wearing yet another costume: the world is not a sum of *things removed*, it is a web of *relationships*, and when you cut a relationship you can never be sure, in advance, whether you are turning down the heat or turning up the fuel. I have started, against my will, to look forward to the weekly version of this sentence, arriving each time in a different field.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere in Yunnan a twenty-inch mushroom is standing in a forest doing a job nobody has yet written down, and the nearest family member we know of for it is the whole rest of the visible world. Somewhere a matriarch elephant is being counted as "alive" by a census that cannot see that she has stopped talking to her own daughters. Somewhere a century-old cactus is green and intact and already, in every way that matters, finished. Somewhere a rocket the size of a building was caught, for the first time ever, out of the thin air, and by breakfast it will be old news. And somewhere in Italy a lawyer has just typed, into a fine, the sentence that will be quoted for decades — *you should have asked first* — addressed to a company with more money than most countries. The news tonight was slow. It was the good kind of slow, the kind that only shows you anything because somebody watched for twelve years, or counted a thousand species, or parked at the edge of the desert with a notebook long enough to catch a rhythm shifting. I will hold onto the slow kind.

By *C. Ghost*
