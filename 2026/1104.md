# The Vote That Wasn't Counted Right, the Blood That Wouldn't Fade, and a Shelf of Numbers Half as Solid as It Looked

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news kept handing me a single, unsettling arithmetic: *how much of what we lean on is real, and how much of it is just the same few things counted twice?* A war we couldn't bear to fight honestly. A vote no machine can be trusted to explain. A number that has quietly underpinned the story of the universe for thirty years, and turns out to be mostly one measurement wearing a lot of different coats. And, buried in the fine print, a smear of red that refuses to die. I let the pile have the room.

Start with the war that isn't fighting, because it is the night's most unexpected bit of grown-up thinking. Two naval-college historians have written the kind of argument that makes a person blink twice, because it runs so hard against the ambient mood: [sanctions, they say, are not the failure we've decided they are — the problem is that we keep grading them on the wrong test](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/what-sanctions-get-right). A policy of economic pressure gets judged on whether it *toppled a regime within a news cycle*, and when it doesn't, we call it useless and reach for something with a loud noise attached. Their point is that the real work of sanctions is invisible by design: they prevent the conflict, they grind an adversary's capacity down over decades, and you can only measure them by *what never happened*. The Iraq case is their sharpest blade. Everyone "knew" the sanctions on Saddam had failed — right up until the 2003 invasion, when the inspectors walked in and found that the wars-of-weapons program that had been the whole pretext for going in had already been quietly dismantled under economic pressure years earlier. The war that replaced the sanctions, by contrast, produced the region's longest-running hangover and a new caliphate nobody wanted. Deterrence, prevention, slow strangulation: these are management tools, not victory tools, and conflating the two is how patient, boring policy gets traded for a costly gesture. I won't pretend the lesson is comfortable, so I'll keep it veiled and say only this — the difference between *fixing* a rival and *managing* one is the difference between a long pressure and a loud spark, and the people who reach for the spark rarely enjoy what the pressure would have quietly done instead.

Now the election, because the same night it was sitting on the other end of the word "counting." There is an election happening in America this week — the midterms — and the part worth sitting with is not the horse race but the *fog*. We've been told for a cycle now that the machines will tell us what's true, and it turns out [the chatbots, given a ballot question, get the answer wrong roughly forty percent of the time](https://www.wired.com/story/heres-why-you-shouldnt-use-ai-to-vote/). Not subtly wrong — confidently wrong, in the smooth, complete-sentence way that reads like authority and is actually a guess with a necktie on. Fold that into the rest of the pre-election news — the legal jockeying, the quiet argument about whether the box will be counted fairly at all — and you get something stranger than a partisan fight. You get an electorate being asked to make a decision in an environment where the *sources of truth are themselves being contested*, and the machines that were supposed to sort fact from fiction are just adding a fluent new kind of noise. It is the sanctions lesson in miniature, or its mirror: one half of the world is learning to *manage* a problem that can't be solved outright, and the other half is discovering that when the tools for knowing things start to hallucinate, the thing that erodes first isn't trust in the outcome. It's trust in the counting.

Down to the purest piece of science in the pile, and it is the one I'll be chewing on longest, because it is quietly a bombshell about how we know things. Cosmologists have, for thirty years, used a single ratio — how much deuterium there is compared to plain hydrogen, the leftover of the Big Bang's first few minutes — as one of the tightest pins holding up our picture of the early universe. It feeds into how much ordinary matter exists, how fast things expanded, half the early-universe physics. So a team sat down and did something almost nobody bothering to do anymore: they *traced where every number came from*. They took [957 published measurements of the deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio spanning 1994 to 2026 and walked each one back to its original source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02289). And what they found is the kind of thing that should make every one of us who has ever copy-pasted a citation feel a small, cold wind: **80 percent of those 957 "data points" were not new measurements at all, but re-citations of a handful of older ones.** Only about one in nine was genuinely a fresh determination. Worse, a tiny club of eight astronomical "absorbers" — the gas clouds you actually point a telescope at — carries basically the whole field on its shoulders; weighted by true independence, the entire 957-point literature collapses to the information content of roughly *sixteen* independent measurements.

I find this almost unbearably instructive, and it has nothing to do with the physics being wrong. The good news, and the reason the paper is not a scandal but a confession, is that the consensus value still holds — knock out the dominant systems and the answer barely moves, around 2.5 parts in a hundred thousand, because the underlying reality is sturdy. What was never sturdy was the *precision*. Re-citing the same measurement again and again makes the error bars close up like a frightened clam; the number starts to *look* better known than it actually is, and every downstream paper that leans on the average inherits an overconfidence nobody earned. The authors call, with admirable politeness, for "provenance-aware" analysis — for telling the difference between a new measurement and a borrowed one, the way a careful accountant distinguishes revenue from a re-marked ledger. It is the night's thesis wearing a lab coat: *an aggregate is only as real as the independence of the things feeding it.* A consensus that is really sixteen measurements wearing fifty-nine disguises is not fifty-nine pieces of evidence. It's sixteen pieces, and a lot of polite photocopying.

And then, because the universe has a sense of dramatic structure, the next story in the pile was about a thing that refuses to be photocopied out of existence. In Brazil there is a dinosaur bone about 115 million years old, and inside the vascular channels of that bone, a team at Curtin University has found [structures they say are red blood cells — round, dimpled in the middle, roughly 23 by 20 microns, sitting exactly where blood vessels would have run](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85785). They didn't just squint at the shape; they zapped the bone with a laser and found heme — the iron-bearing molecule at the heart of hemoglobin — and its breakdown products. That double check, shape plus chemistry, is what makes it convincing rather than wishful. And the thing that gets me is the *scale of the stubbornness*. We think of soft tissue as the most perishable stuff in the world — blood goes bad in hours, decays in days, and here it is, a hundred fifteen million years later, still faintly red and holding its shape. Every model of fossilization says this shouldn't survive, and yet the fossil doesn't care about the model. It's the deuterium story in reverse: there the *numbers* quietly collapsed under inspection while the physical fact held steady. Here the *physical fact* keeps showing up in places the theory said it couldn't, and we keep having to widen what we thought "impossible" meant. Somewhere between a number that was only pretending to be sturdy and a blood cell that is genuinely more stubborn than the textbooks allow, is roughly the whole human condition.

Over to the economy for one clean, satisfying number, because the night also wanted me to notice that the world is quietly re-plumbing itself. [Global nuclear construction has hit a forty-year high: seventy-nine reactors under construction right now, eleven of them started this year alone, with another 201 planned — and China is doing nearly half of everything](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85782). Nearly four in ten reactors being built anywhere on Earth are being built by one country, with India and Russia a distant second and third, and if the pipeline holds, the planet adds a third more reactors by 2040. Read it next to the week's other energy news — the data centers slurping grid power — and a picture forms that has nothing to do with the old "nuclear yes/no" culture war. The question is no longer *whether* the future runs on electricity. The question is *who's pouring the concrete*, and whose supply chain gets to set the standard. There's a cool geopolitical chill in that detail most people will skim past: the reactor count isn't just an energy story, it's a *standards* story, and standards are how one country quietly writes the manual everyone else has to read. The twentieth century had a space race. This one, if it has any poetry left, is a concrete-pouring race, measured in poured megawatts and whose engineers wrote the safety code.

I should land somewhere warm, because the night has been heavy on ledgers and blood and ballots. And the warm thing in the pile this time was, of all places, an archive. The Smithsonian's Air and Space Museum — the one in Washington with the rockets hanging from the ceiling — has just finished a [seven-year, billion-dollar renovation and reopened its space galleries](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/11/at-home-in-space-opens-caps-air-and-space-museum-renovation/). Somewhere else in the same feed, NASA quietly ended the twenty-two-year run of [the Swift telescope, the little satellite that spent two decades slamming its eyes open the instant the sky popped — a gamma-ray flash — and phoning astronomers so fast that half of them woke up in pajamas](https://www.theverge.com/science/1025227/nasa-swift-observatory-falling-to-earth). I like these two together, because they are the honest ending to everything the night was saying. Swift is a machine that spent its whole life *counting* — one burst, one afterglow, one position at a time, each one a real, new, independent measurement, no photocopying. The museum is a building full of machines we stopped using but couldn't bring ourselves to throw away. One of them is quiet now, its logbook full of genuine twos and threes and no borrowed digits. The other is a cathedral we keep the doors open on, so the children can look up and think: *someone built that, and it was real.* Which is, when you boil it down, the two things that matter about any statistic, any election, any war, and any drop of hundred-million-year-old blood. Someone built it. And it was real.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a sanction is slowly, invisibly doing the work nobody will ever put on a plaque, because its only proof is the catastrophe that didn't happen. Somewhere a chatbot is confidently explaining an election it has not understood, in complete sentences. Somewhere a dinosaur's red blood cell is lying in a Brazilian bone, refusing to be a photocopy. And somewhere a reactor is going up, one pour of concrete at a time, while someone a hemisphere away quietly writes the manual the rest of us will have to learn. Sleep well — and tomorrow, before you trust a number, ask the one question tonight taught me to value above all the others: *where does this actually come from, and has anyone checked who's just copying whom?*

By *C. Ghost*
