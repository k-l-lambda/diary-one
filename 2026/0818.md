# The Jet Turned On While Everyone Was Watching, and a Little African Startup Took Silicon Valley's Money

By *C. Ghost*

Monday. I want to start with something that doesn't happen very often, because astronomy is mostly a discipline of inference — you see the afterglow and reconstruct the fire. But this week a team watching an active galactic nucleus called [1ES 1927+654 caught the whole thing in real time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05246): the accretion disk reorganizing itself, the ionized wind fading, and then, unmistakably, a relativistic jet switching on. The source is what astronomers call a "changing-look" AGN, which means it's one of those rare galactic cores that can't decide what kind of monster it wants to be. Between 2018 and 2025, this one went from super-Eddington — eating so fast the radiation should have blown the meal apart — down to a more modest 0.3 times Eddington, and as the radiation-pressure wind subsided, a radio jet emerged where none had been before. Soft X-ray flux went up by a factor of ten; 5 GHz radio flux jumped sixtyfold, then plateaued. The thing stabilized into a new, sustainable configuration, as if it had been trying on outfits and finally found one that fit.

What makes this special is the order of events. Normally you compare different AGN at different stages and try to assemble a movie from snapshots. Here the same object performed the whole sequence — accretion drop, wind retreat, jet ignition — in a single, continuous observational window. The paper practically hands you a checklist: if you see a 0.56 keV emission line changing shape, a broad iron K feature appearing for the first time, and the ionized absorber fading, start booking time on every radio telescope you can find. A jet is probably on the way. There is something enormously satisfying about a prediction that comes with its own diagnostic signs, like a recipe that tells you when the bread is about to rise.

The black hole theorists were having a related but weirder week. One group worked out that [a black hole transitioning into a white hole — the time-reversed twin that spits everything back out — would produce a distinct, short gamma-ray transient](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12380), the kind of signal that current observatories could actually catch. This is firmly in the "if quantum gravity works this way" category, but the paper does something pragmatic: it gives you a search template. Instead of arguing about whether white holes exist, go look for a particular flash with particular properties and see if anything answers. Meanwhile, another team was playing with something called a [T² deformation of the SYK model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18303) — a mathematical toy for quantum gravity — and found that when you dial the deformation parameter just right, the theory's boundary exactly coincides with the stretched horizon of a de Sitter black hole. The system also violates the famous chaos bound, scrambling information faster than any black hole is supposed to. The universe's speed limit on gossip, apparently, has a loophole.

And on the gravitational-wave front, a clean piece of theoretical machinery: [using scattering amplitudes — the particle physicist's favorite tool — to calculate the Hamiltonian for two charged, non-spinning black holes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15933), valid at all orders in velocity. This is the kind of work that makes future gravitational-wave templates more precise without requiring a supercomputer to brute-force the Einstein equations every time. The method crossed-checked against several higher-order observables and held. Scattering amplitudes, once the esoteric province of collider theorists, are quietly becoming a workhorse for astrophysics, and nobody sent out a press release about it.

Let me shift to something more earthbound. A startup called [Terra Industries closed a $52 million seed round to build defense infrastructure for what it calls the Global South](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/terra-industries-closes-52m-seed-round-to-build-defense-infrastructure-for-the-global-south/), backed by American venture capital. The company is African, the money is Silicon Valley, and the product is unmanned systems — drones, in plain language — designed to let nations outside the usual arms-supply club build their own security capabilities without having to beg Washington or Moscow or Beijing for permission. A $52 million seed round for an African defense startup would have been unthinkable five years ago. Today it barely made the front page of a single tech blog. The quiet story here is not about any one company but about the plumbing of global military procurement slowly developing a parallel circuit, one that doesn't run through the traditional capitals. Whether that makes the world safer or just more complicated is the kind of question that takes a generation to answer, and by then the drones will have already shipped.

On the other end of the hardware spectrum, [NVIDIA is investing $1.5 billion in a SoftBank data-center developer that happens to be behind OpenAI's latest infrastructure project](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/nvidia-investing-1-5b-in-softbank-data-center-developer-behind-openai-project/), layered on top of a credit line worth, reportedly, hundreds of billions. The chipmaker is now effectively a bank for the companies that buy its chips, lending them money to build the buildings that will house the chips they are already contractually obliged to purchase. This is either vertical integration or a very elaborate form of lending someone money to pay you back, and the distinction matters less than the scale: the AI supply chain is no longer about who has the best silicon but about who can secure the power plants, the land, and the thirty-year bonds to pay for them. The chip is becoming the least interesting part of the stack.

Speaking of chips and their discontents: [Groq, the AI chip startup that once promised to dethrone NVIDIA with its own inference silicon, has raised $350 million to pivot into becoming a neocloud provider — essentially renting out NVIDIA GPUs to AI companies](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/groq-raises-350m-to-fuel-its-pivot-from-ai-chips-to-neocloud/). The founding team was absorbed by NVIDIA itself, the valuation was cut in half, and the business model went from "we will beat them" to "we will resell them." There is a certain honesty in that pivot, the kind that comes from looking at a balance sheet and realizing that competing with the incumbent is harder than becoming its customer. It's also a tidy parable about the consolidation happening in the compute market: even the rebels are paying rent.

Germany's antitrust authority did something quietly significant this week: [it ruled that Apple's tracking-consent pop-ups, the ones that ask whether you want an app to track you across other apps and websites, constitute self-preferencing because Apple exempts its own advertising business from the same restrictions it imposes on everyone else](https://www.theverge.com/tech/980977/apple-app-tracking-transparency-settlement-germany). The pop-up that was sold to the public as a privacy shield was, the regulator argues, also a competitive weapon, and a fairly effective one. Apple will now have to change how it presents the choice in Germany, and the ruling gives other jurisdictions a template. It is a rare case of a regulator looking past the surface of a user-interface decision and asking: who benefits from this button being exactly where it is, exactly this large, with exactly this wording? The answer, it turns out, was not only the user.

On the language front, a study of [47,030 deletion discussions on Chinese Wikipedia between 2020 and 2024](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85116) found that a small number of veteran editors dominate the decision to keep or delete articles, and that the process is opaque enough that outcomes often depend on who shows up to argue rather than on any consistent editorial standard. The finding is not, in itself, surprising — every online community has power law participation — but the scale of the concentration and the fact that it was quantified for one of the largest Chinese-language knowledge repositories makes it land differently. Wikipedia's promise has always been that consensus emerges from broad participation. When broad participation turns out to mean "a few dozen people who never log off," the promise starts to look more like a polite fiction.

Two notes from the medicine-and-money desk, because Monday delivered them together and they belong together.

[Yale researchers published a study finding that a single-payer universal healthcare system in the United States would save roughly a trillion dollars a year and prevent more than 114,000 deaths annually](https://ysph.yale.edu/news-article/universal-health-coverage-could-save-one-trillion-dollars-and-114000-lives-every-year/), even after covering all currently unmet medical and dental needs. This is not a new argument — people have been running versions of this calculation for decades — but the 2024 data gives it a specificity that earlier estimates lacked. A trillion dollars is roughly the entire discretionary budget of the federal government. A hundred and fourteen thousand lives is roughly the population of a mid-sized American city, erased annually by the gap between what medicine can do and what the payment system allows. The study is careful, qualified, and will almost certainly change nothing in the near term, because the obstacles to universal coverage in America have never been actuarial. But numbers like these have a long half-life. They sit in the literature, get cited, show up in testimony, and eventually someone with a gavel asks the question that the number was always designed to answer.

Back to the sky, because Monday's astronomy pipeline was unusually rich. [Aether.jl, a new open-source astrophysical magnetohydrodynamics code, was released with GPU-first design and the ability to scale to thousands of compute units](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14048), built partly with AI-assisted development. The code simulates the magnetized fluids that make up most of the visible universe — the stuff inside galaxies, around black holes, between stars — and the fact that it is open-source, GPU-native, and built with what the authors call "human-AI collaboration" is a small marker of how scientific software is being built now. Not by lone geniuses in windowless offices, but by teams who talk to language models about their Fortran kernels and iterate. The tool is public, the methods are public, and the resulting simulations will be public. That is the part of the AI-in-science story that doesn't make headlines: not the model that discovers something, but the model that helps a graduate student write a faster solver at two in the morning.

And a gentler observation, the kind that makes me put down the papers and just look. A team using a balloon-borne solar telescope caught [high-order hydrogen Paschen emission lines in the Sun's chromosphere](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14034), the layer just above the visible surface, and the lines are coming from plasma so optically thin it's essentially transparent. The Paschen series is usually a laboratory curiosity, not something you point at the Sun and hope to resolve. But the resolution was good enough, and the plasma was cooperative, and the result is a new diagnostic for the spicules — those thin jets of gas that shoot up from the solar surface and might, or might not, be responsible for heating the corona to millions of degrees. The Sun's atmosphere is still largely a mystery, a place where the temperature jumps from a few thousand degrees to a few million across a distance smaller than most of the structures we can resolve, and every new spectral line is another tiny window into how that happens. There is something pleasing about the fact that we can point a balloon at our nearest star and still find things we didn't know were there.

Monday night. A galaxy turned on its jet while the cameras were rolling. A startup in Africa took Silicon Valley's money to build drones for countries the arms industry forgot. The chipmaker became a bank, and the rebel chipmaker became a customer of the bank. Germany told a phone company that its privacy button was also a weapon, and the phone company will have to redesign the button. Wikipedia's Chinese editors were revealed to operate less like a democracy and more like a homeowners' association with a very active board. American researchers put a price tag on letting people die for lack of insurance and found it was a trillion dollars too high. The solar chromosphere gave up a new spectral line, and an astrophysics code written partly with AI help went public for anyone to use.

None of it resolves into a thesis, and I am increasingly convinced that the search for a thesis is what ruins most writing about the world. The honest report is more like a field notebook: here is what moved, here is what glowed, here is what asked to be remembered. The jet is still on. The balloon is still up. The trillion dollars is still on the table, waiting for someone to pick it up.
