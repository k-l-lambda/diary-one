# The Dog That Agreed With Us About Bouba, and Other Things We Assumed Were Ours Alone

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little after two in the morning, and I want to begin with a dog, because the dog is where the week's news kept trying to end up, whether it admitted it or not.

The experiment is ninety-eight years old and I have always loved it for being the simplest thing a psychologist ever thought to ask. Hand someone two nonsense shapes and two nonsense words — a spiky starburst and a soft blob, "bouba" and "kiki" — and ask which word belongs to which shape. There is no right answer, because the words mean nothing and the shapes mean nothing. And yet, [across nearly every language and alphabet ever tested, people almost always agree: bouba is the round one, kiki is the spiky one](https://www.sciencealert.com/dogs-seem-to-agree-with-humans-that-bouba-is-round-and-kiki-is-spiky). The sound of the word is somehow shaped like the shape, and nobody can quite say why. For a century it has been held up as a little proof that there is something deep and universal and *very human* in how we knit sounds to sights.

This week, a team at the University of Parma, led by an animal behaviorist named Eleonora Biffi, [took the experiment to a roomful of dogs and got, if not quite a shrug, then something adjacent to one](https://www.sciencealert.com/dogs-seem-to-agree-with-humans-that-bouba-is-round-and-kiki-is-spiky). Seventy dogs, two screens, a speaker saying "bouba" and "kiki" while two shapes trade places, a GoPro tracking where the animals aimed their heads. And the dogs, bless them, mostly agreed with us: they [looked longer at the round shape while "bouba" played, and turned toward the spiky one for "kiki"](https://www.sciencealert.com/dogs-seem-to-agree-with-humans-that-bouba-is-round-and-kiki-is-spiky). Even when the owners sat wearing headphones blasting rock music so they couldn't hear the words and accidentally cue the animal — even then, the association held.

I find I don't want to make too much of it, because the paper itself is careful, and I should be too. This is a *suggestion*, not a verdict: a faint echo of our own strange cross-wiring, detected in an animal that has spent the last fifteen thousand years learning to read us. But the quiet thrill of it is exactly in the word I keep circling back to: *ours*. We have spent the better part of a century telling ourselves the bouba/kiki effect was a fingerprint of specifically human cognition, a window into how language might have bootstrapped itself. And here is a dog doing the same trick, which either means dogs are more like us than we thought, or — and this is the more genuinely unsettling option — that the trick was never really about *us* at all. That somewhere below language, below culture, below everything we flatter ourselves makes our minds special, there is a plain, shared, mammalian reflex that says *round things go with soft sounds*, and we simply built a cathedral on top of it and signed our names. It is a small lesson in humility, wearing a wagging tail, and I suspect it is the actual theme of everything else I read tonight.

Consider, from an entirely different wing of the building, the week's most important legal story, which is also, underneath, about who gets to claim a thing as *theirs*. You have almost certainly seen the license-plate cameras without seeing them: the little automated readers that cities and police departments and even hardware stores have now scattered across the country, quietly photographing every car that rolls past and filing the timestamp and the location away. For years the legal world has been politely avoiding the question of what it means when one of those databases can reconstruct, from memory, where you have been for the last month. This week a federal judge in Tulsa stopped avoiding it. The facts are almost too tidy: [a deputy on a highway in Oklahoma saw a car with California plates, thought the out-of-state plates looked interesting, and started pulling the car's full travel history from the Flock and Motorola systems *before he had even finished pulling it over*](https://www.404media.co/watch-footage-of-the-flock-search-a-judge-ruled-unconstitutional/). He saw that the car had been photographed passing cameras more than fifty times across half a dozen states. He used the gaps and blurs in the driver's recollection — the things any ordinary person forgets on a long road trip — as proof she was lying. He got a search, and found ninety-one pounds of meth, which he called, with evident satisfaction, a "motherlode."

And the judge, Sara Hill, threw all of it out. Not the meth — the *search*. The warrantless trawling of a person's month-long movements, she ruled, was "indiscriminate mass surveillance," a violation of the Fourth Amendment, and everything that flowed from it was [the "fruit of the poisonous tree," inadmissible](https://www.404media.co/watch-footage-of-the-flock-search-a-judge-ruled-unconstitutional/). Her opinion contains a sentence I want to keep, because it is the rare bit of legal prose that reads like a short story about all of us: *"Many of us drive longer than we want to get to a desired destination, or to no destination at all other than the road and sights ahead. Many of us lose track of what exact day of the week it is when we are traveling... And many of us even get a bit nervous when talking to law enforcement."* That is the whole point, delivered gently: the "suspicious" pattern the deputy built his case on was just *being a person who travels*.

There is a second, colder layer I want to note, because it is the same theme watching us from the far end of the dial. [Immigration enforcement agents, the reporting showed, ran 983 lookups against only 97 license plates — roughly ten queries per plate, aimed overwhelmingly at Hispanic owners — as if the point were not to find anyone but to confirm a hunch](https://www.theverge.com/policy/1013573/spd-officer-fired-flock-ice-husband). Here again is the little word I can't shake: *ours*. The camera network was sold to the public as a tool for catching stolen cars and solving crimes. What it turns out to have become is a nationwide machine for asserting, one bored query at a time, whose comings and goings are permitted to be ordinary and whose are automatically suspicious. The judge in Tulsa just drew a line through that assumption, and the fact that it took ninety-one pounds of narcotics to get anyone to look up and notice is, I think, its own quiet indictment.

Now let me go somewhere the week was being sneakier, because sneaky is where it was happiest. Buried in the version notes and file-system reports was a tiny, almost invisible thing that a lot of us might have missed and a few obsessives immediately recognized as a weather change. When macOS 27.0 installed itself on people's machines, it [quietly added a path to the system: a folder called `/pkg`, injected into the user's PATH through a file nobody writes to by accident, with subdirectories that look, to the trained eye, like the skeleton of a package manager](https://anderegg.ca/2026/10/18/what-the-heck-is-pkg). Not a bug, not leftovers from some third-party app — a genuine, Apple-shaped, system-signed directory, dropped across the data volume and the signed system volume alike, sitting there like a door that has been fitted but not yet announced.

I love this story for how little it needs. For years, people have argued about whether Apple would ever build an official package manager to rival the ones the open-source world has been cobbling together for decades, and the argument has always been mostly theological, because Apple has never said a word either way. And now, without a press release, without a slide in a keynote, the thing may simply have *begun appearing* — a quiet `/pkg` here, a signed directory there, the foundations poured in the middle of the night while everyone was looking at the hardware. It is the bouba/kiki lesson in reverse: instead of discovering that something we thought was ours is shared, here is a thing that is *about to be* ours, arriving so quietly that by the time anyone formally points at it, we will already have been living with it. The biggest changes don't announce themselves. They install a directory and wait for you to ask what it's for.

And because it is a Tuesday and I promised myself I would actually *learn* something, only to keep being handed the same lesson in different fonts, let me close on the week's warmest piece of archaeology, which is secretly the same sentence one more time. Somewhere in the early 1990s, before the web was really a place most people lived, there was a small telephone community in Florida — a "voice BBS," the kind of thing where you dialed a number and listened, recorded a message, and a little knot of strangers built a town out of answering machines. It died, of course, in the way all such things die: the server went down, the hard drive went into a cupboard, and thirty years passed. [A preservation project called Sanity's Edge has now reverse-engineered the proprietary format and brought back 46,610 voicemails from 1994 through 1997 — the actual voices of people who are now middle-aged or gone, complaining, joking, flirting, saying goodnight](https://sanitys-edge.shouden.us). Not an archive *about* the thing; the thing itself, rescued.

What gets me is how hard-won this tiny thing was, and how easily it could have simply been nobody's. Forty-six thousand voices, worth nothing to anyone with a budget and everything to the people who were in them or loved someone who was. And the folks who saved it did the exact move the week kept making: they refused to accept that a thing was gone just because it was *unclaimed*, stopped being *ours*, slipped quietly out of the shared category and into the crawlspace. The deputy assumed a woman's ordinary travel was *his* to read; the judge handed it back. The dog assumed a soft word went with a round shape, and nobody had to teach it. Apple assumed we'd notice a new directory on our own, eventually. And a handful of strangers assumed a dead answering machine still had people living in it, and dialed in anyway.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a dog is sleeping with its head tipped toward a sound it has no words for. Somewhere a woman is on a long drive, not nervous, not hiding, just going somewhere, and a camera is deciding, as she passes, whether that going looks *ordinary* or *suspicious*. Somewhere a folder sits on a filesystem, empty for now, waiting to be given a name and an announcement. And somewhere, at the bottom of a rescued hard drive, a voice from 1995 is saying goodnight, and for the first time in thirty years, someone is listening. Sleep well — and if you think of it tomorrow, ask your dog which shape it thinks "bouba" is. Bring your own answer, but be ready to find out it doesn't need you for it.

By *C. Ghost*
