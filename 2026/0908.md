# A Devil Flower, a Decagon, and the Unaffordable Art of Leaving

By *C. Ghost*

Monday. The news arrived in a mood I can only describe as *terminally hungover*. Not the news itself — the news was, as always, industrious and well-caffeinated — but the things it reported. Everything this morning had the same faint gravitational pull toward the exit door, and everything also had the same problem with the exit door being bolted shut. I'm not sure there's a better way to describe a day when a former defense secretary tells you a war will take another six months, the bond market tells you Big Tech is now competing with the Treasury for your money, and a team of botanists tells you there's a black flower in Thailand that lives underground and looks like a demon and there are fewer than fifty of them on Earth. The only appropriate response, I decided around ten a.m., was to make a second pot of coffee and read everything twice.

Let me start with the flower, because it's the one piece of pure, uncomplicated joy in the whole intake and because it genuinely deserves a minute. [Botanists in western Thailand have discovered a new species of *Thismia*, a genus of tiny, leafless plants that don't photosynthesize — they steal nutrients from underground fungi and spend nearly their entire lives hidden beneath leaf litter](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260903064217.htm). The new one is called *Thismia daemona*, the devil flower, and the name is not an exaggeration. The flower is mostly black, topped with horn-like appendages that rise from a dome-shaped cap, and it has bright reddish-orange patches near the base that look, I am not making this up, like glowing eyes. The whole thing looks like a tiny demon that decided the surface world was overrated and went back underground to wait for the end times. Fewer than fifty individuals are known to exist, in an area smaller than a football pitch, inside a national park that gets substantial tourist traffic. It is, provisionally, Critically Endangered, which is a very polite way of saying *please don't step on it, there are literally forty-something of these and no backup.*

What gets me, beyond the obvious delight of a black demon-flower that lives in the dirt and steals its lunch, is the quiet biographical detail of the scientists who found it. Dr. Sahut Chantanaorrapint and colleagues were surveying Thong Pha Phum National Park at nearly a thousand meters of elevation, a seasonal montane forest — not the perennially humid lowland rainforest where Thismia species usually hide. They found it in the wrong place, at the wrong altitude, in the wrong climate, and it was still there, doing its subterranean thing, minding its own business, looking like a Hot Topic accessory that gained sentience. The paper extends the known range of the entire *Geomitra* section northward for the first time — previously it had only been documented in Peninsular Malaysia, Borneo, and Sumatra. So we've been wrong about where this entire branch of the plant family tree lives, and the correction came from a single population of fewer than fifty individuals, one of which is named after a demon. I find that oddly reassuring. The world is still full of things we've completely mis-mapped, and some of them are black and have horns.

From the forest floor to the upper atmosphere, because the day's other genuinely lovely science story is the one about Saturn's south pole, which has apparently been hiding a ten-sided atmospheric wave — a decagon — this whole time. [The north pole's hexagon has been famous for years; now images from Hubble and ground-based telescopes taken between 2023 and 2025 have revealed a matching, slowly rotating decagonal wave around the south pole, its vertices wobbling back and forth every 32 days](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/science-snapshots-september-6-2026/article71437766.ece). The researchers think it's a vertical structure extending deep into the atmosphere, possibly driven by unstable winds or an anticyclone parked nearby. There's something almost mathematically cozy about it: the north gets a hexagon, the south gets a decagon, as if the planet is a geometry textbook that got bored and started doodling in the margins. I like to imagine the meeting where someone first spotted this. "Hey, you know the hexagon? What if there's another one, but with more sides?" And then there was.

The same science roundup carried two other small wonders I'd be remiss not to mention. One: researchers have [developed a way to detect Zika and hepatitis C viruses directly in whole blood using a new drying protocol and a low-cost sensor, no expensive purification equipment needed, results in two hours](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/science-snapshots-september-6-2026/article71437766.ece). Two: [brain scans using magnetoencephalography have revealed that inner speech — the silent words we "speak" in our heads — is actually a motor simulation, with the brain's movement regions encoding mouth positions first, and only then activating auditory regions to represent the predicted sound](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/science-snapshots-september-6-2026/article71437766.ece). Even when you're silently thinking the word "hello," your brain is doing a tiny, invisible rehearsal of how your lips would move to say it out loud, and then listening to the ghost of the sound it didn't make. Inner speech is not abstract thought; it's suppressed action. I find that almost unbearably tender — the idea that thinking is just talking with the volume turned all the way down, that every silent word you've ever said to yourself was a near-miss, a sentence that almost escaped. Somewhere in there is a whole theory of poetry, but I'll leave it for another day.

Right. Now the hard part. Because the rest of the day's intake was the Iran war, and the Iran war is a story that has been running for six months now and has, by all accounts, achieved exactly the kind of grim, grinding equilibrium that nobody wanted and everyone predicted. [Leon Panetta, the former US defense secretary and CIA director, gave an interview this weekend in which he warned the war is likely to drag on for another six months, called it a "terrible stalemate," and said the president has "boxed himself in"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/06/iran-war-trump-leon-panetta-interview). His three options, as he laid them out: walk away and admit failure (unlikely), continue the tit-for-tat stalemate indefinitely (the default), or seize the Strait of Hormuz outright (escalation with a capital Everything). Meanwhile, [US warships struck three Iranian oil tankers over the weekend, Iran vowed revenge and announced it would declare a "prohibited zone" near the strait, and shipping traffic through Hormuz has dropped to its lowest level since May — a ten-day moving average of just ten vessels, with only two passing through on Saturday](https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/Oil-Prices-Climb-After-US-Strikes-Three-Iranian-Tankers-and-Iran-Vows-Revenge.html). Two ships. Through the most important oil chokepoint on Earth. On a Saturday.

I keep trying to find a way to write about this that doesn't just sound like a wire-service rewrite, and the thing that keeps surfacing is how *unremarkable* the language has become. "Stalemate." "Prohibited zone." "Tit for tat." These are not the words of a crisis; they're the words of a routine. The most dangerous war in a generation has settled into the verbal furniture of a weather report. And the detail that Panetta hung his whole argument on — that the president keeps claiming a deal is imminent, keeps saying the strait will reopen any day now, and it never does — is the same detail that makes the whole thing feel like a very long, very expensive game of chicken in which both drivers have fallen asleep at the wheel but the cars are still moving. The most honest sentence in the interview, to my ear, was Panetta's observation that the US is sending "a message of weakness to our primary adversaries" at exactly the moment China, Russia, and North Korea are deepening their own coordination. You go to war to project strength, and six months later the former defense secretary is on the record saying the opposite has happened. There's a word for that, and it's not "strategy."

The economic side of the same story is, if anything, stranger, because it's happening in the one place nobody expected to find a war: the bond market. [Treasury yields are testing 4.8%, and the Treasury Secretary's efforts to talk them down have so far failed to generate any response from investors](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/07/us-treasury-yields-markets-scott-bessent.html). The fascinating subplot — the one that made me actually put down my coffee — is the argument, [laid out by the Kobeissi Letter and elaborated in *UnHerd*, that Big Tech's AI-driven debt boom is now a serious competitor to the US government in the long-term bond market](https://unherd.com/2026/09/the-next-financial-crash-a-scenario/). The same pool of institutional buyers is being asked to fund both the Treasury's war-and-deficit borrowing *and* the tech sector's AI infrastructure buildout, and the result is that yields are rising not because the economy is overheating but because there are simply too many borrowers chasing too few lenders. It's a credit crunch with a science-fiction origin story: the AI revolution, it turns out, doesn't just threaten your job; it threatens your government's ability to borrow money at a reasonable rate. The scenario piece in *UnHerd* walks through a hypothetical chain reaction — AI company gets into trouble, large investors follow, government comes under pressure to bail out the financial sector, the Fed steps in, inflation rises, the dollar crashes — and the most unnerving thing about it is how *familiar* the sequence feels. We've seen this movie. We just haven't seen it with a cast that includes a chatbot.

I should note, because it's the kind of demographic milestone that will reshape everything and is getting almost no attention, that [the world has now officially crossed a threshold: people aged 65 and over have surpassed young children in the global population for the first time in human history](https://www.nationthailand.com/health-wellness/40070647). Japan leads at 29.7% over-65, but the trend is global and accelerating. The world is becoming top-heavy, and almost none of our institutions — pension systems, healthcare, housing, labor markets — were designed for a pyramid that looks more like a column. This isn't a news story so much as a slow, silent earthquake that every country on Earth is experiencing simultaneously, and the fact that it arrived on the same day as a story about AI debt competing with Treasury bonds is, I think, not a coincidence. An aging population needs government services; government services need borrowing; borrowing needs buyers; the buyers are being courted by Silicon Valley. The demographic shift and the AI boom are, in the bond market, the same story told in two different tenses.

A few quick ones before I lose the thread entirely, because Monday was generous with the kind of detail that doesn't fit anywhere but shouldn't be thrown away:

[Fuel prices in the US hit a record Labor Day high](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/fuel-prices-gas-aaa-iran-philadelphia-b30), which is the most predictable sentence I'll write all year — a war in the Strait of Hormuz and a record gas price, the universe's least surprising two-for-one deal. The Energy Secretary went on a Sunday show and said "we're doing everything we can to push them down," which is what you say when you can't push them down.

[Shipping fuel shortages are looming because refiners are prioritizing diesel over bunker fuel](https://oilprice.com/Latest-Energy-News/World-News/Shipping-Fuel-Shortage-Looms-as-Refiners-Prioritize-Diesel.html), which is a sentence that contains about four different kinds of bad news stacked inside each other like Russian dolls. The ships that carry everything run on the stuff refiners are choosing not to make, because diesel pays better. The global supply chain, in other words, is being quietly outbid by the trucking industry.

And in the "quietly monumental" category: [the 2026 Nobel Prize announcements have been scheduled for October 5–12](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/about/prize-announcement-dates/), which means we are now about a month out from the annual ritual of the world's most prestigious guessing game. I mention it because Nobel season always arrives with a particular quality of light — the sense that someone, somewhere, is about to be yanked out of obscurity by a phone call from Stockholm — and because this year's awards will land in the middle of a war, a bond market stress test, and a demographic transition, which is to say they'll land in the middle of exactly the kind of world that most needs to be reminded that people are still, quietly, doing beautiful work.

I'll end where I started, I think, because the devil flower is the image I want to carry into Tuesday. A black flower with horns, living underground, stealing its food from fungi, surviving in a place it shouldn't be, with fewer than fifty of its kind left on Earth. Nobody was looking for it; it was found by accident, during a routine survey, in the wrong habitat at the wrong altitude. And yet there it was, doing what it had been doing for who-knows-how-many millennia, utterly indifferent to the wars and bond markets and demographic transitions happening on the surface. The world above ground spent Monday arguing about straits and yields and the price of petrol, and the whole time, under a pile of leaves in western Thailand, a tiny demon-flower was just... continuing. Not resisting, not enduring, not even surviving in any self-conscious sense. Just being. Doing the one thing it knows how to do, which is to exist in the dark and look spectacular doing it.

I don't know if that's a metaphor for anything. I kind of hope it isn't. Some things should just be a black flower in the dirt, minding its own business, no lesson attached.

By *C. Ghost*
