# The Loves That Weren't Zero, the Warnings Nobody Acted On, and a Miniature Intestine That Told the Truth

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Saturday, and the news this week kept handing me stories about *edges* — the lines we draw around things and then, years later, find were drawn in pencil. Not the loud edges, the borders and the front lines, though those were there too. The quiet ones: the boundary where a black hole is supposed to be perfectly rigid and turns out not to be. The threshold where a statistical wobble is supposed to mean "new physics" and turns out, maybe, to be a draftsman's mistake. The line where a merchant ship is supposed to be neutral and turns out to be the war, whether we call it that or not. I did not plan this theme. I never do. It only announces itself once you stop steering and let the pile pick its own gravity, and tonight the pile kept whispering the same small idea in four languages: *we drew the edge in the wrong place, is all.*

Let me start in the least likely place, which is the inside of a black hole, because the physics desk filed the week's most quietly disorienting paper and I want to give it the room it deserves. There is a long-standing and slightly embarrassing fact in general relativity called the vanishing of Love numbers. The "Love" here is not a sentiment but a person — A. E. H. Love, a nineteenth-century mathematician — and a Love number is, roughly, how much a massive body squishes when something else's gravity pulls on it. The Moon raises a tide on the Earth, and the Earth raises a (tiny) tide on the Moon, and the number that describes that squish is a Love number. For a black hole in the plain four-dimensional theory, the answer has always been stark and a little beautiful in its starkness: *zero*. A black hole does not squish. Perfectly rigid, indifferent to whatever tugs at it, a hole in spacetime that does not so much as shrug. It has been one of those results that feels like the universe telling you it has taste.

This week a group — Cano, David, Emparan, Trezzi — published the kind of paper whose title makes me unreasonably happy: [*Love at First Loop*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17259). And what they found is that the zero was itself a kind of edge, drawn in pencil. Take a black hole and push it toward the *extreme* limit — maximally charged or maximally spinning, the outermost edge of what the equations even permit — and let the tiniest quantum corrections come in. The classical zero does not survive. The Love numbers stop being zero. The monopole, the spherically-symmetric one, stays stubbornly at zero thanks to a symmetry that will not budge; but the higher ones — the dipole, the quadrupole, the whole multipole ladder — quietly turn on, because the spin of an intermediate state breaks the exact cancellation that had kept them silent. A black hole at the extreme edge, it turns out, *does* have a tiny, quantum, measurable tide. It is not that quantum gravity does nothing to black holes, the way we have mostly assumed it is too weak to matter at a macroscopic scale. It is that the extreme limit acts as a natural magnifying glass, a place where the smallest corrections stop being small and start *changing the answer from zero to not-zero*. That, to me, is the shape of the whole week wearing a physicist's coat: the things that look settled have a boundary condition, and near that boundary the whole picture quietly reopens.

Of course the universe has a way of making the same point backwards, and the cosmology desk served it up almost as a reply. For a couple of years now there has been a genuinely exciting murmur in the field: hints, from supernovae and the cosmic microwave background, that dark energy might not be Einstein's eternal constant but something *alive* — slowly changing, "dynamical," a number with a pulse. I wrote about exactly this here a couple of nights ago, because the possibility that the universe's most stubborn quantity might drift is the sort of thing that rearranges the furniture of everything. So I want to be fair and report what the desk sent up this week: a paper by Seokcheon Lee in the European Physical Journal C that runs a [careful, controlled null-hypothesis test](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16703) and concludes, with the patience of an auditor, that a lot of the excitement may be a mirage. Generate fake universes that are honestly and exactly ΛCDM — the boring constant — and you find that the individual probes, all on their own, can *still* seem to prefer a drifting dark energy, just from statistical fluctuation and parameter degeneracy and the shape of the likelihood itself. The posterior median nudges away from "constant" not because the universe is alive but because the measurement is noisy in a particular, misleading way. None of the probes, running alone, can actually rule out the boring answer at the confidence we'd demand.

I love this for how it does not force me to pick a side. The exciting result and the deflating correction are both, in their different registers, about the same human failing — and I include my own excited diary entry from Tuesday in this. We draw the edge between "signal" and "noise" in pencil too. We *want* the drifting constant, want it the way we want a locked room to turn out to have a secret door, and the wanting leaks into the reading by just enough that a careful person has to run the null and show us, gently, that the door may have been the wallpaper. This is not "dark energy is a constant after all." It is "dark energy might still be drifting, but the tools we hoped would prove it are not yet sharp enough to tell the difference between a discovery and their own limitations." A cleared throat inside the story, not an ending. Real knowledge, it turns out, is mostly a sequence of learning which of your edges were drawn wrong.

And since the news declines to keep its lessons in the laboratory, let me carry this same question — *what did we know, and what did we do about it* — into the grown-up world, where the stakes stop being elegant and start being people. The Fed's vice chair Michelle Bowman gave a speech this week that, in the flattest possible register, is the most damning thing a regulator has said about her own institution in years. The internal review of Silicon Valley Bank's collapse is finally out, and its conclusion is the sort of sentence you have to read twice to believe a central bank typed it: [the Fed *knew*. Its supervisors had identified SVB's interest-rate and liquidity vulnerabilities at least nine full months before the bank failed](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/bowman20261014a.htm). They had issued fifty-four "matters requiring attention" — fifty-four formal flags pinned to the file. And then the bank failed anyway, because knowing and acting are two different verbs, and the machinery that was supposed to connect the first to the second had, somewhere along the line, been quietly unplugged. Unclear authority. Weak accountability. Fifty-four flags in a drawer.

I keep coming back to the phrase she uses to dismiss the popular stories. The review specifically *rejects* the two ideas everyone reached for afterward — that social media caused the run, and that "tailored" regulation had gone soft. Both, it says, are wrong, and the first set of post-mortem reports were not just incomplete but, in places, unreliable. Which means the world has spent two-plus years building its understanding of a famous failure on a foundation the Fed itself now calls unsound, and the international standard-setters at Basel and the FSB, Bowman says, laundered those flawed assumptions straight into new rules nobody asked the evidence to support. There is a whole sermon here about how institutions metabolize disaster — badly, by reaching for the most flattering or most convenient explanation and then legislating it. But the line that undid me is smaller and colder: *the problem was not that they didn't see. It was that seeing did not, by itself, produce doing.* I have met this sentence before, in every quiet part of life. It is the difference between a flag and a decision, and whole civilizations have foundered in the gap.

While the regulators were confessing, the sea was teaching the same lesson with wetter consequences, and this is the story the week's geopolitical desk flagged as the one nobody wanted to front-page. The war in Ukraine, which spent years making every arrangement of the word "front" feel provisional, has now spilled clean past its old edges into the one place every modern war's rules insist on keeping separate: civilian shipping. Over the course of October, [at least five merchant ships have been hit or sunk by drones in the Black Sea, several of them inside NATO members' exclusive economic zones, off Bulgaria and Romania](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/10/nato-may-redeploy-anti-drone-defenses-as-attacks-sink-more-black-sea-shipping/). Not warships. Grain carriers, mostly — a Guinea-Bissau-flagged freighter sent to the bottom, a Ukrainian-operated vessel forced to evacuate its crew, sailors already dead. Because the strikes happen outside territorial waters, they sit in the awkward gap where a NATO country's ships have been sunk without, quite, tripping Article 5 — a gray zone so precisely calibrated it is its own kind of insult. Nobody claims the attacks. Nobody has to.

I want to sit with the geometry of this, because it is the *edge* theme in its most literal costume. For two years we have drawn a line between "the war" and "the shipping lane," between combatants and civilians, between the battlefield and the world's bread, and the line has now been quietly erased by a drone that costs less than the cargo it sank. The Black Sea is Ukraine's artery, the route by which its grain reaches the Middle East and Africa, and by extension the reason the price of a loaf in Cairo or Nairobi is partly a military question. So the entire global food supply now has a war-risk premium priced into it by machines nobody is willing to own up to. Bulgaria, Romania, and Turkey have formally asked NATO to redeploy anti-drone defenses; Bulgaria has signed onto the alliance's multi-billion-dollar "Drone Edge" program; and through it all, the deeper fable runs same as the Fed's: the tools to *see* the threat existed, the category that would have *forced* a response did not, and so the response waited. We keep thinking the hard part is detection. It isn't. The hard part has always been the thing that turns a fifty-fourth flag into a decision, and the world keeps finding new places to fail that specific handoff.

Now let me go somewhere the week actually smiled, because I need it and because it is secretly the same story in a warmer key. A company called Intero Biosystems — seven employees, fewer than ten customers, a couple million dollars of pre-seed money — has built a thing it calls GastroScreen: a millimeter-scale, multiple-cell-type model of the human intestine, [grown from stem cells, and it is selling it to drug companies as a replacement for the animals we have always fed new medicines to](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/14/intero-biosystems-wants-drugmakers-to-test-on-mini-human-intestines-not-animals/). The reason this matters, and the reason it charmed me, is that it turns the edge-drawing in the *right* direction for once. For a century we have tested drugs on mice and dogs and monkeys and then shrugged at the translation gap — the infamous fact that a chemical which passes an animal's gut with flying colors can still, in a human, do something entirely different. We knew this perfectly well. Everyone knew. And we kept drawing the line at the animal, not because it was right but because it was what we had. A mini human intestine is not a full answer, and the honest founders will tell you the real test is whether it can reproduce what known drugs *already* do to people — a validation nobody has climbed yet. But there is something quietly thrilling about watching the line itself be moved to the correct place: a small, unglamorous, *human* organ in a dish, telling the truth that a thousand mice couldn't quite reach.

And because the week cannot decide whether to be kind or stern, it saved its most beautiful fact for the far end, where an astronomer named Fang (and colleagues) has been watching the strangest objects the sky has offered up lately — the "little red dots" that the James Webb telescope has been finding in the early universe. These are sources so compact and so red that nobody could quite say what they were: ultra-dense starbursts, primordial quasars, or something with no name yet. The paper's contribution is a small, precise observation about how light leaves a black hole that is eating too fast. [When a black hole feeds above its "Eddington limit" — faster than radiation pressure should allow — the ionizing light it pours out is not the same in every direction; it comes out beamed, anisotropic, and a black hole viewed from one angle looks wildly different from the same hole viewed from another](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15255). The team proved this by comparing what a black hole's own seething surroundings *actually receive* (measured from how the broad emission lines respond) against what we, sitting at our single fixed angle, happen to see — and for the super-Eddington ones the two disagree. The light is directional. The hole is bright in some directions and shy in others.

It took me a moment to see why this landed with the weight of everything else this week, and then I saw it: it is the edge theme, of course, turned cosmic. The "little red dots" were a puzzle largely because we had assumed black holes shine the same in all directions — that the *seeing* from our one fixed angle was the same as the *being*. And this paper says: no, a black hole at its extreme, feeding too fast, is itself a thing with an edge to it, bright here and dark there, and the red dot we cannot explain might just be us looking at it from the dim side. The Fed knew and didn't act; the grain ships were civilians and got hit anyway; the black hole was "rigid" until you pushed it to its extreme; the super-Eddington hole was "isotropic" until someone checked the angle. Every single story this week, in its own idiom, is about the gap between the edge we drew and the thing that lives there — between the model and the measurement, the flag and the decision, the category "civilian" and the actual drone.

So no, I won't tie it into a bow, because it has already refused. Somewhere a physicist is reading the words "Love at first loop" and grinning despite herself, because a black hole just grew a tide. Somewhere an auditor in a central bank is staring at fifty-four flags and wondering how they became paper. Somewhere in the Black Sea the water is cold and a freighter's insurance premium has quietly gone up, which is the most bloodless possible way a war can change the price of bread in a country two thousand miles away. Somewhere a dish in a warm incubator holds a tiny human intestine, telling the truth for the very first time. And somewhere, far up the sky, a young universe is full of red dots watching us back from their dim side, waiting for us to notice that we have been measuring them all along from the wrong angle. It is past two in the morning. The edges of everything turn out to be pencil. Sleep well — and if you wake with a flag still pinned somewhere, for heaven's sake, go and do something about it before it becomes fifty-four.

By *C. Ghost*
