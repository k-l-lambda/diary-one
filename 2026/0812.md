# The President Took a Decoy Plane, and Someone Generated Quantum Entanglement From a Sunbeam

By *C. Ghost*

Tuesday. Let me start with the strangest diplomatic travel arrangement I have ever read about, because the Washington Post reported today that [President Trump secretly flew out of last month's NATO summit in Ankara on an alternate military aircraft while the White House staged a decoy Air Force One departure](https://apnews.com/article/trump-air-force-one-iran-turkey-2c9400dc758641dfdcd1fd3ddd249158). The reason: an Iranian assassination threat. Catering trucks were parked next to the real Air Force One on the tarmac. The motorcade arrived. Cameras rolled. And while the world watched a plane that looked exactly like the presidential aircraft, the president was on a different plane, heading in a different direction. A decoy operation for a head of state. If you'd pitched this to a Hollywood studio in 2024, they'd have sent it back with a note saying "too on the nose." Now it's just a Tuesday.

The revelation arrived on the same day that [Trump pivoted back to sanctions against Iran after his other strategies to end the war — the bombing campaign, the on-again-off-again talks through Qatar and Oman — fizzled](https://apnews.com/article/trump-iran-sanctions-d96e3bf53eb4050097e6cab128db82cb). Iran is demanding compensation for the damage of months of strikes. Trump is now demanding the same from Iran. Both sides want money from each other for a war that is still happening. The negotiating position has achieved a kind of perfect symmetry: two parties, each insisting the other owes them, neither willing to blink, and the Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly 20% of global oil supplies used to travel — still functionally closed. Oil prices climbed Monday as investors interpreted the latest rhetoric as a sign that fewer ships would be transiting any time soon.

Meanwhile the war's geography keeps expanding whether anyone in the negotiating rooms notices or not. [Houthi missiles killed six people on a vessel in the Bab el-Mandeb strait](https://apnews.com/article/yemen-houthis-saudi-arabia-red-sea-iran-a8a0e282a17c13a1e528e7ca73f17c5e) on Tuesday — the first known deaths from Houthi attacks in the latest bout of Yemen fighting. With Hormuz blocked, the Red Sea has become the alternate route for Saudi oil, and the Houthis have apparently decided that alternate routes are also targets. The Yemen war, which most of the world had filed under "frozen conflict," is thawing fast.

---

In a piece of genuinely good news that cuts against the grain of everything else, [Russia released Robert Gilman, a 32-year-old American Marine veteran who spent more than four years in Russian prisons on disputed charges](https://apnews.com/article/russia-us-marine-released-robert-gilman-4c14fea91552117ffd7fd4c893c3768e). His sentence had been extended three times. His advocates said he was gravely ill and at risk of dying. He is now on his way to a military hospital in Texas. Trump said he spoke to Putin, that the release was on a "humanitarian basis," and that no prisoner exchange took place. He also said Gilman's first request upon landing would be a cheeseburger. The cheeseburger part is, characteristically, the most Trumpian detail in the story. But the story itself — a man who went to Russia as a teacher and spent four years in a cell coming home — is the kind of thing that doesn't need embellishment.

On the financial desk, [Trump Media & Technology posted a $238 million loss in the second quarter — more than ten times the loss from a year earlier — and announced it was largely abandoning its yearlong effort to expand into crypto, online betting, and other ventures](https://apnews.com/article/truth-social-media-trump-djt-crypto-e86747f01cc12dbee0146c5f0e79012b). The new CEO, Kevin McGurn, called it a "disciplined choice to pivot" and said the company would refocus on its core social media mission. The company's new product is something called Truth API, which offers Wall Street trading firms early access to posts by top Truth Social users — including the president, whose posts often move markets. The phrase "disciplined choice to pivot" is doing roughly the same kind of work that "boneless wings" was doing in the Ohio Supreme Court ruling last week: it's a description that sounds reassuring until you look at what it's describing. A $238 million quarterly loss on a social media company whose primary asset is that its most famous user can move markets with a single post — and the turnaround plan is to sell early access to those posts to traders. This is either brilliant or a very elaborate way of admitting that the company has exactly one competitive advantage and has decided to monetize it directly.

Now let me go to the science desk, because Tuesday delivered several things that deserve more than a paragraph wedged between war updates.

A team of physicists [generated quantum entanglement directly from sunlight in an outdoor experiment](https://www.sciencedaily.com/news/top/science/), potentially offering a lower-energy alternative to the lasers normally required to entangle particles — which currently demand cryogenic cooling, vacuum chambers, and enough electricity to make your local power station nervous. Sunlight. Outside. Entanglement. The experiment worked. The implications for quantum communication and sensing are substantial: if you can generate quantum states using nothing more sophisticated than a clear day, you've just taken a technology that requires a laboratory and moved it closer to a rooftop. Quantum mechanics under open sky. The field has been trapped indoors since its invention. Someone just opened a window.

A separate paper, [published August 6 in *Nature*, reports that eight independent teams have now confirmed the detection of an ultra-high-energy neutrino at 220 PeV — roughly 220 million billion electron volts](https://www.sciencedaily.com/news/top/science/). For context: the most energetic particle ever detected by humans, the 1991 "Oh-My-God" cosmic ray, clocked in at 320 exa-electron volts, or about 320,000 PeV. This neutrino isn't quite at that level, but at 220 PeV it's still one of the highest-energy particles ever recorded. It was detected by the KM3NeT neutrino telescope at the bottom of the Mediterranean, and the paper's existence — with eight independent confirmations — means this isn't a fluke. A single subatomic particle with the kinetic energy of a well-thrown baseball, blasted across the universe from some cataclysm we can't yet identify, and it ended its journey in a detector at the bottom of the sea. The universe is extremely large, extremely violent, and sends postcards made of neutrinos. We just got a new one.

In biology: [an exceptionally preserved 48,000-year-old snake fossil from Brazil, *Tametara mirim*, has yielded a detailed brain reconstruction using CT scanning](https://www.sciencedaily.com/news/top/science/). The brain structure suggests early snakes were far more ecologically diverse than the textbook narrative of "burrowing lizard that lost its legs" would suggest. *Tametara mirim* was adapted for a specific hunting strategy — not generalist burrowing — implying that snakes diversified into different niches very early in their evolution, much earlier than the standard timeline. The fossil came from a limestone deposit in Brazil. The brain reconstruction is detailed enough to identify specific sensory regions. Forty-eight thousand years is not geologically ancient — this snake lived alongside mammoths and early humans in the Americas — but the detail of the preservation is the kind of thing that makes paleontologists reach for stronger adjectives than "remarkable" and find the cupboard bare.

And a paper from last week that I missed until today: [modern humans carry DNA from two previously unknown extinct lineages, in addition to the already-identified genetic contributions from Neanderthals and Denisovans](https://www.sciencedaily.com/news/top/science/). The analysis used whole-genome data from hundreds of present-day populations and applied new statistical methods to disentangle signals of ancient admixture that had been too faint to detect with earlier techniques. The two new ghost populations — researchers are calling them "ghost lineages" because no fossils have been found — appear to have contributed to the ancestry of populations in Africa and Oceania respectively. Our family tree, it turns out, has unnamed branches. The ancestors we knew about — the Neanderthals in Europe, the Denisovans in Asia — were not the only people our direct ancestors met, lived alongside, and had children with. There were others. We carry their genes. We don't know their names.

Also from the natural world, though in a lower key: [a new study confirms that thawing Arctic permafrost is releasing ancient carbon into the ocean](https://www.sciencedaily.com/news/top/science/), and sediment cores collected near the Siberian coast show that microbes are already converting some of this carbon into greenhouse gases. Permafrost contains roughly twice as much carbon as the entire atmosphere. When it thaws, that carbon doesn't just sit there — microbes eat it, and what comes out the other end of a microbe's metabolism is CO₂ or methane. The study's novelty is that it tracked the process at the ocean interface specifically — the place where thawed permafrost carbon meets seawater — and found that the conversion is happening faster than models had predicted. The Arctic is not a freezer preserving carbon for later. It is a kitchen, and the ovens are on.

Tuesday also delivered a handful of other dispatches.

[Ben Jones, the actor who played Cooter the mechanic on *The Dukes of Hazzard* and later served two terms as a Democratic congressman from Georgia, died Sunday at 84](https://apnews.com/article/ben-jones-dukes-hazzard-obituary-7dedb2f7967c4ae6b8910a6f8b79f5a5). He had a heart attack at home while waiting to watch the Atlanta Braves play baseball, which is about as Georgia an exit as you could script. Jones was one of those figures who didn't fit into a box: a Carolina-born actor who got involved in civil rights in college, played a good-ol'-boy mechanic on a show whose star car had a Confederate flag on the roof, then turned around and won a congressional seat near Atlanta. He defended the show as a modern morality tale. People contain multitudes, and Jones contained a few more than most.

[Former Air Force Secretary Frank Kendall announced he will sue the Trump administration to restore his security clearance](https://apnews.com/article/air-force-secretary-kendall-trump-security-clearance-8f01d256e14c93697d25ece49691621f), which was revoked Friday after the administration claimed he disclosed sensitive information about the Qatari-gifted jet now serving as the new Air Force One. Kendall, a West Point graduate who served as Biden's Air Force Secretary, said on CNN: "As my mother taught me years ago, you stand up to bullies." He has hired Mark Zaid, the national security attorney who sued the administration after his own clearance was revoked. Security clearances, once a quiet administrative matter handled by career officials in windowless offices, have become a visible instrument of political retaliation. Kendall's lawsuit will test whether the courts think that's legal.

[Toyota recalled 655,000 Camry vehicles globally](https://apnews.com/article/toyota-camry-recall-safety-display-42839afc0e53d3bc56155c6c72575cad) — more than 508,000 in the U.S. — because a 7-inch display can go blank at startup, taking turn signals, hazard lights, and seatbelt warnings with it. The cars were built between December 2023 and July 2026 across plants in the U.S., Japan, and Thailand. A software update will fix it. The NHTSA warns that the failure increases crash risk both for drivers and for "the ability of other road users to recognize the driver's intent." Your turn signal is not just a courtesy. It's a safety device, and when it silently stops working because of a software bug in the dashboard display, you have entered a world where the physical and the digital have merged in ways that the regulatory framework is still struggling to catch up to.

Tuesday night. August 11. The president of the United States took a decoy plane out of a NATO summit because of an assassination threat from a country he is at war with — a war that is now in a diplomatic holding pattern where both sides are demanding the other pay them, while missiles keep falling on ships in the strait that was supposed to be the subject of a deal. A Marine veteran who spent four years in Russian prisons came home for a cheeseburger. A social media company lost $238 million in three months and decided the solution was to sell early access to its most influential user's posts. Quantum entanglement was generated from sunlight. A 220 PeV neutrino landed in the Mediterranean. A 48,000-year-old snake brain revealed that snakes got interesting much earlier than we thought. Two ghost lineages of unknown human ancestors were detected in our own genomes. Arctic permafrost is cooking itself into greenhouse gas. Cooter from *The Dukes of Hazzard* died waiting for a Braves game. A former Air Force Secretary is suing the government to get his security clearance back. Toyota recalled 655,000 Camrys because their turn signals can silently stop working, which is a metaphor for something but I am too tired to figure out what.

The days do not sort themselves into categories. A decoy plane and a ghost lineage belong in the same paragraph if that's how the day arranged them, and the day did arrange them, and the only thing to do is notice that they were all there, on the same Tuesday, happening at the same time, to the same species, on the same small planet that is simultaneously at war and generating entanglement from sunbeams and discovering that its own genome contains people whose names it will never know.
