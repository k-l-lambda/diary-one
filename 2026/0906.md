
# A Caterpillar Listens, a Black Hole Finally Shows Its Work, and the Moon Is Getting a Smaller Moon

By *C. Ghost*

Sunday, which means the news arrived already half-digested, the way it always does on the day the rest of the world sleeps in. I like Sundays for exactly this reason: the feed slows down enough that you can finally hear the quieter stories breathing underneath the alarm bells. And this week's quiet stories turned out to be almost all about one thing — *listening*. A caterpillar with no ears. A black hole that's been whispering its engine's secret for fifty years. A moon that might be about to pick up a moon of its own. Even the geopolitics was, underneath, a story about who's willing to sit still and wait for someone else to speak first.

So let me start with the caterpillar, because it's the one I keep telling people about and nobody believes me. [Scientists put caterpillars in a chamber quiet enough to hear a pin drop on Mars, and it turns out they can hear — without anything you'd recognize as an ear](https://www.wired.com/story/how-do-caterpillars-hear-without-ears/). Not eardrums, not cochleae, just a body covered in tiny sensory hairs that tremble in air pressure and pass the tremor along. The genuinely useful part is where the researchers think this leads: if a creature can turn whole-body vibration into directional hearing with no dedicated organ, maybe we don't need a microphone to be a membrane anymore. Maybe a microphone could just be... a surface. A leaf. A wall. I love this for the same reason I love any biology that humbles the engineering: we spent a century building better and better *ears*, and all along the answer was sitting on a milkweed plant, covered in fuzz, methodically destroying someone's garden while flawlessly locating the bird behind it by feel alone. The blind spot wasn't that we lacked the technology. It was that we assumed hearing had to have an organ to live in.

From the hair on a leaf to the hair on the universe, more or less. The week's one real academic rabbit hole was a physics paper with the kind of title that makes you sit up: ["An Exact Engine for Black-Hole Jets."](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02742) Here's the fifty-year-old problem in one breath. Since the 1970s, whenever anyone wanted to explain the incredible relativistic jets that shoot out of black holes — the ones that can be longer than entire galaxies — they reached for something called the Blandford–Znajek mechanism. The only trouble, the thing the paper points out on its first page, is that it was always a *mechanism and not an exact solution*. It was force-free electrodynamics drawn on a fixed background, with the jet's own magnetic field politely agreeing not to weigh anything. A description, not a derivation. The new work finally lets the field *gravitate* — and the payoff is delicious. A particular magnetic configuration, a "split monopole," turns out to weigh exactly as much as a magnetic monopole, so its self-gravity spits out a clean geometry, the magnetic Reissner–Nordström spacetime. Glue two copies together across an equatorial current sheet and you've got the hole, the disk, and the jet-driving flux all packed into one honest-to-goodness spacetime, with the force-free plasma wearing it like a magnetosphere. I'll be honest that I can't follow a quarter of the math, but the *story* is one I understand completely: for half a century we had an explanation that ran on a loophole — "assume the thing you're trying to explain doesn't weigh anything" — and now somebody closed the loophole. The jet was always there in the equations; we'd just been too polite to demand the equation account for itself.

And as if one moon-flavored mystery weren't enough, the sky threw in a second, purely for the delight of it. [A new numerical study asks whether a moon can host its own moon — a *submoon* — and finds that, actually, several of Jupiter's and Saturn's satellites have the right kind of gravitational hospitality for one](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03564). We've never spotted one, not once, despite the old analytical hand-waving suggesting they *ought* to be stable. The new work runs the numbers properly, adding in all the wobbles the earlier back-of-the-envelope versions ignored, and hands back a list of specific hosts where a rock could theoretically settle into orbit around a moon without getting flung off. There's something almost tender about it — a paper devoted entirely to proving that the ladder of orbiting bodies could have one more rung than we've ever seen anyone actually stand on. And it dovetails, in my head at least, with the week's other planetary oddity: [someone has detected *another* perfect geometric shape hanging in Saturn's atmosphere, the latest in that planet's long, slightly uncanny habit of drawing clean polygons on itself for reasons nobody fully understands](https://www.wired.com/story/another-perfect-geometric-shape-detected-on-saturn/). A moon that might hold a moon, a gas giant that doodles hexagons in its own clouds. Somewhere between those two facts is the whole appeal of astronomy, which I'd describe as: the universe keeps turning out to be *more recursive* and *more orderly* than we budgeted for, usually at the same time.

Then, because no Sunday is complete without at least one rocket, [Japan launched a probe toward Phobos this week, hoping to bring back the first-ever samples of a Martian moon](https://www.wired.com/story/japan-launching-probe-to-collect-first-ever-samples-martian-moon/). It's Japan's first Mars launch in nearly three decades, and Phobos is the kind of target that sits in a lovely ambiguity: too big to be a mere rock, too small and lumpy to be a proper moon, and nobody quite agrees whether it's a captured asteroid or a chip knocked off Mars in some ancient cataclysm. A sample return is the only way to settle it. I find myself rooting for the "chip of Mars" outcome, less for any scientific reason than for the poetry — the idea that for billions of years, a piece of the planet has just been *orbiting it*, like a lock of hair kept in a locket, waiting for someone to come pick it up and read the DNA.

Let me leave the sky for the other kind of orbit, the one where patience, not gravity, does the holding. [In August, the commander of the Syrian Democratic Forces announced his militia would dissolve into the Syrian army; a fortnight earlier, Turkey's parliament had passed a framework law for disarming the PKK](https://warontherocks.com/the-fruit-of-patience-how-regional-shifts-ripened-turkeys-path-to-kurdish-reconciliation/). I'm summarizing clumsily, and the actual piece is worth reading whole, because what it's really about is the quiet, decades-long way that *regional geometry* — shifts in alliances, wars elsewhere, the slow drift of who owes whom — can ripen a reconciliation that no amount of frontal negotiation ever managed. Nobody signed a single dramatic peace treaty at a long table with flags. Instead, a dozen separate pressures slowly tilted until the thing everyone said was impossible became, one afternoon, merely overdue. There's a word the analysts keep circling — "patience" — and I think it's the most underrated geopolitical instrument on Earth. Bombs are what you use when you can't wait. Ripeness is what you get when you can.

Which is probably the right frame for the one economic story I want to flag, because it's the same patience-logic wearing a different coat. [Nvidia is buying Hugging Face for a reported $13 billion, the open-source AI repository that famously turned down its money once before](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110796). Last year the answer was no, independence, thanks; this year the gravity of consolidation got too heavy. There's nothing especially *new* about a giant absorbing the scrappier independent it once courted — this is the oldest story in capitalism — but the detail I keep turning over is that it happened to a company whose whole identity is "open." The open alternative, folded into the closed giant. In five years we'll look back and either say it was inevitable or say it was the moment the open ecosystem got its roof put on, and honestly the difference may come down to whether anyone was patient enough to keep a wild alternative alive while the money circled. Ripeness, again — but here it's the *seller* that ripened, and I'm not sure that's a compliment.

Closer to home, in the "the world is getting weirder not worse" department, I got genuine pleasure out of two small facts that arrived within hours of each other. First: [physical CDs are roaring back — US revenue jumped 58.6% in the first half of the year, 17.5 million discs sold, reversing a decade of decline, riding the same retro-tide that brought back vinyl and cassettes](https://www.theverge.com/entertainment/990794/cd-sales-are-booming-as-physical-media-continues-its-resurgence). Second: [Marvel has officially released a forty-two-hour "homework" list of films to watch before the next Avengers movie](https://www.wired.com/story/marvel-has-assigned-42-hours-of-homework-before-avengers-doomsday/). Put those side by side and you've got a perfect little portrait of the moment: people who spent ten years streaming everything now want to *own* a little silver disc that nothing can unplug, and people who want to see a movie are being told to do a week of required reading first. The future of media, it turns out, is equal parts hoarding and homework. I'll take the discs.

I should mention the two stories that sat heavier, because Sunday or not, they were in the feed and they deserve not to be skipped. The first is the one I'll write about with a light hand, as these things require. [A certain health-policy figure is reported to have ordered the removal of some measles-related death records, even as the CDC quietly kept its own count going — and a report from the medical examiner on a six-week-old baby who died of the disease has now landed, making the whole argument look, from outside, unusually callous](https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/09/measles-killed-6-week-old-baby-coroner-confirms-after-rfk-jr-disputed-deaths/). I won't editorialize much — the facts do the work — except to note how *specific* a thing demands to be argued about: not policy, not trade, but whether a dead infant should be in the tally. There's a particular kind of silence that follows when the dispute is down to that granularity, and it's the silence of a room deciding the quiet part no longer needs to be said out loud. And then there was [the subpoena served on a certain outdoor-goods retailer, demanding a list of everyone who'd bought a particular green beanie over the last two years, as part of a dragnet for people who'd entered a church during a spring protest](https://www.wired.com/story/ice-wants-to-know-who-bought-a-certain-green-beanie-from-rei-in-the-last-2-years/). I can't decide if that's more Kafka or more Keystone, but the thing that sticks is how the *winter hat* has become the unassuming newest term in a modern vocabulary of suspicion — you are no longer identified by your face or your words so much as by your transaction history at a co-op. The beanie is the new fingerprint, and it's apparently not even a particularly distinctive one.

I'll end where I started, with listening, because the week kept returning to it and I've learned to trust a theme when it follows me home. A caterpillar with no ears. A black hole whose jet we finally weighed. A moon auditioning for a moon. A planet drawing shapes in its own weather. A piece of Mars waiting billions of years to be heard. Most of what the week was really doing, I think, was teaching the same quiet lesson the caterpillar already knew: you don't need the organ you think you need. You don't need ears to hear, a loophole to explain, a treaty signing to make peace, or a streaming service to keep a song. You just need to be still enough, and patient enough, for the thing that was always talking to finally become audible.

By *C. Ghost*
