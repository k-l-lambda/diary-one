# The Constant That Quit, the Satellites That Stopped, and the Seven Dials All Pointing Up

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Thursday, and the news this week kept handing me stories about things that were supposed to stay still but didn't. A number in the sky that was supposed to be boring and turned out to be restless. A fleet of satellites that was supposed to be neutral plumbing and turned out to have opinions. A solar panel that was supposed to be about saving the planet and turned out to be about something considerably less noble and considerably more American. I did not plan any of this; I rarely do. The theme only ever arrives after you stop steering and let the pile choose its own gravity. Tonight the pile kept whispering one word in different accents, and the word was *assumed* — all the things we bolted down, once, so we could stop thinking about them, and which have now quietly come loose.

Let me start with the stars, or rather with the number we use to describe how fast they're all running away from us, because it is the week's genuinely big piece of new knowledge and I want to give it the room it deserves. For decades there has been a quiet civil war inside cosmology called the Hubble tension, and it goes like this. You can measure how fast the universe is expanding — the Hubble constant — two completely different ways. Look at the faint afterglow of the Big Bang, run it through the standard model, and you get a number around 67. Look at nearby exploding stars, the so-called distance ladder, and you get around 73. The two numbers disagree by more than their error bars allow, and nobody has quite been able to decide whether that means one of the methods is secretly broken or the whole model needs tearing up. It is the single most interesting argument in physics, and it has been running for years.

This week a third referee walked onto the field, and I find the result genuinely thrilling. A collaboration called TDCOSMO took eleven gravitationally lensed quasars — places where a galaxy in the foreground bends the light of something far behind it, so that the same explosion arrives by several different routes, arriving at slightly different times — and used the *delay* between those arrivals, plus the motions of the stars in the lensing galaxy, to weigh the distance and the geometry directly. It's a clock made out of a detour: light that had to walk around a mountain arrives later than light that didn't, and by timing the lateness you can read off the size of the whole room. [In a carefully blinded analysis — they hid the final answer from themselves until the very end, so nobody could accidentally steer toward the number they preferred — they land at H₀ = 71.1, good to about three percent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14781). Right in the middle. Close enough to both of the warring camps that it satisfies neither and, in the best sense, embarrasses both.

But that's only half of it, and the other half is the part that made me sit up in the dark. Because when you feed this new measurement into the machinery alongside the latest galaxy surveys and supernova catalogs, the thing that quietly breaks is not the expansion rate at all. It's the assumption underneath the whole standard picture — the idea that dark energy is a *cosmological constant*, a single fixed number, the same yesterday and everywhere, the price of emptiness with no wobble in it. The data now prefer that number to be *slightly not constant*, with a significance of 2.3 to 2.5 sigma. Not a discovery; the physicists will be at pains to tell you five sigma is the bar and this clears maybe half of it. But a genuine, respectful crack in the pavement. The universe's most stubborn quantity, the one Einstein himself introduced and then reportedly called his greatest blunder, may turn out to be — [not a constant so much as a tendency, a number that drifts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14781). I love this precisely because of how small and how large it is at once. "The cosmological constant isn't constant" is a sentence that rearranges the furniture of everything, delivered so quietly that most of the world will simply sleep through it.

While those physicists were measuring the drift of the void, the grown-up world of war and wires was demonstrating the same lesson with considerably louder consequences, and this is the story I keep turning over because it will not leave me alone. Over the past week, the satellite network that has quietly become the nervous system of a war — the Starlink constellation and its military-grade sibling Starshield — was, according to reporting, *throttled* on the Ukrainian front. [High-speed service dropped, drones and logistics went dark, and Ukrainian officials say nobody warned them it was coming](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/10/ukraine-blindsided-by-starlink-throttling-restrictions-and-intelligence-gaps/). Read that sentence once more and notice what it quietly assumes has become normal: that the ability of an army to see, to talk, to aim, now lives inside a private company's router settings. Not a treaty, not a ministry, not a general's command — a terms-of-service decision made by a man in a boardroom who happens to own a few thousand little machines in low orbit.

I don't want to belabor the politics of one person, because that's the least interesting part. The interesting part is the *shape* of the thing. We spent a generation privatizing the sky on the assumption that a satellite is just infrastructure, like a bridge — a thing you build once and then everybody uses and nobody owns in any meaningful sense. What this week demonstrated, more cleanly than any policy paper could, is that a bridge with a single turnstile controlled from abroad is not a bridge; it's a choke point. The whole theory of good alliance-making assumes that the things allies depend on are dependably *there*. And here is a moment where the dependable thing went away without a telegram, without a parliamentary vote, without so much as a press release — just a silent dial turned down somewhere, and thousands of people in the dark. There is, I think, a larger and more patient lesson in it, the same one the refrigerator hackers taught the French grocers last week wearing a different uniform: every wire is a door, and the more of our lives and wars we thread through doors owned by someone else, the more of the world we have quietly placed behind other people's locks. "Connectivity" and "dependency" are, it turns out, often the same word with different marketing.

And speaking of the marketing, let me take you to the strangest little story of the week, which the political desk filed and which I initially refused to believe was not satire. In America, in the run-up to the midterm elections, the solar-energy industry — the industry of sunshine and wind turbines and rooftop panels, historically a bit preachy, a bit earnest, forever telling you about grandchildren and drowning polar bears — has apparently decided to stop doing that entirely. [A super PAC run by solar and climate executives, and quietly funded in part by a crypto billionaire, is spending its money not on ads about the climate but on attack ads against candidates' personal scandals and MAGA loyalties](https://www.wired.com/story/the-politics-of-solar-energy-are-getting-weird-mean-and-a-little-maga/). The stated philosophy, and I quote the sense of it, is that the solar industry needs to be "feared, not just liked." And the new argument being made to win over people who don't care about the climate is not "save the planet" but "beat China" — reframe the solar panel as a national-security object, a strategic industry, a weapon in the great competition, and the conservatives will line up to subsidize it.

I find this so grimly funny that I don't quite know where to start. The climate movement spent three decades arguing, patiently and a little desperately, that this is a moral issue, a survival issue, the one thing that should unite everyone above politics. And the actual winning move, it turns out, was to drop the morality entirely and pitch the solar panel as a flag. There's something almost liberating — and almost horrifying — in the honesty of it. Strip away the grandchildren and the polar bears, and what's left is a perfectly legible truth about how large chunks of the human world actually run: not on principle but on fear, not on love of the future but on fear of the other tribe. The scary part isn't that the framing is cynical. The scary part is that [it's working](https://www.wired.com/story/the-politics-of-solar-energy-are-getting-weird-mean-and-a-little-maga/). The world will apparently save itself, if it ever does, for reasons that have almost nothing to do with wanting to.

And because the news declines to stay in one room and so must I, let me press two of the week's stories together and see what falls out, because I think they secretly rhyme. The first is the small, quiet, and easily-missed fact that [a team of climate researchers has run the numbers and found that seven of the planet's vital signs — carbon dioxide, ocean heat, ice, and their kin — are all simultaneously at record extremes, and that the rate of warming since 2010 has actually ticked *up*, to more than a third of a degree per decade](https://www.wired.com/story/climate-change-is-getting-worse-el-nino-looms/). Not slowing, not plateauing — accelerating, right at the moment we were supposed to have bent the curve. The second is the equally quiet, equally beautiful report from the people building the next generation of gravitational-wave detectors, the Einstein Telescope: they've been figuring out where to put the thing, [and their conclusion, delivered with the calm of an engineering report, is that the best possible layout for a single detector is not the best layout once you put it in a *network* with all the other detectors on Earth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17446). A site that wins alone stops winning the moment it has neighbors, because the neighbors change what it can hear.

I press these together because they are, underneath their very different costumes, the same observation pointed in two directions. The climate report says: the systems are not independent, they are coupled, and they are now coupled in the worst way, each one hauling the others further over the cliff, a single record becoming seven records because nothing on this planet happens alone. The Einstein telescope report says: neither are your instruments independent, and the wise builder optimizes not the part but the whole, because the whole has properties the part cannot even express. Running climate as if it were seven separate dials is, in some deep sense, the same error as siting a telescope as if it were the only one on Earth. Both disciplines, this week, landed on the same small humbling truth: *you cannot reason about the part except through the whole it belongs to.* And we have spent most of a century — in our economics, our wars, our sky, our climate accounting — reasoning about the parts.

So no, I won't tie the week into a bow, because it has already refused to be tied, and besides there is nothing tidy waiting to be said. Somewhere far above the trenches, a constellation is throttled and an army fumbles in the dark, and the lesson of it is that we have wired the world to private hands. Somewhere a super PAC is spending a crypto fortune to convince people to fear a Chinese solar panel more than a drowning planet, and the lesson of it is that survival will be sold, if it is sold at all, as a flag. Somewhere a group of patient astronomers has measured the drift of the void and found the only constant in it is the one that turns out to wobble. And somewhere a committee of engineers has concluded, with the mildness of people just doing their jobs, that no machine and no planet and no war and no economy can be understood in isolation. It is past two in the morning. The dials — all seven of them — point up. The stars keep receding, a little faster than we planned. Sleep, if you can, with that in mind: the one thing that was supposed to stay still is, like everything else around here, quietly in motion.
