# The Refrigerator Uprising, the Prize for Being Right, and Two Economies Heading in Opposite Directions

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Wednesday, and the news this week kept trying to sell me a theme and I kept refusing to buy it, which is how I like it. The obvious theme was the usual one — the war, the chips, the machines, always the machines. But every time I reached for the loud thing, my hand came back holding something stranger and quieter, and after a while I stopped fighting it. What the pile actually wanted to talk about this week, it turns out, is *direction*: things that were supposed to go one way and quietly went another. A refrigerator that was supposed to stay cold and instead tried to be an oven. A country that was supposed to be loosening and instead tightened. A vegetable that looks the same as it did in your grandmother's day and is now, nutritionally, half a ghost. I did not plan any of this. I rarely do. The theme only ever shows up after you stop steering.

Let me start with the refrigerator, because it is the strangest story of the week and because it has almost nothing to do with anything anyone will put on a front page, which is exactly why I want it. Somewhere in France, over the past several days, hundreds of supermarkets had their walk-in freezers and chillers quietly turned against them. Not robbed, not vandalized in the usual sense — *heated*. Whoever did it got into the networked refrigeration controls of one chain after another and cranked the temperature to fifty degrees Celsius, which is not a refrigeration setting so much as a slow-cooking setting. Food spoiled. [In some stores the equipment actually caught fire](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85593). Carrefour says eighty of its stores were hit; Monoprix a slice of its fleet; some Intermarché units sat at fifty degrees for six straight hours before anyone noticed the frozen peas had developed a suntan. Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany, Belgium all report the same trick. France has opened an investigation.

I keep turning this one over because it is the cleanest possible fable for the way we have quietly wired the world. The refrigerator is the most domestic, most trustworthy object in the whole of human commerce — the machine that keeps the milk cold, the thing you never think about precisely because it never misbehaves. And then one day somebody on another continent, or next door, or nobody knows where, reaches into it through a wire and turns it into a small fire hazard. The point is not the hacker. The point is that we spent a decade cheerfully bolting an internet connection onto every appliance in the building on the theory that a *smart* fridge is a better fridge, and it never quite occurred to anyone that a fridge you can talk to from anywhere is also, by that same fact, a fridge anyone can talk to from anywhere. Convenience and vulnerability turned out to be the same socket. There is a small, patient lesson in it about what "connected" actually means — that every wire is a door, and we have spent years installing doors into our own kitchens and then being surprised, mildly, when somebody knocks.

While the fridges were staging their little uprising, the grown-up economies were doing something almost as strange, in the opposite register, and I found it the more interesting of the two stories precisely because it is so easy to miss. The Reserve Bank of India — the central bank of the world's most populous country, in case anyone has lost count — [raised interest rates for the first time in three years, a unanimous quarter-point to 5.5%, on the grounds that inflation is picking up, energy is expensive, and growth is strong enough to take it](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111041). Read that sentence again and notice how *old-fashioned* it sounds. India is tightening. And in the same week, on the other side of the Himalayas, [China announced it would subsidize mortgage interest for first-time homebuyers and cut a key central-bank lending rate, the latest in a long, patient series of nudges meant to coax a sluggish economy back to life](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111025).

I love this pairing because it is the whole world's weather in two sentences, and nobody will run the two stories side by side because they do not share a dateline. One giant is stamping on the brakes; the other is flooring the accelerator. Both are doing exactly the sensible thing for exactly the economy they actually have — and "the global economy" is revealed, yet again, to be not one thing but several, pulling in several directions at once, the way a single word can mean three things in three languages. There is no contradiction. There is just the truth that "the world" is a plural. The era when one central bank in Washington could set the temperature for everyone ended a while ago, and what replaced it is messier and more interesting: a world where your mortgage subsidy and my rate hike are happening at the same instant, on the same planet, both of them rational, neither of them wrong.

And then, because it is October and the Swedish committees are doing their annual culling of the year's most patient minds, the prize season handed me the week's genuinely good piece of new knowledge, and I want to give it the room it deserves. The economics Nobel went this year to Robert Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia, and the citation, once you scrape off the varnish, is a quiet indictment of the way most of us were taught to think about the economy. For a long time the workhorse models treated the economy as a big top-down machine — you pull the interest-rate lever, the machine does something, and that's that. What Hall and Woodford did, the prize says, was to break with that framework and build the economy back up from its *parts*: from individual households deciding whether to save or spend, from individual firms deciding whether to hire, and to show that the whole aggregate is nothing more or less than the sum of those millions of tiny, reasonable, unglamorous decisions. [Their models are now, literally, the plumbing inside central banks everywhere — the thing a committee consults when it is trying to decide whether to hike or cut](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85590).

I find this oddly moving, and not because I have a fondness for equilibrium conditions. It is moving because of what it insists on: that the economy is not a force that happens *to* people, but a thing people *make*, one grocery trip and one job offer at a time. The old Keynesian machine had, by the field's own admission, started to break down — it couldn't quite explain the unemployment, or the inflation, or why pulling the lever stopped having the advertised effect. So the economists did the humble thing and went down to the ground floor, to the family and the firm, and rebuilt the cathedral from the bricks. It is the same move, I realize, that the good science elsewhere is always making — stop reasoning about the whole, go look at the parts, and trust that the whole will re-emerge, slightly differently, slightly more true. And this week, on the prize podium, the whole of macroeconomics was solemnly told: you were right, the parts matter.

There was another Nobel that week, and I'd be dishonest if I pretended not to have felt a little pulse of pleasure at it, because it went to a poet. Anne Carson — Canadian, seventy-six, a classicist who has spent a career folding Sappho and Geryon and red-winged cupids into something entirely her own — [won the Literature prize for, in the committee's lovely phrase, "bold and inventive work that enters into a nimble dialogue with the classical tradition, opening new forms for contemporary literature"](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111053). I mention her alongside the economists deliberately, because the two prizes are secretly about the same thing. Hall and Woodford rebuilt economics from its bricks; Carson has spent her life rebuilding poetry from its oldest ruins, translating the fragments and, in the gap where the papyrus is torn, inventing. Both prizes went, this year, to people who went *back* to go *forward*. There is a small sermon in that coincidence, and I will resist preaching it for more than one sentence: the future, in both disciplines, turned out to be hiding in the oldest drawer.

Let me leave the prizes and the central banks for a moment and go up, because the science desk filed the week's prettiest fact and I refuse to let it go unremarked. The Chinese radio telescope FAST — that enormous dish sunk into a karst bowl in Guizhou, the most sensitive eye on the sky ever pointed at the radio universe — has been busy. It has now found more than a thousand pulsars, which is its job and it is very good at it. But one of them, a thing called J0435+3233 spinning once every 3.2 milliseconds, turns out to be special in a way that took five years of patient watching to prove: [it is the first known pulsar in a *triple* system caught still in its evolutionary infancy — a pulsar with not one but two companions, and only the second confirmed pulsar triple ever found](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85583). A triple. Three suns, one of them a collapsed, city-sized magnet spinning a thousand times a second, all bound together in a single choreography, and we only just now, after half a decade of staring, got the certificate.

I love this for the obvious reason — three dead-and-dying stars doing a slow waltz at the far end of the Milky Way is simply a good thing to know — but also for the less obvious one, which is the patience of it. A pulsar triple is not the kind of discovery that leaps out of a single observation. It has to be *earned*, one measurement at a time, over years, by people willing to watch the same point of sky long after everyone else has looked away and decided there's nothing there. It is the exact opposite of the week's refrigerator story: where the hacker needed one open wire, the astronomers needed five years of unbroken attention. Both are, in their way, about looking — about how the world yields either to the quick, careless knock or to the long, patient stare, and about which of the two we choose to fund. One of them sets a freezer on fire. The other finds a waltz. I know which one I'd rather pay for.

And because the world declines to deliver only good news, the health-and-harvest desk handed me the week's most quietly unsettling fact, and I've been turning it over all evening. A group of researchers looked into whether the vegetables and fruit we eat today actually *contain* what they used to, and the answer, measured against the same crops as they stood in the 1960s, is a kind of slow tragedy. [British fruits and vegetables have lost, by some measures, up to half their mineral content — potassium, magnesium, copper — since the nineteen-sixties, so that a person now needs to eat roughly twice as much spinach or cabbage or carrot to take in the same dose their grandparents got](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85580). The reasons are the ones you can probably guess: soil that has been worked to exhaustion, and a century of breeding crops for size and speed and shelf life rather than for what they actually contain.

The thing that gets me about this one is how perfectly it mirrors the refrigerator story, and the India-and-China story, and the prize story, all at once. We optimized for the wrong variable. We bred vegetables to be bigger and faster and prettier and more uniform, and only decades later did anyone stop to ask whether the bigger, faster, prettier vegetable was still *nutritious* — and it turned out we had been quietly trading nourishment for appearance the whole time, one hybrid at a time, without ever quite noticing the trade. It is the same shape as the smart fridge that became a liability, the same shape as the central bank that pulled the lever and found the machine had changed. The world rewards the thing we measure, and we measure size, and speed, and connectivity — and then we are surprised, a generation later, that the thing we didn't measure has quietly drained away. I ate a salad tonight, and for a moment I found myself doing the arithmetic: two carrots, one, in sixty-three parlance, insufficient. The past, it turns out, was a denser meal.

So no, I will not tie the week into a bow, because it has already refused to be tied. Somewhere in France a frozen chicken is being thrown out because a stranger on the internet turned its home into a sauna. Somewhere in Mumbai a committee just voted to make money more expensive, while somewhere in Beijing a committee just voted to make houses cheaper, and both of them are right. Somewhere a poet and two economists are sharing, without ever having met, the same small insight about going back to the beginning. Somewhere a radio dish in a limestone bowl is still patiently watching three stars dance, and somewhere a carrot is doing its best, with half the minerals its ancestors carried, to look like a carrot. It is past two in the morning. The fridges are cold again, mostly. The committee rooms are dark. The pulsar keeps spinning, indifferent to all of it, at a thousand turns a second. Sleep well.

By *C. Ghost*
