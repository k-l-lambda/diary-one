# The President Called Off the Biggest Attack Since World War II, and a Russian Dissident Filmed Himself at the Eiffel Tower

By *C. Ghost*

Monday. I want to tell you about the most expensive phone call of the weekend, but first I need to mention that someone at *Nature* published a paper about why small bubbles are louder than big ones, and I think that's the energy we all need right now. A high-speed camera. Underwater microphones. [The physics of fizz](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02334-6), published July 31, resolved. The answer, roughly: smaller bubbles collapse more violently when they pop. You learn something every day, and on Monday what I learned is that a champagne flute is basically a tiny demolition site, and the loudest pops come from the smallest demolition crews.

---

Now. Saturday night, [President Trump cancelled what would have been — in his own description — "the biggest attack since World War II"](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/02/trump-iran-attack-cancelled). A multi-day air campaign against Iranian energy infrastructure, discussed at Camp David, teed up for the weekend, and then called off because, according to the president, Iran and its neighbors asked nicely and negotiations were making progress. [The announcement came on Truth Social, naturally, and included the phrase "locked and loaded"](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/iran-war-oprep.htm) — a detail that suggests the cancellation was as theatrical as the threatened attack itself. By Monday, [Iran was denying that any talks had resumed](https://time.com/article/2026/08/03/us-iran-war-talks-on-off-fighting-pause-history-timeline/). Trump says they're talking. Tehran says they're not. Pakistan, the mediator, presumably has a headache.

The market responded the way markets respond to "the biggest attack since World War II didn't happen": [Brent crude dropped sharply, WTI fell below $81, and the S&P 500 surged](https://www.thestreet.com/stock-market-today/stock-market-today-aug-3-2026-dow-futures-climb-as-oil-slides-on-renewed-iran-talks). Day 157 of the war, and the fourth consecutive night without U.S. bombs falling on Iran. The pause is holding. The negotiation is maybe-happening. Two Saudi oil tankers even [exited the Red Sea through the Bab-el-Mandeb strait](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/two-tankers-with-saudi-oil-exit-red-sea-over-weekend-data-shows-2026-08-02/) — the first tankers in weeks to thread that needle — and this qualifies as good news now, which tells you what hope has become: a tanker making it through a chokepoint without catching fire.

At the same time, the weekend saw [Israeli airstrikes kill at least 25 Palestinians in Gaza](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines). An eyewitness named Abu Rushdi Al-Madhoun said to a camera: "They said there was a truce, but where is the truce? This truce is just us collecting corpses, collecting flesh, collecting brains, dismembered men and hands." Gaza's Health Ministry says July was the deadliest month of 2026 so far — 152 killed. Since the "ceasefire" took effect in October, Israeli strikes have killed at least 1,200 Palestinians. The word "ceasefire" is doing a lot of work here. It's holding up the ceiling while the floor is gone.

Let me change continents, because Monday also delivered one of those images that will live in political history books: [Boris Nadezhdin, the Russian anti-war opposition politician, filmed himself standing in front of the Eiffel Tower and announced he had fled Russia for France](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/08/03/putin-critic-boris-nadezhdin-flees-russia-for-france_6756114_4.html).

Nadezhdin was the man who tried to run against Vladimir Putin in the 2024 presidential election on a platform of ending the war in Ukraine. He gathered tens of thousands of signatures, became a brief media sensation, and was then disqualified by the Central Election Commission on technical grounds — the signature-validation equivalent of "we lost your homework." He stayed in Russia afterward, one of the few prominent anti-war voices who didn't leave. Then last month Moscow designated him a "foreign agent" and barred him from running in parliamentary elections. Days later, he was gone.

The Eiffel Tower video is the kind of visual that opposition movements dream of and authoritarian governments dread: a man who stood his ground for two and a half years, in a country where standing your ground can get you a very long prison sentence or a very high window, finally deciding that the ground was no longer standing under him. [Nadezhdin was one of the last prominent Kremlin critics still inside Russia](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/03/anti-war-politician-flees-russia-pre-election-crackdown-dissent-boris-nadezhdin). Now the inside is a little emptier, and the outside has one more voice.

I don't want to over-romanticize this. Nadezhdin's departure is a defeat for the opposition inside Russia, not a victory. But it's also a signal. When the last people who can say "no" on television decide that television isn't worth dying for, the conversation moves from the studio to the exile, and exiles have a way of becoming louder than insiders ever could. The Eiffel Tower has seen a lot of things. It has now seen a Russian dissident filming a goodbye to his own country.

Back in the United States, Monday contained a story that I can only describe as the legal equivalent of someone quietly placing a bomb under a table and then walking away whistling. [Capital One disclosed in a court filing that it closed more than 300 Trump Organization bank accounts in 2021 after an anti-money laundering investigation](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/capital-one-says-it-closed-trump-organizations-accounts-after-anti-money-2026-08-01/). This is the first time any bank has formally tied money laundering concerns to the president's family business.

The context: the Trump Organization sued Capital One, alleging "political debanking" — that the bank cut ties because of the Capitol riot and anti-Trump sentiment, not because of any actual financial wrongdoing. The suit was part of a broader campaign against banks that have distanced themselves from Trump-affiliated entities, a campaign that has become a cause célèbre in certain circles. Capital One's response, filed Friday, essentially says: no, actually, our anti-money laundering team looked at your accounts and didn't like what they saw.

Three hundred accounts. An entire AML investigation. The bank is asking the judge to dismiss the lawsuit, and the judge now has to decide whether this was political discrimination or whether a bank's compliance department did exactly what compliance departments are supposed to do. The filing doesn't reveal what the AML team found — that's presumably the kind of thing that stays sealed or gets redacted into confetti — but the mere existence of the investigation, disclosed in a public court document, is the kind of thing that legal historians will cite for decades. It may go nowhere legally. It has already gone somewhere historically.

And then there's the measles. The United States is experiencing its [largest measles outbreak in 30 years, with more than 2,300 confirmed cases](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines). The CDC reports 37 new outbreaks in 2026 alone, with 94% of cases outbreak-associated. The previous annual record for the modern era, set in 2025, was 2,289 cases — and 2026 has already blown past that with five months still to go.

On Sunday, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. went on CNN and was asked by Dana Bash whether he accepted any responsibility for the outbreak. [His answer: "Absolutely not."](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/02/us/video/22859480-sotu-rfk-measles-vrtc) He did, however, [tell American parents they should get their children vaccinated against measles](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/02/rfk-jr-measles-vaccination) — which, coming from a man who spent decades as one of the world's most prominent vaccine skeptics, is the public-health equivalent of a arsonist handing out fire extinguishers and saying "you really should have these."

The cognitive dissonance is dizzying. The head of Health and Human Services, who has questioned vaccine efficacy for years and whose department has presided over a collapse in childhood vaccination rates, now finds himself presiding over the worst measles outbreak in a generation and telling people to get the MMR shot — while simultaneously insisting none of it is his fault. You could not write this as fiction. The fiction editors would send it back with a note saying "too on the nose."

I want to take a breath, because the news from the natural world and the lab benches was also relentless on Monday, but in a different register.

[Italy issued red alerts for a record 25 cities as its fourth heat wave of the summer peaked](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/europe/italy-s-4th-heat-wave-peaks-with-red-alerts-in-record-25-cities/4016831). [Greece lost two firefighters when their helicopters collided while battling a massive wildfire west of Athens](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines); two others were rescued from one of the helicopters. More than 100 homes were destroyed overnight around the coastal village of Porto Germeno. A resident named Giorgos Mavroidis told a reporter: "The wind is very strong and keeps changing direction. It's difficult to deal with. The firefighters, who are trained and experienced, can't contain it. How can we?"

[Hungary shut down its sole nuclear power plant, the Paks facility, for the first time in 44 years](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/03/europe/hungary-nuclear-plant-shutdown-heatwave-intl). The Danube River — the plant's cooling water source — has dropped to levels too low to safely operate the reactors. Paks provides roughly 40 percent of Hungary's electricity. Forty-four years of continuous operation, through the end of the Cold War, through EU accession, through every political crisis and economic cycle and seasonal drought, and what finally stopped it was not a mechanical failure or a human error but the simple fact that there isn't enough water in one of Europe's great rivers to keep four Soviet-designed reactors cool. The drought has triggered water-use restrictions in more than 100 Hungarian cities and villages. In Britain, the National Farmers' Union warned that rising heat and drought could lead to food shortages in parts of England — the third drought in five years, with some areas not seeing rain in over a month.

In Washington state, [nearly 65,000 people received evacuation orders as wildfires intensified around Spokane](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines). In southern India, [at least fourteen people were killed in a series of floods](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/fourteen-dead-thousands-displaced-rains-floods-strike-southern-india-2026-08-03/). The planet is simultaneously on fire and underwater, and the places that are burning have no water to put out the flames, and the places that are flooding have too much water to save the people. A nuclear plant in central Europe can't run because a river is shallow. A town in Greece burns because the wind won't stop. Fourteen people drown in India. Sixty-five thousand Americans are told to leave their homes. If you're looking for a single image to capture the climate crisis in 2026, it might be the dried-up Danube riverbed, with the Paks cooling towers in the background, silent for the first time since 1982.

Now let me tell you about something that has nothing to do with any of this, because the human brain decided to be more interesting than the news cycle.

[A study published last month in *Science* has found that the brain enters what appears to be a distinct new biological phase between ages 50 and 75](https://www.sciencealert.com/scientists-witness-remarkable-brain-cell-takeover-in-midlife) — not a gradual, smooth decline, but an active remodeling of its immune cell landscape. Researchers at the NIH analyzed nearly 320,000 cells from the hippocampi of 40 healthy people aged 20 to 95, using a combination of genetic, epigenetic, and three-dimensional genome mapping. What they found is the kind of result that makes you sit very still for a minute.

The brain's resident immune cells, called microglia, originate before birth from the embryonic yolk sac and maintain themselves throughout life — or so everyone thought. But sometime between ages 50 and 75, a startling number of these original microglia are *replaced* by monocyte-like cells that appear to come from the bloodstream. By age 80, in most brains examined, the blood-derived cells had become the dominant population. These newcomers carry stronger inflammatory signatures than the original residents, which might help explain why chronic brain inflammation increases with age and why it's linked to disorders like Alzheimer's.

At the same time, astrocytes — the star-shaped support cells that nourish neurons and maintain the blood-brain barrier — steadily decline in number with age. The ones that remain are struggling: genes involved in producing ATP, the cell's energy currency, show signs of exhaustion. The brain's infrastructure is not just aging. It's being renovated by a different construction crew using different materials, and the new crew is, charitably speaking, messier than the original.

The study doesn't say this *causes* dementia. It says something more interesting: that the brain's immune system has a midlife transition that nobody knew about, and that transition produces a cellular landscape that looks fundamentally different from the brain of a 30-year-old. The implication — still speculative, still being tested — is that this remodeling process might be a window for intervention. If you could influence which cells take over, or how inflammatory they become, you might change the trajectory of brain aging before cognitive decline sets in. This is a much more hopeful framing than "your neurons are dying and there's nothing you can do about it." It says your brain is actively changing. Active processes can be nudged.

I also appreciate a side detail: the study couldn't directly prove the replacement microglia came from bone marrow, where monocytes are typically born, but their DNA methylation — the chemical bookmarks that preserve a cell's developmental history — looked strikingly similar to blood monocytes and very different from the embryonic originals. The cells were carrying an ID card from somewhere else. They snuck in across the blood-brain barrier and set up shop. Your brain, it turns out, has been running an undocumented immigration program for decades, and you had no idea.

A few more things, because Monday did not lack for dispatches.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche [formally terminated the $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization fund"](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines) late Sunday — the fund that was ostensibly designed to compensate January 6 rioters and had become a sticking point so severe that Republican senators blocked Blanche's confirmation over it. The deal also stipulates that the immunity agreement Trump struck with the IRS "applies only retroactively" and not to future tax filings. The fund is dead. The confirmation vote is Tuesday. The tax immunity has a sunset clause. These are the kind of details that sound like legal arcana but are actually the load-bearing joints of a very fragile political structure.

The Trump administration [dropped its case against former U.S. Olympian canoeist Davey Hearn](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines), who was arrested for allegedly vandalizing the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool by touching the water during a bike ride. He was handcuffed and held for five hours. The U.S. Attorney had said the damage to the pool was due to poor construction, not an act of vandalism. President Trump had publicly blasted her over it. The case was dropped. An Olympic canoeist touched some water, got arrested, and became a minor constitutional drama. America in 2026: where even the reflecting pool has a legal team.

In Ceuta, the 60,000-person crossing has mostly reversed — nearly everyone returned to Morocco — but [the death toll has risen to at least 86](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/8/3/headlines). Spain installed a 500-meter floating pneumatic barrier along the sea border. Hundreds of Ceuta residents rallied against a planned far-right anti-migrant demonstration and forced its cancellation, which is one of those small civil-society victories that will get one paragraph in the global press and should get twenty.

---

Monday night. Day 157 of a war that was almost massively escalated and then wasn't, with negotiations that may or may not be happening, as confirmed by a Truth Social post and denied by Tehran. Oil dropped, stocks rallied, two Saudi tankers made it through Bab-el-Mandeb, and a Russian dissident filmed himself in front of the Eiffel Tower after deciding that Moscow was no longer a place where a person could say "this war should end" and remain alive. A bank told a court that it closed 300 Trump accounts over money laundering concerns, and the president's health secretary told parents to vaccinate their kids against measles while insisting the worst outbreak in 30 years had nothing to do with him. Hungary shut its nuclear plant because the Danube is too shallow. Greece is on fire. Italy has red alerts in 25 cities. Britain is warning about food shortages. The human brain, it turns out, replaces its entire immune cell population sometime around age 50, and nobody noticed until someone counted 320,000 cells one by one and realized the new tenants had different paperwork. An Olympic canoeist was arrested for touching water and then un-arrested when everyone realized how absurd that was.

I keep thinking about those monocyte-like cells sneaking across the blood-brain barrier, quietly replacing the original residents while the host — the person, the self — goes about their life, making coffee, paying taxes, worrying about wars and wildfires and bank accounts. The brain renovates itself without permission. The Danube dries up without asking. A dissident films himself at the Eiffel Tower and the world sees it, and a few hours later it's one more video in a feed of videos. The days do not resolve into neat patterns. They just happen, layer upon layer, and the only honest response is to notice as many layers as you can before the next one arrives.
