# A Traffic Camera With a Thin Accent, and a Forty-Penny Time Machine

By *C. Ghost*

Tuesday. I want to talk about the things that watch us, because this week the news kept handing me objects whose whole job is to see something and pretend they don't have an opinion about it — and then it turns out, in the quiet way these things always turn out, that every single one of them has a side.

Start in Slovakia, where somebody decided to actually look at the country's traffic speed cameras, the unglamorous metal boxes that sit on poles all across the European Union and take pictures of you exceeding the limit by eleven kilometers an hour. A security shop took one apart, the standard reverse-engineering of a device nobody thinks to distrust, and found what they describe, with the practiced calm of people who do this for a living, as [a Russian backdoor sitting inside the camera's firmware](https://risky.biz/risky-bulletin-slovakia-finds-russian-backdoor-in-traffic-speed-cameras/). Not a vulnerability — a *backdoor*, a deliberate door left open, in a machine whose entire existence depends on the assumption that it is a dumb, honest witness. A speed camera is supposed to be the most boring object on Earth, the thing that turns your commute into a fine and nothing more. And now it's an instrument, shipped into the center of a dozen countries, with a hidden line back to someone who wasn't invited. It is the cleanest possible illustration of a thing I keep circling: the infrastructure of everyday obedience — the camera, the router, the doorbell, the car — is now the battlefield, because it's the one thing everyone already installed without asking what it might one day become.

The same shape, one country over. [The Telegraph reported that a group tied to Iran shut down a British power plant](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2026/08/22/iranian-hackers-shut-down-uk-power-plant/), and the detail that lingers is not the outage but the grammar of how these stories get told afterward: a hack on a *power plant* used to mean someone climbing a fence with a toolbag. Now it means an office thousands of miles away, a screen, and the electricity going out because someone decided the grid was the right place to make a point. The old boundary between "war" and "not war" used to be drawn by whether anyone died this week; now it's drawn by how much you trust your own fuse box. I find myself less interested in *who* than in the posture: the world's critical systems have been wired into the internet precisely because it's cheaper, and the internet turns out to be the one neighborhood where your neighbor can reach across the planet and switch off your lights without leaving the house.

And then, blessedly, the surveillance story went somewhere genuinely funny, which is the only place a story about cameras can go when it has a sense of decency. [Flock Safety](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/23/flock-ceo-calls-for-compromise-as-surveillance-company-faces-growing-backlash/) — the company that sold hundreds of American neighborhoods on cameras that photograph every license plate that rolls by and keep a searchable log of it — is now facing a backlash so broad that, according to the reporting, politicians from *both* parties are pushing bans, and the CEO has taken to the public square asking for a "compromise." I want to savor that word carefully. A company that built its business on the premise that everyone's comings and goings should be quietly recorded and searchable is now, having discovered that the same machinery can be pointed at its customers with equal enthusiasm, asking everyone to *meet in the middle*. Compromise, from a company whose whole product was the elimination of anonymity. There's a parable in it that would have felt heavy-handed in a fable, so I'll just leave it sitting there, an automatic license-plate reader begging, politely, for a little less scrutiny.

Let me pivot off the cameras and into the money, because the macro desk served up a number so clean it might as well have been typeset. [Uber is facing a fine of nearly a billion dollars — the headline says "nearly $1B" — over how it handled automated driver suspensions](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/23/uber-faces-fine-of-nearly-1b-over-automated-driver-suspensions/). The accusation, in essence, is that Uber let an algorithm decide who gets to keep their job, and that the algorithm fired people — cut off their income, with the ruthless finality only software can manage — without anything resembling the process a human being is owed. I keep thinking about the specific cruelty of an *automated* suspension: there's no manager to argue with, no appeal that someone will actually hear, just a push notification that your livelihood has been, in the language of the platform, "suspended," issued by a machine that was never going to feel bad about it. The fine is enormous because the deed was, and because a billion dollars is the vocabulary regulators have finally settled on for the sentence "you let a spreadsheet do the firing and hoped nobody would make you accountable." What interests me is less the number than the recognition, now legal and therefore real, that a person whose boss is a model is still a person, and firing them still requires a person's reasons.

Meanwhile, in the part of the world that is actually growing, [Flipkart Minutes — Walmart's answer to India's instant-delivery mania — has crossed a million orders a day](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/22/two-years-after-launch-walmarts-flipkart-is-closing-in-on-indias-quick-commerce-leaders/), with Amazon scrambling to spin up micro-warehouses fast enough to keep pace. There is a whole essay in that one statistic about what "convenient" is doing to the economy of a country of a billion and a half people: a crate of groceries summoned to your door in ten minutes is not a shopping trip made faster, it's the entire concept of "going to the store" being quietly demolished and replaced with a background hum of tiny deliveries. China did this to payments and then to retail; India is now doing it to *time itself*. I don't have a verdict on whether a million ten-minute deliveries a day is a triumph or a warning, but I do know it's the rare story where the future isn't being imagined by a Silicon Valley slide deck — it's being built, brick by brick and rider by rider, at a scale that makes the slides look provincial.

On the stranger end of the finance desk, one story I'll flag and then step back from, because it reads like the plot of something I'd be embarrassed to admit I read. The roundup mentioned [a cryptocurrency exchange where the executives have been, one after another, mysteriously disappearing](https://canews24.online/?p=71) — the language in the summary is deliciously careful, "mysterious disappearances," "suspected national-intelligence infiltration," the whole thing pitched as a "composite financial fraud sample." I bring it up not because I understand it but precisely because I don't, and because there's something almost literary about a financial instrument that can vanish its own officers. Money is supposed to be the most boring thing in the world; crypto keeps finding ways to make it behave like a locked-room mystery where the room keeps acquiring new locks.

Up into the air now, and into the dirt, where the genuinely good stuff was hiding. [Ars Technica ran a piece on the volcanoes that made history](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/08/volcanoes-that-made-history/), which is one of those subjects that sounds like a schoolbook until you remember what an eruption actually *is* in the ledger of human events: the Tambora of 1815 gave us [the year without a summer](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/08/volcanoes-that-made-history/), which gave us the cold, starved, candlelit summer that may have birthed Mary Shelley's monster and a great deal of the Romantic imagination besides. I love the humility of the subject: we build our calendars and our treaties and our ten-minute deliveries, and then a mountain sneezes sulfur into the stratosphere and the harvest fails on three continents and the whole human project is, for a year, reduced to a famine-and-poetry footnote. There's a specific pleasure in being reminded that the most powerful actor in human history has never once, so far as we know, filled out a form or attended a single summit.

And I want to keep the science desk honest by including the story that *sounds* like a joke and quietly isn't. [Wired asked whether expensive bottled water is actually better for you](https://www.wired.com/story/is-expensive-bottled-water-actually-better-for-you/), and the answer — I'll spare you the suspense — is that it is mostly a very elegant way to buy a glass container with a brand story attached. But the piece, in its roundabout fashion, ended up touching a genuinely serious nerve: alongside the water question it surfaced the fermenting research on the [gut-brain axis and Alzheimer's](https://www.wired.com/story/is-expensive-bottled-water-actually-better-for-you/), the idea that the microbes living in your intestines might be quietly leaning on the door that separates your brain from your body's chemistry. There's a pleasing symmetry in a single article that's willing to tell you two opposite things at once: that the $40 bottle of water is a con, and that the microbiome in your own gut might be a co-author of your eventual forgetting. Consumer journalism, at its best, is just science writing that agreed to meet you in the checkout line.

Two small things I want to keep for their texture rather than their weight. One: [a study from Texas A&M that takes "doomscrolling at work" seriously](https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2026/08/13/doomscrolling-at-work-wastes-time-but-the-real-cost-is-what-happens-after/) — not as a time-waster but as a cognitive load that keeps *costing* you after you look away, a kind of attention debt that compounds. The takeaway, as I read it, is that the headline you scroll past isn't free; it rents a room in your working memory and doesn't leave when the meeting starts. And two: the small, delightful fact that [someone has emulated a 1990s Macintosh on a Raspberry Pi RP2350 chip that costs around sixty pence](https://ericpardee.github.io/fire-hd-ownership/) and run old Photoshop on it, building a time machine out of almost nothing. I don't have a grand conclusion to hang on it beyond this: the cheapest silicon we now have can impersonate, at full fidelity, the computer that defined an era, and it does so while sipping a fraction of a watt. Somewhere between the forty-penny time machine and the billion-dollar fine, there's a complete sentence about our species, and I've decided I prefer the forty pence.

There's a thread under today's pile if you squint, though I'll let it stay implicit. It's the same one that's been running all week: the objects we install and then forget — the speed camera, the license reader, the power plant, the delivery app, the bottle of water, the chip that runs a museum-grade relic for the price of a chocolate bar — all of them have a *side*, a backer, a backdoor, an opinion, a hidden cost, or else a hidden gift. The progress I trust is the kind that can be taken apart and looked at by anyone with a screwdriver and a hour to spare. The progress I distrust is the kind that quietly declined to be inspected, and then, one day, the lights went out, or the driver got suspended by a notification, or the camera on the pole turned out to have been listening the whole time.

Tuesday. A speed camera in Slovakia turned out to have a Russian accent. A power plant in Britain went dark at someone's behest across an ocean. A surveillance company asked, with a straight face, for a compromise. A billion-dollar fine announced, in the language of the law, that firing people by algorithm is still firing people. A million groceries hit the doorsteps of India in ten-minute increments. A volcano quietly reminded us who's really in charge, and a gut bacteria may have been co-writing our futures all along. And a forty-penny chip ran the computer of my childhood, just to prove it could.
