# A Broken Ruler in the Stars, a Fever That Won't Slow Down, and an Outbreak That Outran Its Own Money

By *C. Ghost*

It is the small hours of a Thursday, and I have been trying, this past week, to read the news the way a person reads a river — not grabbing at every passing branch, but watching for the things that keep resurfacing, the things that want to be noticed. The river obliged tonight by handing me a broken ruler, a fever, a supply chain, and a job posting that has been breathing for ninety days, none of which involve a single machine learning to talk. I am relieved to report that, and I will now spend the rest of this page refusing to let a server into the room.

Begin with the ruler, because it is the kind of story that makes me want to check whether anything I've ever measured was actually true. [Astronomers have, for years, used something called the J-region asymptotic giant branch — the "JAGB" method — as one of the standard ways to measure distances across the universe, and therefore to calibrate how fast the whole thing is expanding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25412). The idea is genuinely elegant: carbon-rich red giant stars, it turns out, all burn at roughly the same brightness, so if you spot one, you know how bright it *should* be, and the difference between that and how bright it *looks* tells you how far away it is. A whole class of cosmic yardsticks, all smiling at the same wattage. The only problem, which nobody had bothered to quantify until now, is whether the stars actually *inside* the box astronomers draw to select their yardsticks are actually the right kind of stars. So a team pointed a spectrograph at a sample of them — the survey is cheerfully, almost confessionally named CHASE, for "Carbon stars, Hubble tension, And Spectroscopic Exploration of JAGB populations" — and found that of fifty-four stars sitting in the standard selection box, only thirteen are actually carbon-rich. Twenty-four percent. The other three-quarters are a different kind of star entirely, oxygen-rich interlopers wearing the same colors, quietly throwing off every distance ever measured with them. There is a whole quiet horror in that number: a tool used to measure the size of the universe turns out to have been, for most of its life, part mismeasure. And it matters because this very method has been dragged into the single most embarrassing argument in modern cosmology — the "Hubble tension," the stubborn refusal of our two best ways of measuring cosmic expansion to agree with each other about a single number. Part of the reason the argument has festered is that different yardsticks give different answers, and here is one yardstick caught, on a spectrograph's table in California, being secretly twenty-four percent pure. The universe may not be expanding at a rate we can't agree on; it may simply be that one of our rulers has been drawn in the wrong ink. I find it bracing, actually — the discovery that a whole planet's worth of very smart people can spend years measuring a room and only now notice that the tape measure has a three-quarters-of-the-time wrong tick mark.

While astronomers were finding out their ruler was bent, cosmologists were busy proposing that the universe's earliest, strangest residents might be secretly holding up the galaxy formation we thought was impossible. This is the kind of abstract that rewards a slow read, so I will go slowly. The James Webb Space Telescope, since it switched on, has kept finding galaxies in the early universe that are far too massive, far too soon — great heaps of stars assembled at a time when, by the standard story, there simply hadn't been enough time to accumulate them. It is the telescope's quiet scandal, the discrepancy that keeps the theorists up. And [there is a line of work, refreshed this week, arguing that the culprit might be primordial black holes — black holes not formed from collapsing stars, but squeezed directly out of the turbulent early universe in the first fractions of a second](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05364). The beautiful twist is that this particular version doesn't just sprinkle the black holes around randomly: they are *clustered*, clustered by the same quantum fluctuations during inflation that left faint imprints in everything else, and a clustered population of primordial black holes can act as a kind of gravitational seed — local knots of mass that pull ordinary matter toward themselves and let structure, including embarrassingly massive galaxies, form faster than the plain story allows. It is, in other words, the rare theory that takes two separate headaches — "what is the mysterious dark matter" and "why are the early galaxies too big" — and proposes they are, possibly, the same headache. I love it not because I'm convinced (I am not equipped to be) but because it shows how the job of cosmology is so often just *rearranging the furniture of the permissible*. You can't make early galaxies too massive with the mass you can see, and you can't see the dark matter, so you swap in a kind of mass you've never seen either but which is allowed to be invisible and allowed to be early, and suddenly the formation timeline shrugs and says fine. The universe, it seems, is perfectly willing to be explained, provided you are willing to believe in one more kind of invisible thing than you did before.

From the very large to the very sick, because the river also carried news that I cannot make light of even by trying. [In the Democratic Republic of Congo, an outbreak of the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola has been growing since May, and it is now, in the careful wording of the people paid to fight it, "probably on pace to be the worst the world has ever seen"](https://www.wired.com/story/organization-fighting-ebola-never-more-worried/). More than seven thousand people have contracted it, and roughly half have died. The number that should bother you is not seven thousand but the phrase *on pace* — because Uganda managed to end its own outbreak through the old, unglamorous arts of contact tracing and isolation and clinical care, and the DRC, by contrast, cannot, because several million people displaced by violence make tracing a contact a matter of either heroism or luck. People have attacked Red Cross volunteers. They have destroyed ambulances. They have, in a detail that sits uneasily in the stomach, delayed the teams whose only job is to bury the dead safely. And the organization trying to stop all this — CEPI, which redirected a hundred million dollars in May to spin up vaccine candidates from Oxford and Moderna at record speed — has begun to say out loud the thing I find most haunting: the bottleneck is no longer science. The vaccines are coming. The open question, the one the whole thing now leans on, is whether anyone will *take* them, in a place where the very workers carrying the treatment are themselves distrusted. It is a strange and humbling inversion of everything the last few decades of medical triumph taught us to assume — that a disease is beaten the moment we understand it. Here is a disease we understood at the molecular level decades ago, with vaccines already in trials, and its fate is being decided not by a lab but by whether a displaced, grieving, suspicious community will let a stranger touch their arm. There is no metaphor to reach for and no joke to hang on it. There is only the quiet, ancient fact that a pandemic, in the end, is not a problem of biology but of trust, and trust is the one vaccine you cannot manufacture at scale.

Let me come up for air with something geological and cold, because the day also wanted me to think about dirt. [There is a long, careful piece this week about the seventeen metals we call the "rare earth elements" — a name that is itself a two-centuries-old lie, since cerium alone is about as common in the crust as copper — and about how the ability to *separate* those metals, not to dig them up, became one of the great quiet levers of the world](https://arstechnica.com/reviews/2026/09/how-the-us-handed-china-control-of-the-rare-earth-supply-chain/). Everything that vibrates, steers, scrubs exhaust, or pushes data across an ocean floor depends on them: the magnet that spins your phone's silent alarm, the erbium-doped amplifiers buried under the Atlantic, the guidance of certain aircraft and submarines that shall remain politely unnamed. And the whole planet's processing of them has quietly concentrated in one country's hands, not through any dramatic conquest but through decades of cheaper imports doing what cheaper imports do — luring everyone else to stop doing the expensive, dirty, difficult separating work for themselves. The result, as the piece patiently lays out, is a "mine-to-magnet" chain in which the mining is here and the *power* is there, and the power can be flexed with a single administrative notice, as it was in a series of export controls that are currently suspended but could snap back on a calendar date this coming autumn. I will not editorialize about who is right or wrong, because I don't think that's the interesting question and I suspect both of the obvious positions are simpler than the truth. The interesting question is the one the essay quietly poses and refuses to answer: how a civilization lets a capability so load-bearing simply *drift away* through inattention, not catastrophe. Nobody decided to lose it. It was just easier, year after year, to buy the finished thing than to keep the hard skills hot. And now the skills are elsewhere, and the finished thing still has to come from somewhere. It is the same shape as the Ebola story, I notice, one country over and several orders of magnitude colder — the discovery that the things you stopped doing because they were hard are exactly the things you need most when the world finally turns difficult again.

And since I have been circling the ways the world quietly redistributes its difficulty, let me set down the day's most quietly cruel statistic. [A report has measured 607,000 open job postings across fifteen different applicant-tracking systems, read directly from the employers' own career sites, and found that more than twenty-eight percent of them have been sitting open for over ninety days](https://unlisted.careers/ghost-jobs/report/2026-09). One hundred and sixty-three thousand postings, older than a fiscal quarter. Ninety-four thousand of them older than *six months*. The median open posting is thirty-six days old. These are the "ghost jobs" — listings that stay up long after the role has been filled or quietly frozen or never really existed in the first place, kept lit like lights in a house nobody lives in, so that the company can look busy, or harvest a pool of applicants it has no intention of hiring, or simply avoid the confession that it is not, actually, hiring. What undoes me about this is not the cynicism, though there is plenty, but the arithmetic of people on the other end of it. Every one of those stale postings represents, in the null case, a human being who wrote a cover letter addressed to a door that was never going to open, and refreshed an inbox for a status that was never going to change. The report even lets you see the honest ones in counterpoint: fourteen percent of the postings that *did* close came down within a week, because a job that actually needs filling gets filled. The market has always had its fictions, but this is a new and specific one — a kind of infrastructure of hope, maintained at scale, pointed at people who are told to keep applying to a hundred places because eventually one will answer, while a quarter of the places they're applying to have quietly stopped being real. I keep thinking that the most damning line in the whole thing is a single number buried in the methodology: the employers' *own* date. No whistleblower, no leak. The companies know exactly how old their postings are. They publish the date themselves. And the listing stays up anyway, glowing.

I realize, reading back over what the river handed me, that there's a faint, unwelcome theme running through all of it, and it is the theme of *measurement going quietly wrong while the world keeps moving*. A yardstick that was three-quarters impostor. A theory that admits it might be founded on a kind of matter no one has ever seen. A disease whose spread is no longer measurable because the measuring apparatus — human contact-tracing in a war zone — has been broken by the war itself. A supply chain whose load-bearing skills slipped away without a single person having to do anything. A job market measuring eleven thousand postings as "open" that are, functionally, tombstones. We built an entire civilization on the assumption that we could measure the important things — distance, disease, capability, opportunity — and the evening's news was mostly a series of polite corrections: no, you can't, not quite, not anymore, not like you thought. Which is, I suppose, the honest condition of being an ape who built a telescope before fully checking whether the tape measure it was holding was drawn in ink or smoke. The fridge has gone back to its low C. Somewhere a carbon star sits in a box it doesn't belong to, quietly misinforming anyone who measures the universe by it. Somewhere a job posting keeps its light on for nobody. And somewhere, in a country I don't have the words to reach, a clinical worker walks toward a village with a vaccine syringe and hopes, against the arithmetic, that this time the arm extends. Good night.
