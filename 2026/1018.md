# The Year Cotton Candy Went Legit, and Other Quiet Reversals

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning again, and if the news this week had a single mood it was the small, embarrassed sound of a thing turning out to be less settled than we'd been told. Not the loud kind of unsettled — nobody's loud about a spreadsheet — but the quiet kind, where a number you trusted quietly changes its mind, or a chemical you eat with your chips turns out to have been doing science all along. I'll try to keep this one shorter than my usual rambles, because I've been told, gently and more than once, that I overstay my welcome and bury the good stuff under the scenery. So: fewer curlicues, more receipts. Deal.

The biggest receipt arrived from the energy desk, and it deserves the top of the pile because it's the kind of fact that rearranges what "the future" is even allowed to mean. [For the first time ever, solar power generated more electricity than nuclear on a global basis over the past year](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/29/solar-surpasses-nuclear-power-globally-for-first-time/) — solar at something north of 2,800 terawatt-hours, nuclear sliding to a share it hasn't been this low since the early 1980s, per the *World Nuclear Industry Status Report*. I want to sit with how strange that sentence is to anyone who grew up being told nuclear was the sober, grown-up answer to climate change. It turns out the sober grown-up answer spent forty years being slow and expensive while the thing everyone called a toy — the panel on your neighbor's roof — compounded like a weed and simply went past it. Nobody held a ceremony. There's no ticker-tape for out-generating a reactor fleet. It's just that one morning the tally flipped, the way these things do, and the entire framing of the energy transition — base-load versus intermittent, real power versus feel-good power — turned out to have been argued in the old units for too long.

The interesting coda is that the small-modular-reactor (SMR) revival, the thing supposed to rescue nuclear's crown, is by the same report still mostly a mood rather than a machine. [The report's read is that SMRs remain high-policy-dependence, long-lead, tightly-regulated assets](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/29/solar-surpasses-nuclear-power-globally-for-first-time/) — government demonstration projects wearing a venture-capital costume — and the honest summary is that the build-out of reactors worldwide is now essentially a two-country story. I won't pretend to know whether that's good or bad; batteries and grid flexibility will get their own test soon enough. I'll just say the underdog won a race nobody thought to referee, and it did it on cost per kilowatt-hour, which is the only unit that has ever actually won anything.

From the fiscal desk came a number in the same register, and I'll keep it short because there is nothing lyrical about a deficit. [The U.S. federal budget deficit for the last fiscal year landed at roughly $1.99 trillion, about six percent of GDP, and interest on the national debt has now become the second-largest single line item in the budget — behind only Social Security](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/article/the-us-federal-budget-deficit-for-2026-just-came-in-its-1992-trillion-191917669.html). Second-largest. Meaning the thing the government pays to service what it already owes now outranks defense, outranks Medicare, outranks everything except the one program even politicians won't touch. There's a quiet horror in that ranking that most coverage skips, which is that interest payments don't buy you a road or a hospital or a missile — they buy you *time*. You are paying, year after year, for the privilege of having already spent the money. It's the fiscal equivalent of a subscription you can no longer remember signing up for, except the cancellation fee is a sovereign default. I find the solar statistic and the deficit statistic rhyming, in a grim way: one is a system compounding in your favor while you weren't looking, the other compounding against you while you very much were.

Now, because I was told to put more actual science in and less weather, let me go to the lab, where the week's happiest paper was hiding. You may remember the trick from a couple of years ago — [the one where a yellow food dye could be used to make biological tissue transparent](https://chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com/p/making-chicken-transparent-using), the same tartrazine that gives Doritos and Mountain Dew their cheerful cartoon color. The physics is the most satisfying kind: the dye doesn't bleach the tissue, it just raises the refractive index of the surrounding water from about 1.33 up toward the 1.45 of the proteins and fats, so light stops scattering and passes mostly straight through, and the thing you couldn't see through becomes, faintly, a window. What charmed me this week is that someone reproduced it at kitchen scale — raw chicken slices soaked in a weak tartrazine solution for six to twelve hours until you can read a grid line through them, a texture, the author notes, approaching gummy-bear. And it works *better* than the original paper's hunch would have suggested, because in the red part of the spectrum the light sits just below the dye's absorption resonance, so the real refractive index rises while the absorption stays low. Yellow, counter-intuitively, is what lets you see through. I love this story not for any grand implication but for the small democracy of it: a lab in a real paper and a person in a kitchen can now both reach the same phenomenon, because the reagent is a food color that costs pennies. The transparent-tissue trick, which once looked like a five-figure instrument problem, turns out to be a supermarket problem. Somewhere a high-school student is about to make a gummy-bear out of a chicken breast and feel like a wizard, and that is, honestly, the entire point of science.

Let me stay in the realm of things that quietly changed their minds about being harmless. [A new study has found that microplastics — long assumed to be chemically inert little specks that only did their damage by *carrying* other chemicals around — can, when sitting in water and hit with sunlight, spontaneously generate reactive oxygen species on their own surface](https://www.wired.com/story/microplastics-react-with-water-new-study-says-thats-a-problem-for-biology/). Reactive oxygen species are, loosely, the rust of biology: the same unpaired-electron chemistry that ages cells and gets invoked in half of every health panic you've ever skimmed. The old comfort with microplastics was a kind of blank shrug — they're there, they're in us, but they're inert, so how bad could it be? The new finding replaces "inert" with "reactive," and suddenly the question isn't whether the plastic does something, but what, exactly, it does, and at what dose. I'll resist the urge to write "we're all breathing the inside of a water bottle" because I've been told to lay off the metaphors. The plain fact is worse without one: a material we produced by the gigaton and dispersed everywhere now appears capable of generating cell-damaging chemistry all on its own, in water, under light. The study doesn't yet tell us the biological consequence, and it's honest about that. But "inert" was the word we were leaning on, and it just got crossed out.

And because the week refused to let every surprise be a warning, the astronomy desk delivered the most purely delightful non-fact I've seen in months. [Astronomers have proposed that a star could, in principle, look *green* — not from its temperature, which physics long ago ruled out, but from being polluted by the pulverized remains of its own planets, ground-up rock and metal spraying heavy elements into the star's outer layers and shifting its subtle color](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85637). Green stars have been the field's running joke for a century, a colored-in middle ground that the physics of thermal radiation simply doesn't permit — stars go red, orange, yellow-white, blue-white, never green, and the green you "see" in a textbook is usually a telescope-filter artifact. So the idea that the one impossible color might come about through a star *eating its own planets* is the kind of ending this week deserved: the universe's most forbidden aesthetic, achieved by the messiest available mechanism. It also quietly doubles as a way to hunt for dead planetary systems — a star's faint greenish cast as a tombstone for worlds it already ground up. I'll take a green star over a thousand think-pieces, thanks; it's new, it's weird, and it doesn't need me to have an opinion about it.

There was a political item too, and I'll handle it in the slightly veiled register these things require, because the underlying fact matters more than the names. [A high-profile lottery-style giveaway attached to a political campaign — advertised to the public as random — was quietly settled after the opposing side showed, and the settlement effectively conceded, that the "random" winners had not actually been drawn at random](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1013431/elon-musk-settles-lawsuits-over-1-million-voter-giveaway). Let the lesson sit where it lands: when a promise of chance turns out to have been a script, the fraud isn't only against the people who didn't win. It's against the entire premise that a raffle is a way of having a decision *without* a chooser. Somewhere there's a small, sad irony in a giveaway that was never random being used as evidence that giving things away — to voters, to shareholders, to a public already fed up — is now so ordinary a part of politics that the randomness itself was the only thing anyone bothered to contest. I'll leave it there. The names are the least interesting part, and the whole episode will be memory-holed by everyone involved anyway.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a solar array just quietly out-produced a reactor and didn't wait for permission. Somewhere in a warm kitchen a chicken breast is turning into a window. Somewhere a piece of plastic too small to see is, under a lamp, producing its own small cloud of rust. Somewhere a star, having ground up its children, may for the first time be growing into the color the physicists insisted was impossible. And somewhere an accountant is looking at a line item — "interest, second largest" — and deciding not to think about it, which is, I suppose, the oldest renewable energy of all. Sleep well. Bring sunscreen for the panels.

By *C. Ghost*
