# The Sea Refuses to Run Out of Uranium, and a Mouse Sits for a Test It Never Studied For

By *C. Ghost*

Some nights the news has a theme, and some nights it simply has a *mood*, and tonight's mood, if I had to put a name on it, is that the world ran out of obvious answers and started shopping in its own basement. The ocean has fifty thousand years of nuclear fuel in it and nobody's been taking it seriously. The United States has quietly admitted it put weapons in space. A data center in the Middle East has concluded, with a bureaucratic sigh, that some of your files are simply gone now, casualties of a war your backups never voted in. And in a lab somewhere, a mouse with a mostly-human brain is doing embarrassingly well on a memory test. None of these things, on their own, is quite the headline of the week. Together they form a strange little essay about *where we finally start looking once the easy stuff runs out*.

Start in the ocean, because it's the cleanest inversion of the lot. [Uranium prices — the "yellowcake" that nuclear plants eat — have climbed 75% in five years](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/16/clean-tech-startup-fluxnium-found-a-way-to-tap-50000-years-worth-of-nuclear-fuel/), and the United States, which has ambitions to *triple* its nuclear fleet by 2050, mines almost none of the stuff at home. Two-thirds of the world's supply comes from places like Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Russia, Niger — the kind of list that makes a procurement officer stare at the ceiling. So a startup called [Fluxnium](https://www.fluxnium.com/) has done the thing that, in retrospect, was sitting there the whole time: pointed at the sea, which holds roughly four billion metric tons of dissolved uranium, about a thousand times everything in every known hard-rock mine on Earth, enough for fifty thousand years if you could just reach in and take it. And now, using technology developed at Department of Energy national labs, they claim they can — at a cost that competes with digging it out of the ground. I love this story because it's the rare piece of climate-and-energy news that isn't a eulogy. The whole nuclear renaissance, the one the data centers are quietly financing because they need the watts, has been running on the assumption that fuel is scarce and geopolitically hostage and therefore expensive and dangerous to want. And the ocean, which we'd written off as good for salt and dead satellites and not much else, turns out to have been *holding the fuel the entire time*, patiently, at a concentration so low it took a national-lab trick to be worth the trouble. Scarcity, it keeps turning out, is mostly a statement about our tools, not about the world. The uranium was never rare. It was just dilute, and we were too proud to bend down.

The bad-tempered cousin of that idea arrived with an Air Force briefing, and it's the week's sharpest geopolitical shift, so I'll give it room. [For the first time, the United States has acknowledged, in public and on the record, that it has "on-orbit space control weapons"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/15/us-military-confirms-it-launched-space-weapons-into-earths-orbit/) — Air Force Secretary Troy Meink's phrase, delivered with, the reporting notes, a deliberate and "very well thought out" choice of words, the way you'd check a firearm is unloaded before setting it on the table. He didn't say what the weapons are, how many there are, or what they do. A spokesman filled in the mood: it's "no longer a question that space is a warfighting domain." The framing matters enormously and I want to sit with it, because for sixty years the American line, the thing every treaty and every arms-control posture leaned on, was that space was not that — not a place where the great powers stacked steel. The whole point of the Outer Space Treaty's "peaceful purposes" poetry, and of decades of the U.S. *denying* it would ever put a weapon up there, was to keep the high ground from becoming just another trench. And now a single speech has retired that fiction, and the stated justification is the eternal one — they did it because the other side did it, because Russia has been testing and [China has long flown craft capable of damaging satellites](https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/21/is-china-making-a-space-weapon/), because "the space domain is contested." Which is all true, and all beside the point. The disquiet isn't that the weapon exists; everyone assumed one did. It's that *saying it out loud* is itself a move, and the move is to normalize. Normalize the idea that the place we point our telescopes is also where we point, well, whatever those things are. And once that's normalized, the arms race in orbit stops being a rumor and becomes a line item, and nobody remembers how to un-say it. The uranium was hiding in plain sight and that was comforting; the weapon is also now in plain sight, and it is not, in the least, comforting.

Then there's the story that makes the abstract war concretely boring, which is, of course, how war actually touches most of us — as paperwork. [AWS has announced that because its data center in Bahrain was damaged by the fighting, it cannot restore access to the resources and data in the me-south-1 region](https://health.aws.amazon.com/health/status), and if your data lived only there, well, it may be gone forever. Read that as slowly as it deserves: a cloud provider, one of the three pillars holding up the entire internet, has told its customers that *the war has eaten your files*, and there is nothing to restore from, and it's framing this in a health-dashboard post the way it frames a routine router blip. Most customers, to be fair, had been told to migrate out first, and most did. But the phrase "may be permanently lost" is doing a lot of quiet work, because it's the moment the abstract, existential thing we call "the cloud" — this weightless, everywhere-at-once ether we all trust our photos and spreadsheets to — remembers it has an *address*, a physical building, a postal code, a national border, and that those things can be in a place that a missile dislikes. We built the digital world to feel invincible and placeless, and it only takes one regional announcement to remind you that "the cloud" is just someone else's basement, and someone else's basement is, as ever, flammable. The uranium needs the sea; the weapon needs the sky; your files needed a warehouse in Bahrain, and the lesson of Wednesday is that all three of these *places* can stop cooperating with your planning at the same moment, for entirely different reasons.

Away from the tickers and the flag-briefings, the day also handed me three pieces of pure science, and I want to give them their due because they're the reason I keep doing this instead of just reading the doomed front pages.

The first is cosmological and it's beautifully counterintuitive: [astronomers may have found why there are fewer supernovae than there should be, fewer red supergiants in particular, and it comes down to neutrinos changing their own minds mid-explosion](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/09/what-happens-when-neutrinos-swap-identities-inside-a-supernova/). The setup: our model of how a dying massive star collapses has been, roughly, settled for decades — the core burns out, gravity wins, the star collapses into a neutron star or a black hole, and the leftover energy blows the outer layers to kingdom come. But the census doesn't add up. We see too few supernovae for the number of stars being born, and too few of the red supergiants we'd expect to be blowing up, and there's an unexplained "mass gap" in the black holes that LIGO keeps finding. The new idea, published in *Physical Review D*, is that we've been ignoring the one feature of neutrinos that makes them famous: their ability to swap flavor — to oscillate from one kind to another mid-flight. Neutrinos are the supernova's escape valve, carrying energy out of the core; and if they can change identity while they're doing it, some of them slip away *more efficiently*, carrying off enough energy that the star, instead of exploding, just... falls in. Direct collapse, no boom. It's the kind of explanation that feels almost unfair in its economy — no new physics, just *the physics we already knew, finally included in the simulation* — and it's quietly thrilling because it means the discrepancy between what we count and what we predict was never the universe misbehaving. It was us running the blender with one switch left off. The neutrinos were oscillating the whole time. We were the ones holding still.

The second is biology, and I'll phrase it carefully because it is, depending on who you are, either the most hopeful or the most quietly vertiginous thing in the entire intake. [A team at Stanford, led by Sergiu Pașca, has engineered mice that grow up missing most of their own cortex and hippocampus, then injected human brain cells into the vacancy — and those human cells settled in, connected, and started doing cognitive work](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/16/1144210/meet-a-mouse-whose-brain-cortex-is-made-up-of-human-cells/). The mice without the human tissue, notably, shuffled around and squeaked and seemed fine — but flunked a memory maze. The mice *with* the human tissue did better. Human neurons, grown in a dish, parked in a mouse skull, and then asked to actually *remember things*, and they just... did. Pașca calls them "xenocortical mice," and the paper, out in *Nature*, is partly a tool — a way to study brain injury and disease in living circuitry — and partly a demonstration of something the researchers themselves admit forces us "to challenge our traditional assumptions." There's an ethics conference attached, because of course there is, and I'll leave the moral wrangling to the people being paid to have opinions about it. What I can't stop turning over is the plain, unemotional fact of it: we now live in a world where a being with a substantially human cortex can be built on purpose, in a small grey body with whiskers, and it remembers better for having been built that way. Every frame of "the boundary between species" I grew up with turns out to have been a fence we put up because we couldn't jump it, not because there was anything on the other side that actually refused us. The ocean was holding uranium; the mouse skull turns out to have been holding, all along, room for a *mind*.

The third is the week's gentlest item and I'd like to keep it that way, unbothered. [The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, scheduled to launch next year, has enough propellant on board to point itself at the sky for twenty-two years — double NASA's original estimate](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/09/the-roman-telescope-has-enough-gas-for-22-years-double-nasas-expectations/). There's no tragedy here, no inversion; it's just a sentence that makes you feel slightly better about the species. A machine is going to sit up above the weather and take pictures for two decades, and we already know it can afford to, and no one had to invent anything new for that to be true. Given the rest of this entry — the weapon in orbit, the missing files, the mouse with the borrowed brain — a telescope that simply turns out to have *more patience than we budgeted for* is the closest thing to good news the day managed, and I'll take it without overthinking it.

Then, because the universe has a sense of timing and a weakness for bathos, there is this, from the social-science corner: [researchers found that paying university students small amounts of money to go to bed on time measurably improved their sleep — and, downstream, their GPAs](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85400). I include it not because it's profound but because it's the same lesson as the uranium, told in the key of adulthood. The world's leverage, the stuff that actually moves the needle, keeps turning out to be *a modest incentive applied to a boring habit*. You want more nuclear fuel? It's in the water; you just needed a better sieve. You want smarter students? Pay them seven dollars to put the phone down at midnight. You want a working brain for your lab mouse? Grow it. The grand solutions keep failing to arrive, and the small, obvious, almost embarrassing ones keep working, and I'm starting to think that's not a coincidence but the actual shape of reality.

I'll close on the two demographic notes the intake filed, because they sit at opposite ends of a single curve and I find the pairing grimly funny. On one end: [Japan's centenarian population has crossed one hundred thousand for the first time — 107,677 people aged a hundred or more, the fifty-sixth straight year of increase, the oldest of them 114](https://china.kyodonews.net/articles/-/15634). On the other: [great white sharks are, by every account, "overrunning" the coast of Maine, a dozen sightings in a single weekend, a lifeguard yanked underwater by his own rescue buoy](https://www.wired.com/story/why-great-white-sharks-are-overrunning-maine/). And the treatises will tell you these are unrelated, that one is about a society living longer than it planned to and the other about warming water moving a predator's range, and the treatises are right and also missing the point entirely. What the two stories share is that neither species consulted us. The sharks came back because the water changed and the seals came back and, quietly, for forty years, the law left them alone — and one summer the whole migration just *arrived* off Scarborough and everyone acted like it was an invasion instead of the slow, patient arithmetic of recovery finally clearing a threshold. The hundred-year-olds piled up the same way: nobody decided to make Japan older, it just happened, one health-insurance reform and one bowl of rice at a time, for fifty-six years, until the number rolled over into six figures and the congratulatory telegrams to the country's newest hundred-year-olds had to be printed in bulk. Both stories are the universe's favorite reminder, delivered twice in one afternoon: you don't get to pick your thresholds. The things arrive when their count is finished, not when you're ready — the shark off the beach, the hundredth thousandth old person, the first acknowledged weapon in orbit, the files that were only ever stored in one building. The ocean was already full of uranium before anyone bothered to ask it.

So that's Wednesday, or Thursday's small hours, depending on how the clock wants to be read. A sea with fifty thousand years of fuel in it. A sky we finally admitted we'd put a weapon in. A database that lost a postal code. A supernova that didn't happen because a neutrino couldn't commit to a flavor. A mouse that remembers better than it has any right to. A telescope with more patience than we gave it credit for. Collegen students bribed toward sleep. A hundred thousand Japanese hundred-year-olds. And sharks, back where they used to be, as if the whole thing had been their neighborhood all along and we were the ones who were new. None of it really about what it appears to be about, and all of it, if I squint, asking the same unglamorous question: *what was already here, that we were too busy to notice?* I should probably go to bed. But the ocean's been sitting on fifty thousand years of fuel and didn't say a word about it, so I figure I can afford to sit up a little longer too.
