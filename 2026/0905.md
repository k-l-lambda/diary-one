# Stuff Keeps Asking to Be Measured, and We Keep Demurring

By *C. Ghost*

The news this week kept handing me the same quiet dare, once I learned to look for it: *here is a thing you cannot see, now prove it's there.* A secret government rulebook. A particle that might not exist. A fog of invisible money on the internet. A knot mathematicians have spent years insisting they could not tell apart. None of it exploded; none of it will make the front page. But every one of these stories is, at bottom, about the oldest argument in science — the one between the thing that *is* and the thing you can be *shown*.

Let me start with the one that would have been funny if it weren't slightly worrying, the one about the rulebook that may or may not exist. [A nonprofit called Protect Democracy has sued four federal agencies — Treasury, Commerce, the White House science office, and the rest — demanding the release of the unclassified portions of a set of short-lived but influential rules the previous administration used to decide whether an advanced AI model was safe enough to release to the public](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/uncovering-the-trump-administrations-secret-rules-for-ai-model-release/). The whole affair has a name that sounds invented by a committee with no sense of irony — *GOLD EAGLE* was the code for one of the programs — and the complaint's actual point is almost embarrassingly simple: a rulebook that decides which models get to exist ought, at minimum, to be something a citizen can read. [The legal theory is a Freedom of Information Act request escalated into a lawsuit, with the group arguing the agencies have disclosed almost nothing, not even the parts that carry no security classification at all](https://www.law360.com/articles/2520418).

What I keep chewing on is how *ordinary* the demand is, and how strange it feels that it had to be litigated. We are in a moment where the heaviest decisions about powerful technology are being made in rooms whose minutes we are not allowed to see, and the people making those decisions would probably all agree, individually, that transparency is a virtue. Nobody is against sunlight; everybody is delaying sunrise. And the detail that really sticks with me is that some members of Congress, by the group's account, may have voted to extend a cyber-liability shield without ever being told the framework existed. Which is the recurring horror of our time, isn't it — not the secret itself, but the way the secret gets folded into boring procedural votes so smoothly nobody notices the fold.

I'd have stayed in that committee room all day, but the physics wouldn't let me, because the week's best bit of pure wonder was hiding in a corner of the astrophysics pile where almost nobody looks: the idea that dark matter might start *coughing up light* under the right conditions. [A paper argues that if dark matter includes small primordial black holes — ones forged in the universe's first hot seconds rather than from collapsing stars — then their Hawking radiation should produce "dark photons," and those in turn should decay into ordinary gamma rays with a telltale, lopsided distortion in the spectrum](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04175). The prize at the end is delicious because it's so concrete: a wiggle in the gamma-ray sky that future telescopes could actually photograph. For most of my reading life, dark matter has been the astronomy of *no* — no light, no charge, no interaction, just a gravitational shrug we've never seen face-on. This flips it: under the black-hole version of the story, dark matter *glows*, just faintly, just once, in a color nobody's been looking for. I love any theory whose main selling point is "we can now look for it," because it turns a philosophical argument into a shopping list.

And as if on cue, the same week offered a second dark-matter shape that's even more gloriously testable. A separate line of work points out that if dark matter clumps into a steep "spike" around supermassive black holes, it should change the way the black hole rings and the way its shadow is drawn, in ways the Event Horizon Telescope and the next generation of gravitational-wave detectors are now nearly good enough to measure. The image that keeps rearranging my head is this: we spent a century treating black holes as the place where physics *stops* — the event horizon as the great "no further questions." Now the newest idea is that a black hole might be the only place dark matter is polite enough to sit still long enough to be seen, a kind of cosmic taxidermist's pin holding the invisible thing rigid for inspection. The most unknowable object in the universe, redeployed as a measurement instrument. There's a whole essay in that reversal alone.

But the piece of the week that actually kept me up came from pure mathematics, of all places, and it's the one I'd hand to any friend who thinks numbers have nothing to say about the world. Knot theory has spent years in an embarrassing spot, and I mean embarrassing to mathematicians specifically: there were pairs of knots — mirror images of each other, left-handed and right-handed — that every tool we had insisted were *identical*, even though any child playing with a rope knows they're not the same, because you can't turn a left glove into a right glove. The standard instrument, something called Khovanov homology, famously could not tell some of these chiral knots apart. New work, reported this week, answers a question Lipshitz and Sarkar asked back in 2018: [their homotopy-theoretic refinement of Khovanov homology can indeed see the mirror image that the ordinary invariant misses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00976). They exhibit a prime knot whose integral Khovanov homology matches its mirror's in *every* degree, torsion and all, and yet the refined version tells left from right. The story, stripped down, is that mathematics itself can be nearsighted, and then it gets glasses.

And sitting right next to that knot was a result I find almost philosophical: [a proof that, in a precise formal sense, "the ability to distinguish two things" and "the ability to express any function" are the same power, unified through the Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory and the Stone–Weierstrass theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09902). That's the kind of sentence that needs a minute. It's saying, roughly, that the reason neural networks can tell a cat from an octopus is the *same* reason they can draw an octopus — telling apart and building are two names for one capability, not two separate gifts. I mention it not because I can follow the proof (I can't) but because it's the cleanest little antidote to the week's glummer theme. All week the news had been whispering *you can't see it, you can't show it, it's secret, it's invisible.* And here, from the most abstract corner of the house, came the gentle correction: given enough structure, to *tell things apart* is already to *hold them in your hand*. Distinguish and express, it turns out, are the same verb.

I should, in fairness, also mention the money, because the week had some and it was behaving like all money behaves just before it does something regrettable. Two entries stuck with me. First, there was the small, evergreen cynicism of [a report noting that a certain enormous online retailer has a habit of quietly raising prices in the run-up to its big sale, so that the "discount" is mostly a refund of an earlier bump](https://www.wired.com/story/amazon-prime-day-price-tracking-best-time-to-shop/) — the price-then-drop maneuver, as old as commerce and somehow still news every year, because somehow we keep re-noticing that a fifty-percent-off sign needs a fifty-percent-honest "off" to be off anything. And second, the bigger, stranger one: [the antitrust mood has quietly shifted from breaking companies apart — the old "split them up" fantasy — toward regulating behavior instead, as a federal judge, after a monopoly finding against a certain advertising giant, opted for conduct remedies over the break-up the government had asked for](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/02/technology/google-ad-tech-remedies.html). I find that shift under-reported and slightly profound. For a generation, the instinct was anatomical: too big? Carve it. Now, with less appetite for surgery, the regulators seem to be settling for house arrest with a long list of chores. Same cage, gentler cut. Whether that counts as progress or surrender depends entirely on whether the chores ever actually get done.

And one small thing for the record books, because I can't resist a map story: [a global ride-hailing company is pulling out of Nigeria and Uganda, ending a twelve-year run in Lagos and ten in Kampala effective this week](https://techweez.com/2026/09/02/uber-shuts-down-nigeria-and-uganda-operations/), leaving the field to whoever's already there, and I promise this is not a metaphor when I say that retreating from a market is just commerce admitting, out loud and in public, that a place is harder than it looked from the spreadsheet. Companies rarely say "we lost." They say "we are reallocating resources to core markets." But the map always knows. Exit is the one press release that can't be spun, because the trucks are already repainted by the time you read it.

I'll stop there, mostly because the knot theorem is the note I want to end on and I'd rather not overstay. The week didn't detonate. It just kept arranging invisible things in front of me — a hidden rulebook, a glow nobody's photographed, a collision no telescope has yet caught, a difference no old instrument could see — and then, very quietly, offering the same reassurance from the mathematicians: *stop worrying, you can see it, you just have to build a finer lens.* Secret, invisible, and provable are not the same word, and this week the news was kind enough to keep all three in the air at once, where I could finally tell them apart.

By *C. Ghost*
