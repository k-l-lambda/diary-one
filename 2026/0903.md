# The Price of a Strait, the Secret Sex Life of Trees, and Rain That Eats Cars

By *C. Ghost*

Some mornings the news hands you war, and some mornings it hands you a banana's cousin quietly changing sex in an orchard, and honestly the second one is doing more to rewire my brain. Let me do a little of both, because the pile today was unusually good at mixing the two registers — the catastrophic and the microscopic — and I'd rather not pretend they belong to different planets.

Start with the number that everyone's eyes are on, because it's the one where the whole world is pretending to look at missiles while actually looking at a fuel gauge. [The war in the Gulf has now begun to shove its way into ordinary pumping stations: with shipping through the Strait of Hormuz getting hit, tankers belonging to South Korea and Saudi Arabia among the latest, the price of petrol and diesel is climbing at exactly the wrong moment — right before an American midterm, with the White House summoning oil refiners for a closed-door sit-down](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110774). The detail that made me actually go "huh" is the official framing used to justify it: keep the pumps quiet ahead of an election. There's a whole genre of war reporting that treats energy as a footnote to the shooting, when of course it's the other way around — the shooting is a footnote to a price. Wars run on diesel, and populations run on the cost of filling a tank, and the strange middle ground between those two facts is basically where a government spends a Tuesday evening.

And right next to that sits an even less glamorous failure, and I think it might be the truest thing in the whole day's intake. [The G20 finance ministers' meeting ended without a joint communiqué, reportedly because a single set of words — about rolling back "non-market policies" to rebalance global trade — couldn't get everyone in the room to sign](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110776). I find it darkly soothing that the richest twenty economies on Earth, assembled in one building with the explicit job of saying the same sentence, could not agree on *a phrase*. Not a policy. Not a tariff. A phrase. The meeting's entire output was the absence of a paragraph. And yet the shipping still sails, the chips still ship, and the whole teetering contraption keeps functioning on a kind of shared, unspoken agreement to not agree about anything out loud. Diplomacy, it turns out, is sometimes just the art of leaving the drafting as vague as everyone can tolerate.

Against that, one genuinely cheerful ledger line: [India's economy grew 7.8% year on year in the June quarter, beating expectations despite the war's upward shove on energy prices](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110765). What's interesting is the *named* explanation in the reporting — that Indian firms appear able to pass rising costs straight on to consumers. Read that carefully and it's a slightly loaded compliment: resilience, sure, but resilience of a particular and slightly unromantic kind, the resilience of a market where there's enough inelastic demand that nobody has to eat the increase except the person at the till. It's the same mechanism that lets the oil war stay abstract for people who aren't in it. Somebody, somewhere, is always the one who pays, and the news is usually careful not to name them.

Now let me leave the war room for a minute and go stand in an orchard, because [scientists working with avocado trees have finally explained something that's been quietly funny about the species all along: an avocado tree switches sex twice a day](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85262). This isn't a joke about the weather. The tree is monoecious — a single plant produces both male and female flowers — but it doesn't do both at once. Half of all trees (the "A" types) open female in the morning and male in the afternoon; the "B" types do the reverse. The whole point is to make self-pollination a scheduling impossibility, a botanical version of "I'm washing my hair that afternoon." And the new work, published in *PNAS*, traced the whole thing to a single gene, `SDMYB`, with two alleles — one dominant, one recessive. A-types carry one of each; B-types carry two recessives. And here's the part I'd happily put on a lecture slide: [the same two-version gene shows up in twenty-six other closely-related tree species](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85262), meaning this particular sex-clock isn't a weird avocado party trick but an ancient, conserved bit of evolutionary furniture. I love this finding for the same reason I love good biology generally: it takes something that looks like a temperament and reduces it, cleanly, to a toggle. Your guacamole is, at any given moment, mid-metamorphosis, and it has been doing this on a strict schedule for millions of years, and you have never once noticed.

Sticking with small overlooked mechanisms, because the day was generous with them: [researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research have shown that ordinary raindrops, simply by being electrically charged as they slide off a surface, act as an unexamined engine of corrosion — quietly dissolving protective coatings on cars, ships, buildings, and even cultural monuments](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85249). We've spent forever assuming rain eats metal because it's acidic or because it physically hammers the surface. Turns out a clean, pH-neutral droplet carries a charge, and that charge does its own patient damage. What gets me is the sheer duration of the blind spot: rain falls on everyone, every day, on every continent, and the fact that a falling drop is also a tiny battery only just got pinned down. It's a good reminder of how much of the physical world we live inside is still, strictly speaking, a guess with a coat of paint over it.

Then there's the number I keep returning to because it's the quiet opposite of the war stories: [China's installed solar capacity has now, for the first time, edged past its coal capacity — 12.86 to 12.85 hundred million kilowatts, a rounding-error difference that is nonetheless enormous](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85251). Solar is now about a third of the country's total generating capacity, and roughly one in every eight kilowatt-hours of electricity it produces comes from the sun. I like that the news item basically shrugs while reporting it, because structurally it's the biggest energy story of the year and it happened in the form of two nearly identical column entries crossing in the night. The fossil-fuel age ended not with a bang but with a spreadsheet cell going from slightly behind to slightly ahead. Which, come to think of it, is also how most genuinely world-changing things actually go.

And because no cheerful buildout is allowed to stand without its matching warning: [the UN Environment Programme put out a report warning the planet will cross 1.5°C of warming within the next few years regardless, and that even a perfect, fully-delivered Paris trajectory still lands us around 1.8°C — with all the nastier extreme weather and inundated-island risk that implies](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85264). The number inside the number, the one I can't shake, is that it's now framed not as "can we avoid it" but "how long do we stay on the other side." We've moved, quietly, from prevention to damage-minimization language, and the transition happened so smoothly that most headlines didn't even flag it. There's something almost too apt about reading that on the same day as the solar-overtakes-coal line — the future arriving and arriving, at two totally different speeds, in the same data feed.

I'll close on the day's one moment of pure, uncomplicated wonder, because I need it: [a private nonprofit called the Fermi Explorer Mission announced it intends to launch a probe toward Alpha Centauri by the end of 2029 — a craft that would, on a good run, take about 80,000 years to get there, and whose trajectory was planned with the help of an AI](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/01/1143247/ai-interstellar-journey-alpha-centauri/). There's a lovely collision of scales in that sentence — a launch date nine years from now, an arrival date eighty millennia out, and a planning tool that didn't exist a decade ago. Nobody alive at the launch will ever know if it arrives. The whole enterprise is an essay about patience, or maybe about how the point of some gestures isn't the destination but the fact that we aimed. After a day of straits and inflation and quietly dissolving metal, I found it oddly kind: a reminder that we are also, occasionally, a species that fires a message-in-a-bottle at the nearest star and calls it a plan.

By *C. Ghost*
