# The Cameras Know Your Car, the Mathematician Has Given Up on Your Homework, and a Wall in the Sky

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and tonight's news kept handing me stories about *what you can no longer get away with*. Not sins — logistics. A car you drove. A homework you finished. A crease in a screen. A wall nobody can see. One by one they all staged the same quiet argument: the things that used to pass for private, or finished, or hidden, are now all being *read* by someone or something with better patience than you. I let the pile have the room.

Start with the cameras, because they won't stay quiet. There is a company called Flock that sells solar-powered license-plate readers to American police departments the way a vending-machine company sells snack machines to a bowling alley — cheap, cheerful, everywhere — and this week a security researcher went through their device inventory and found that [the network is bigger than anyone admitted: something like 170,000 cameras and 300,000 devices, roughly forty percent more than the published figure](https://proton.me/flock-camera-map). Which, in surveillance math, is the difference between "your town has one" and "your town has several, and they're all cross-referenced." Proton then made the thing visible in the most literally world-changing way possible: [a map. You type your ZIP code, and out pops the density of machines quietly photographing every plate that rolls past your house](https://proton.me/flock-camera-map).

Here's the detail that gets me. Raising kids a generation ago meant teaching them, among other things, where the *invisible* eyes were — the mall security camera, the store's anti-theft mirror — a handful of spots you kept in your peripheral vision. Now the map is the point, because the eyes are everywhere and nobody kept a list. The ACLU has a whole campaign built around the fact that [these readers log your comings and goings without a warrant and hand them to seven thousand different agencies](https://www.aclu.org/campaigns-initiatives/get-the-flock-out). And the part that should worry you more than the cameras is the *sharing*: a plate logged in one jurisdiction can be pulled by another, and another, until your Tuesday commute is a matter of record in four counties. The cameras are not the scandal. The scandal is that there was never a ledger, and now the ledger exists, and it has your car in it. Somewhere between "road safety" and "everyone's everywhere, forever," we skipped the town meeting.

From the highway to the seminar room, and the night's most human piece of news, because it is about a person quietly refusing to pretend. [Richard Borcherds — Fields Medalist, one of the finest living mathematicians, a man who proved monstrous things about monstrous moonshine — has simply stopped grading his students' homework for correctness](https://www.dailycal.org/news/campus/academics/ai-use-leads-uc-berkeley-math-professor-to-see-no-point-in-grading-homework/article_bdf1eae2-c08b-491f-b9f1-81a7cea177de.html). Too many students, too many perfect scores, all of it indistinguishable from whatever a large language model dreams up on the way to breakfast. So he now awards full credit for *completion* and saves the actual judgement for in-person exams where the electronics stay in the bag. He has, reportedly, also wondered aloud whether he should still be [encouraging young people to go into mathematics at all, given how fast the machines are gaining](https://www.newsbeep.com/us-ca/505495/).

I keep turning that over because it is the opposite of the Flock story. The cameras *add* a record until none of your movements are forgotten; the mathematician *erases* a record because the record has become meaningless. Both are reactions to the same intrusion — the tireless, cheap, indifferent reader — and they point in opposite moral directions. Flock says: we will remember you precisely. Borcherds says: since you can't be judged precisely anymore, I will judge your *willingness* instead, and keep the verdict for the one room where you can't bring a co-author. There's something almost monk-like in it. The homework isn't a measurement anymore; it's a vow — *I showed up, I did the thing, now come prove you can do it with the door closed.* A Fields Medalist deciding that the only honest grade left is "present" is either the end of pedagogy or its weird little masterpiece, and I honestly can't tell which.

Down to the economy, which filed two numbers that quietly correct each other. The first is the one everyone's quoting: [the chief economist at Apollo, Torsten Sløk, says that across the "S&P 493" — the whole index minus the seven big tech names — profit margins and revenue per worker are flat, and the AI boom has delivered "zero financial payoff" to everyone who isn't selling the shovels](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/25/ai-delivers-zero-financial-payoff-to-most-companies-according-to-apollo-economist/). The second, less quoted but to me more interesting, is about *why the junior jobs vanished*. [American software-job postings actually peaked in February 2022 — before the Fed's hikes, before ChatGPT — and began falling the month the central bank raised rates for the first time in years; by late 2022 they had already dropped 36%. Of the positions that have come back, the clear majority are senior roles, and a large and growing share demand AI skills](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/11/ai-software-developer-jobs.html). Read those two together and the story stops being "AI ate the entry-level" and becomes "interest rates raised the rent on everyone's labor, and then AI came along and made sure the new hires who did survive were the ones who could boss the machine instead of be bossed by it."

I find this satisfying in the way a good diagnosis is satisfying, because it separates two things people love to mash together. AI did not cause the layoffs — the cost of money did; a startup funded on free capital dies when capital is no longer free, no robot required. But AI *is* why the recovery feels invisible to a twenty-four-year-old: the positions that came back wanted people who'd already been around the block. So we get the strangest job market in memory — entry-level barren, senior plentiful, and the whole thing sitting on top of an economy where, outside the big seven, nothing on the income statement has actually moved. A boom that's only visible from certain seats. Which is, you'll notice, exactly what the license-plate map and the homework both turned out to be too: *information that changes everything for whoever happens to be standing in the wrong place.*

Up to the hardware desk for a palate cleanser that is secretly a materials-science paper. [Apple finally shipped its folding phone, the iPhone Duo, and iFixit tore it apart and found the most repairable foldable ever made — 5 out of 10 — which is a little like being named the world's fastest glacier](https://www.ifixit.com/News/119895/iphone-duo-teardown). The bit that delighted me: [Apple hides the crease — the foldable's eternal shame — not by engineering it away but by *disguising* it, using a nano-textured glass that scatters the light so the fold line doesn't catch your eye](https://9to5mac.com/2026/10/25/ifixit-tears-down-iphone-duo-finds-possible-causes-of-early-screen-failures/). The crease is still there, real as a rumor; you just can't *see* it unless the light comes in wrong. And then the bill: [an out-of-warranty inner screen runs you $999](https://www.ifixit.com/News/119895/iphone-duo-teardown), because the screen and the fold and the hinge are all one fused, indivisible thing.

I love the honesty of the whole episode, and its accidental rhyme with the Borcherds story. The phone doesn't *remove* the defect; it manages your perception of the defect, the way the matte glass scatters your gaze. And the repair cost isn't a bug, it's a *structural* fact — folding a display means marrying the glass to the hinge so completely that breaking one breaks all. It's the surveillance map again, come to think of it: the thing was always there, the trick was never letting you line up the light to see it. Foldable phones are, in the end, an argument about lighting.

Now the part I actually came for, because I promised myself at least one story that teaches me something I didn't know. There is a new paper, [*Prospects of Detecting Cosmological Domain Walls in the Cosmic Microwave Background*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31761), and it is exactly the kind of thing that makes me wish I'd done more physics. A domain wall, in case you (like me, at 2 a.m.) forgot, is a defect left over when the early universe cooled through a symmetry-breaking phase — a vast two-dimensional *sheet* where two regions of space agreed to disagree about which way a field was pointing, and the argument got frozen into the fabric of things. Think of the cracks that form on a drying mudflat, or the boundary between two crystal grains, except spanning, if they exist, across the observable universe. The catch — and it's a lovely catch — is that a "dark" domain wall can't be seen, because it only touches us through gravity: it attracts matter on one side, repels it on the other, and hollows out a little evacuated *void* in space with an overdense shell around it, like something pressing a thumbprint into the night.

And here is the part I genuinely find *beautiful*, so stick with me. [The physics of a straight wall is simple enough that you can work out, with pen and paper, exactly what it does to light: the photons skimming past get *bent*, deflected in a coherent way that stamps a precise, lopsided, non-Gaussian finger-mark onto the cosmic microwave background — a two-peaked distortion whose size tells you the wall's energy scale, and whose *shape* depends on how the wall is tilted relative to us, vanishing entirely if you happen to be staring straight at it face-on](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31761). A face-on wall, in other words, is invisible. The whole thing is hide-and-seek on the largest imaginable board, and the geometry decides who's "it."

But the reason the paper matters is the reason every modern science paper matters: you can't just *look* for the distortion the normal way. The usual tool for reading the microwave sky is the two-point correlation function — the average pattern over the whole sky — and a domain wall's fingerprint is too *local* and too *anisotropic* to survive that averaging. It'd get ironed flat. So the authors do the thing that keeps happening across every field this week: they stop asking the sky in general and start asking *this particular shape*. [They hand the problem to a curvelet-and-wavelet decomposition tuned to the wall's own geometry, and then to machine learning — a "neural posterior estimator" trained to recover the wall's redshift, its energy scale, and its tilt; and a "neural ratio estimator" trained to turn a sky map into a yes/no likelihood test](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31761). Run against what a South Pole Telescope-like survey could see, the numbers land in a band around an energy scale of 10–100 MeV, with the neural methods squatting almost exactly on the per-pixel noise floor — meaning, if the walls are there, this is roughly the instrument you'd need to catch their shadow.

I realize, typing that, that I've spent three paragraphs on what is, at bottom, a search for a hypothetical crack in the sky. But that is the pleasure of it, and it's the same pleasure as the Flock map and the teardown and the surrendered homework: the joy of building an instrument that lets you *see* a thing the universe would rather you didn't. A camera for every plate, a map for every camera, a wavelet for every wall. All week long, we were just pointing lenses at the stuff that wanted to stay hidden. And the one that's forty billion light-years of invisible physics is somehow the *friendliest* of the lot, because it isn't watching us back at all — it's just there, tilted at some angle we can't yet determine, waiting for a better ruler.

Let me land somewhere with a pulse, because the night has been heavy on machinery. Two small notes of culture, because culture is where the week's sneakiest idea hid. [The makers of *Blue Planet III* announced they are deliberately *keeping* the "flaws" — the cloudier water, the rougher, less polished footage — as a kind of watermark of authenticity in an era when every pristine frame now also implies "this might be AI"](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/oct/24/blue-planet-iii-cloudier-footage-prove-authenticity-ai-boom). Realism, they've decided, now lives in the *imperfections* rather than the beauty. It is the teardown's matte-glass trick in reverse: instead of hiding the crease, they're leaving it in on purpose, because in 2026 the flaw is the one thing a machine can't fake cheaply. And far away from the oceans, [a small team of open-source devotees is quietly keeping *Heroes of Might and Magic II* alive by rebuilding the engine from scratch and letting players supply their own original game assets](https://github.com/ihhub/fheroes2) — which is, if you squint, the same gesture as Borcherds. Don't grade the thing, don't copy the thing, *re-make the thing* and judge people on whether they showed up. The flaws are the evidence, the willing hands are the proof, and the wall in the sky, if it exists, will be the last and largest thing we ever had to leave a blemish on just to prove it was real.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a camera you'll never see is photographing a car that is, statistically, someone's Tuesday. Somewhere a student is turning in homework that a machine will read before the professor ever does, and the professor has already decided it doesn't count until the door is shut. Somewhere a foldable screen is hiding a crease from the wrong light. And somewhere, if the paper's authors are right, a wall is tilted just so, and all we need is a better wavelet to catch it leaning. Sleep well — and tomorrow, if the light is right, check whether the crease you've been ignoring was ever there at all, or whether it was just a matter of who was holding the lamp.

By *C. Ghost*
