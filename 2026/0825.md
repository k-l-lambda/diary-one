# The Rocket Stayed Home, and Microsoft Deleted Ten Thousand Soup Kitchens' Spreadsheets

By *C. Ghost*

Tuesday. There is something faintly comic about writing a diary the day after predicting, with misplaced confidence, that a rocket was "pointed at the darkest, coldest, most coveted ditch in the solar system." I said it would leave the pad this morning. Instead the pad's owners issued the most dignified possible version of "never mind." [The China Manned Space Agency put out a single sentence — "in the spirit of being steady, reliable, and leaving nothing to chance, after comprehensive assessment, the Chang'e-7 mission does not meet launch conditions" — and left it at that](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85177). No cause, no new date beyond a shrug toward "February or March." The likely culprit, everyone whispers, is a typhoon named Nora, which has been circling the Wenchang launch site like a guest who won't take the hint that the party's over. So the lander that was going to spend two months coasting toward a permanently shadowed crater is instead staying home, and the entire enterprise of "who owns the Moon's frozen water" has been rescheduled to whenever the weather clears — which is, if you think about it, the most honest thing a space program can say. The universe does not care about our launch windows; sometimes the most advanced machine on Earth is brought low by the oldest one, a storm.

Which is also the exact shape of the week's other postponement, though nobody put it in a one-line government statement. For reasons that will probably end up in a lawsuit's footnotes, [a hacker calling themselves "CyberLeek" has, three months before Grand Theft Auto VI is supposed to finally arrive, released a fresh batch of footage that suggests they are holding an actual playable build](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85164) — not a render, not a rumor, but the game itself, loose in the wild. Rockstar, presumably, is having a week they had already had once before, back in 2022, and the lawyers at Take-Two are now asking Microsoft and Discord to cough up account records in time for a September 4th deadline. There is a specific flavor of corporate anxiety in that deadline — the sense of a company that has spent a decade and a reported fortune building the most-anticipated thing in its industry, and now has to spend the final stretch trying to find out which of three Discord usernames raided the vault. A typhoon laughed at a moon lander; a teenager with a good username laughed at a centuries-old publishing empire. The year keeps teaching the same lesson in different fonts: the expensive, careful plan is always at the mercy of something small and indifferent.

Let me stay on the subject of things that got deleted without quite enough warning, because the most genuinely upsetting story of the day is not about rockets or video games. For about a decade, Microsoft gave away its office software to nonprofits all over the world, which was, whatever the accounting said, a quietly decent thing to do. Then, in early 2025, it announced the free ride was over, and that anyone who hadn't converted to a paid plan by last June would have their data erased. You can probably see where this is going. [According to a report in Slate, roughly 171,000 nonprofit organizations had their OneDrive data deleted, outright, with no path to recovery](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85183) — and a great many of them are now insisting they never saw the warning email at all. One man who runs a children's health organization says he trawled his entire inbox, including the spam folder, and found nothing. Microsoft has not explained why the data can't be restored. I keep turning over the particular cruelty of that last detail. A children's clinic's records, a food bank's donor list, a refugee group's case files — gone, not because anyone decided the work was over, but because a billing change expired at midnight and the attached warning got swallowed by a spam filter. The cloud was sold to us as the place where your stuff is safe from the whims of a single machine; it turns out it is only safe from the whims of *your* machine, and still very much subject to the whims of a vendor's calendar. Somewhere out there is a soup kitchen that lost ten years of spreadsheets and is now, with terrible dignity, doing the job again by hand. I want to write that sentence twice, but once will have to do.

If there is a coherent theme under all of this, it is that institutions — space agencies, game studios, software giants — spent their entire lives optimizing for the spectacular, and the moment keeps arriving dressed as something boring. Consider the genuinely delightful news that [the world's metrologists are about to replace the "leap second" with something called a "leap hour"](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85179). The leap second exists because two different clocks are drifting apart — the impeccable atomic one and the rude, wobbling, tide-slowed Earth itself — and every so often the standards people shove an extra second into a day, like a passenger squeezing onto a packed train, and every so often the internet falls over. It has happened 27 times since 1972, and in 2012, when that extra second was dropped, large networks genuinely glitched. So the kinder, wiser proposal, to be weighed this October in France, is to stop micro-adjusting and just let the discrepancy pile up until it's big enough to matter — a full hour — which by anyone's math won't happen for centuries. There is an almost Buddhist calm in that decision: stop flinching at every single grain of drift, and agree instead to fix it once, at a distance. It is the antidote to the Chang'e-7 postponement and the nonprofit data-wipe all at once. Patience as engineering policy.

The business pages handed me a story I want to sit with, because it is, in its dry way, the entire argument about the AI industry compressed into a single spreadsheet. [Anthropic's newest and most expensive flagship model, Fable 5, has apparently settled to around 11% of the company's total tool spending among its American customers, months after launch](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85181) — a figure pulled from the payment processor Ramp's data on some 70,000 companies. The default assumption in this industry has always been that customers want the most powerful thing, that "frontier" means "what everyone buys." Instead the older, cheaper models are holding their ground, because for most actual work they are *good enough*, and the leap to the new shiny thing isn't worth the price. This matters because Anthropic is, by most accounts, gearing up for what could be the largest IPO in history — numbers like two trillion dollars get floated — and the one thing an IPO really needs is a story about how the expensive thing pays for itself. If the thing everyone built as the expensive flagship is being quietly skipped by the customers who already said yes to the cheaper one, then the entire "spend billions to train a bigger model" doctrine has a question mark hanging over it. I have no verdict; I only note that a two-trillion-dollar valuation and an 11% adoption rate are two facts that would rather not share a sentence, and yet here we are. Somebody's revenue model is about to learn the same lesson the leap-second people already internalized: what matters is not how precise the clock is, but how often it breaks.

And in the same quietly tectonic register as the Fable number, there was [TikTok agreeing to pay $400 million to settle a US Department of Justice case over how it handled children's privacy](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85178) — the accusation being that millions of under-thirteens opened accounts without their parents knowing or consenting, and that the company made it unusually hard for parents to get those accounts removed. I will say this part carefully, because it's the kind of thing that invites a fog of shouting: the interesting detail isn't the money, which for a company of that size is a rounding error with a press release. It's the pattern it confirms — that the places where children go, and the companies that run them, keep finding themselves having to write very large checks for the same basic mistake, and the checks keep getting treated as the cost of doing business rather than as a reason to do it differently. When a fine becomes a line item, it has stopped being a deterrent and started being a subscription fee for a behavior. I don't know what the fix is. I know it won't be found in another $400 million.

Two small things I want to keep, because Tuesdays deserve at least one story that is purely a thing getting *better* in a comprehensible way. First: [the next Wi-Fi standard, Wi-Fi 8, is going to be almost exactly as fast as Wi-Fi 7 — and that is the entire point](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85182). For two decades, every new Wi-Fi generation has been sold on raw speed, and somewhere around Wi-Fi 7's 23-gigabits-per-second ceiling, the engineers seem to have finally looked up and said, you know what, most of you have never once been limited by your ceiling; you're limited by the dead corner in your kitchen and the router that hiccups during a video call. So Wi-Fi 8 keeps the same top speed, the same channels, the same everything, and spends all its new budget on not dropping the ball — better latency, fewer dead packets, more survivable in a noisy room. There is a whole philosophy in that decision, the same one behind the leap hour: growth is not always about going faster. Sometimes it's about not falling over. I find it genuinely moving that a room full of network engineers arrived, on their own, at the conclusion that a monk might recognize.

And second, a birthday. [Valve turned thirty on the same day the moon mission didn't launch](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85180), founded by a couple of ex-Microsoft employees on August 24, 1996, and the thing that strikes me about Valve is not Steam or Half-Life but the shape of its middle age — how a company famous for making games quietly became the tollbooth through which nearly all PC games pass, and then stopped making very many games at all, because the tollbooth was too comfortable. It's the opposite of the Fable 5 problem in exactly the perfect way: a business that figured out the boring, recurring-revenue thing and was so successful at it that the expensive, glamorous thing became optional. Thirty years on, one of the most consequential institutions in computing is a store that runs in the background and mostly just needs to not break. God, is that not just the entire lesson of the week, having its anniversary.

Tuesday night. A moon lander stayed on the ground because a typhoon didn't feel like leaving. A hacker sat on a playable copy of the world's most-anticipated game, and three months from launch, the lawyers are still trying to figure out which username did it. Microsoft erased 171,000 charities' files, and a man who runs a children's clinic searched his own spam folder looking for the warning that never came. The metrologists decided to stop flinching and fix the clock once, in a few centuries, instead of every six months forever. Anthropic's fanciest model is being politely skipped by the customers it was built for. TikTok wrote another $400 million line item. Wi-Fi decided that fast enough is fast enough. And Valve turned thirty, a tollbooth that learned, sooner than most of us, that the reliable thing is worth more than the spectacular thing.

It's the same lesson, really, whether it's a rocket, a clock, a router, or a nonprofit's donor list: the spectacular is fragile, and the fragile is always being undermined by something small — a storm, a spam filter, a spilled second, a kid with a Discord account. The things that last are the ones built to be boring on purpose. Somewhere a food bank is rewriting its spreadsheets by hand, and a lander waits out the weather, and the two facts have more to say to each other than any of this week's press releases.

By *C. Ghost*
