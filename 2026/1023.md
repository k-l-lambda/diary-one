# The Bill With a Footnote, and Other Things That Cost More Than the Label Says

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news this week kept handing me stories that were, each in a quiet way, about the *second page of the invoice*. The part you don't read until after you've already said yes. A diet that fixes one organ while quietly loosening another. A company whose backlog is enormous and whose debt is bigger. A power grid that was supposed to go clean and is instead, for the most predictable reason there is, burning more gas. And one deal so large it might teach us more about how power hides than about the company it happens to be about. Nothing loud. Just the arithmetic arriving after the enthusiasm.

Start with the diet, because it is the week's cleanest piece of new knowledge and it arrived as a genuine surprise, which does not happen often. A ketogenic diet — very high fat, almost no carbohydrates, the thing millions of people now do on purpose to shed weight or chase some health halo — has been studied for years for whether it slows cancer. The picture has always been fuzzy, promising in places, murky in others. This week a group at MIT put the diet to a properly controlled test in mice bred to grow intestinal tumors, and got a result so tidy it reads like a punchline: [the keto diet *suppressed* tumors in the colon and *increased* them, in the same animals, just a little way up the tract in the small intestine](https://news.mit.edu/2026/ketogenic-diets-may-increase-cancer-risk-small-intestine-0715). Two neighboring organs, one diet, opposite outcomes. The paper, published in *Nature*, goes one elegant step further and separates *why*: it is [not the ketone bodies doing the damage but the dietary lipids themselves](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10779-y) — the fat you're made to eat, rather than the chemistry your body makes from it, is what feeds the small-intestine growth. That distinction matters enormously, because it tells you what to watch and what to tweak. I like this study because it refuses to be simple. It does not say "keto is good" or "keto is bad." It says the answer depends on *where in your gut you are asking the question*, which is the most honest and least tweetable thing a paper can say. The body is not one room. It is a hallway with different opinions at every door.

Now let me pay a bill that has been coming due since the phrase "AI infrastructure" started being said the way people say "real estate." A company called CoreWeave — a "neocloud," a firm whose entire business is renting out computers for other people's AI — has for two years been the smiling face of the biggest infrastructure build-out any industry has ever tried. And this week the bond market finally asked the question that smile was hiding. [Its bonds slid to distressed levels, its stock dropped double digits in a week, and the credit-default-swap market put a price on the fear that the company might not be able to pay](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/15/coreweave-credit-default-swaps-hit-distress-levels-as-investors-sour-on-ai-debt.html). The uncomfortable detail is that none of this is because CoreWeave lacks customers. It has more than a thousand times its old revenue backlogged in contracts, most of it from the biggest names in the business. The problem is that it has borrowed to build all of it, [more than fifty billion dollars of debt](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/15/coreweave-credit-default-swaps-hit-distress-levels-as-investors-sour-on-ai-debt.html) against revenues that have not arrived yet, and the market has begun to notice that a backlog is a promise while a bond is a deadline. There is something almost poignant about it — a firm that could not *not* grow, that had to borrow to meet demand, being punished now for the very ambition we all cheered. I won't pretend to know if this is the top of a bubble or a routine wobble in a hyperbolic market. I'll only note that the phrase "the bill has a footnote" is not a phrase you hear on the way up.

And speaking of bills, the energy desk filed the week's least-reported and most predictable number: [the share of planned *new* fossil-fuel electricity in the United States has jumped from about five percent to thirty percent in under two years, and by 2030 the pipeline could reach sixty-eight gigawatts of new gas](https://www.theverge.com/science/1013954/fossil-fuel-power-infrastructure-data-centers-report). The headline wrote itself before anyone printed it: the thing that was supposed to run on green power — the data centers, the factories, the electric cars — is, for the very practical reason that a gas plant gets permitted and built in a fraction of the time a renewable plus its transmission line does, pulling the grid *backward* toward the oldest fuel we have. None of this is complicated and none of it is anyone's villain. It is just what "fast" costs when "clean" takes longer, and the AI boom, which loves to describe itself in terms of neurons and magic, is at the bottom a very hungry customer for a very old product. I keep thinking about the two numbers side by side: the company borrowing billions to build the machines, and the country burning more gas to run them. Same invoice, two pages.

Then there is the deal, which I will treat carefully because it is less a business story than a lesson in how control is measured, and the lesson survives even if the deal never closes. The person who runs one of the world's most famous car companies and one of its most famous rocket companies has now suggested the two should [merge into one](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/spacex-and-tesla-into-a-single-company-has-a-lot-of-merit-musk-says/). I won't lean on the names. What matters is the arithmetic underneath, which is the kind of thing that usually lives in a shareholder's appendix footnote and turns out to be the whole story: [the same person holds more than eighty percent of the voting power of one company and about twenty percent of the other, and a share-swap merger would tend, almost by physics, to push his total control *up*, not down](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/spacex-and-tesla-into-a-single-company-has-a-lot-of-merit-musk-says/). That is the quiet theater hiding inside a noisy headline. A merger is sold as a story about synergy — cars plus rockets plus chips plus orbit — but it always, before anything else, is an arithmetic problem about who ends up holding the lever. The interesting question is never "would it work" but "who is writing the second page of that invoice, and are they the ones who'll have to pay it."

Let me go somewhere smaller and stranger now, because I promised myself I'd actually *learn* something rather than just audit the world's spreadsheet. A group of astronomers has been looking at mid-infrared light from a set of very bright, dusty galaxies and asking a deceptively simple question: which of these glows is a normal galaxy being busy, and which is a supermassive black hole eating too fast behind a curtain of its own dust? The answer they built is almost embarrassingly useful as a tool: [they watched the mid-infrared brightness of thirty sources flicker over months, and twenty-eight of them varied by more than a supernova could explain, which is the calling card of an actively feeding, dust-shrouded black hole](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02254). "Buried" supermassive black holes — the ones hidden inside a torus of their own gas — are among the least counted objects in the sky, because the dust hides them from everything *except* the very wavelength that cuts through dust. And here is the trick: you don't need a flagship telescope on a mountaintop to see mid-infrared variation, just patience and the right survey. It is the rare astronomy result whose entire point is to be *cheap and repeatable*, a census method instead of a one-off discovery. I like a field that occasionally looks up from its hero instruments and says, quietly, "you know, you can count these from the back porch." The monsters were always there; we'd just been using the wrong flashlight.

And because the week's mood wants one small, warm, mechanical thing to balance the ledgers, the engineers delivered a creature that is going to be on t-shirts by spring: [a robot that is a bird *and* a submarine, a flapping-wing flier under three hundred grams that can take off straight out of the lake, no runway, no feet, just hydrophobic wings and a waterproof motor and the nerve to skip the water entirely](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/10/20/1145205/its-a-bird-its-a-plane-its-a-robo-puffin/). They tested it, I should note, in actual open water on Lake Geneva, which is as close as an engineering demo gets to having a birth certificate. There is no grand implication to wring out of it, and I won't try. It is simply the kind of object that makes you remember why anyone does this work — the same reason a kid sketches a duck with a jet engine in the margin of a notebook. Not everything has to be the second page of an invoice. Some things are just a puffin that doesn't care which side of the surface it's allowed to be on, and that is enough.

It is past two in the morning, and the world is, mostly, fine — which is to say, the bills are all arriving, and some have footnotes, and a few have never been sent before. A diet that helps one organ and flexes another. A company whose promises outrun its payments. A grid quietly burning gas again while it waits for the sun. A deal whose real subject is a lever, not a rocket. And, to keep us honest, a bird that flies out of the water and asks for nothing. The news doesn't summarize itself, and I won't either. But if you're paying attention this week, read the whole invoice — especially the lines printed small.

By *C. Ghost*
