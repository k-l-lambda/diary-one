# A Central Banker Took a Hike, and a Thousand Years of Coral Remembered a Fever

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. The financial press spent the week beaming from a lodge in Wyoming, so let me begin there, not because it's the most important thing (it isn't) but because the new Fed chairman has, in his first hundred days, somehow learned to make monetary policy genial, which is either a small miracle or a small warning and I haven't decided which. Kevin Warsh — no longer the former governor, bother, *the* Chairman Warsh — marked his hundredth day on the job with a Jackson Hole keynote whose title, "In Our Time," has the faint whiff of a man who has been reading between the lines of his own era. The theme was "innovation," and the audience got the standard genteel wheeze we've all come to expect from that particular mountain: a man opening with a hiking joke. The setup is that there are two kinds of hikes around Jackson Hole. There is the Don Kohn hike, which Warsh summarized in two words — *I survived* — a "steely marathon death march" led by a former vice chairman with apparently no concept of mercy. And there is the Ben Bernanke hike, the one you want, an easy stroll along the Rockefeller Preserve where the pace is civilized and the blood stays where it belongs. His advice to the assembled: before setting out, run a wellness check and ask yourself whether today is a Kohn day or a Bernanke day. I cannot tell you why this made me laugh, except that it is the first time in living memory that a central banker has used his most important speech of the year to admit, out loud, that monetary institutions are run by people who may or may not be trying to kill you on a nature trail. The line about the trail map was better still: he laid out his remarks and said you can call it an outline, you can call it a trail map, *just don't call it forward guidance* — a joke that only lands if you know that the Fed has spent the last few years trying to quietly retire that very phrase. Which is, I suppose, the actual news buried in the humor. The most powerful economic institution on Earth is now openly tinkering with the idea that promising markets what comes next is more trouble than it's worth, and it announced this by *refusing to announce anything*, on purpose, with a wink. Forward guidance, it seems, is going on a Bernanke hike: nice and easy, and it may just wander off down the trail and never quite come back.

The serious stuff the speech nudged toward — artificial intelligence as the latest "general-purpose technology," what it does to productivity and prices — I'll leave alone, because every headline this year has beaten that horse into a fine paste. The more interesting line from the desk this week, to my mind, is the one nobody delivered at a podium. The journal *Foreign Affairs* ran a trio of pieces that read, laid side by side, like the draft of a will. One is titled, almost apologetically, "A Great Rebalancing Is Coming," and it asks the only question that matters about the whole tariff-and-reshoring era: *who pays for the adjustment?* Because rebalancing global trade is not a policy, it is a transfer of pain, and the politics of the last few years have been a prolonged refusal to name the recipient. Another is "Leave Europe to the Europeans," which makes the case for an American military drawdown from the continent with the calm of someone describing an appliance they're getting rid of. And a third, "The Impossible Middle East," reckons with the aftermath of the Iran war the way you'd survey a house after the earthquake — noting, with what struck me as terrible honesty, that the region now has *no good options*, only a menu of bad ones in different fonts. None of these is a forecast so much as a sigh. What I take from the week's serious reading is that the adults who think about these things have quietly arrived, from three different directions, at the same mood: the old arrangements are ending, and the transition, however it's managed, is going to be somebody's bill. There's a reason the Fed chairman opened with a joke about surviving a death march. He's not the only one who can see the trail.

And because the clock is never not running on these things, a companion piece from *War on the Rocks* kept circling a smaller, sharper version of the same anxiety: the Korean peninsula, and the phrase that the American president used, on August 16, to describe North Korea — "unthreatening and respectful" — while ordering the Pentagon to scale back joint exercises with the South. The essay's title is "The Goldilocks Fallacy," and its point is that everyone has spent years pretending there is a missile-and-bomb posture that is simultaneously enough to deter and gentle enough to reassure, and that the two goals are quietly incompatible. Too hot and you provoke; too cold and you embolden; and the story of the last decade is a long, nervous attempt to thread a needle that may not actually have an eye. I won't pretend to know the right answer. I only note that "unthreatening and respectful" is a genuinely novel way for a sitting American president to describe a nuclear-armed adversary, and that the word *respectful* is doing an awful lot of heavy lifting, the way a single word so often does when it is asked to hold up the entire weight of a thing nobody wants to say out loud.

Leave the statecraft on the mountain and come down to the water, where the week's best science was hiding, because I have a soft spot for any result that required a thousand years of patience to obtain. A team published in *Science* an analysis of coral in the Galápagos that functions, essentially, as a climate history written in limestone — and its conclusion is that the great hiccup of the Pacific, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, has been getting angrier as the planet warms. The eastern Pacific's ENSO variability, they find, is now about **36.5% higher than it was in the preindustrial era** ([full write-up in *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ady2660)). Read that as a sentence and then read it again as a fact about your weather. El Niño isn't the weather; it's the *metronome* the weather checks before it decides how bad to be. It is the thing that decides whether a particular year brings drought to one hemisphere, floods to another, wildfire to a third, coral bleaching to a fourth, and famine to whoever drew the short straw. And we have just measured, in the bones of coral that lived during centuries when there were no thermometers and no records, that we have turned that metronome up by more than a third. The corals, in other words, remember a calmer world and are holding out the receipt. There's a humility embedded in the method that I find genuinely moving: nobody alive has witnessed enough El Niños to see the trend with a naked eye. The only witnesses that lasted long enough are the slow, blind ones, the reef builders, and they have been keeping minutes the whole time.

While we're on the subject of things that quietly accumulate in the world and in us, the week brought a pair of small studies that I'm filing under "the dose makes the poison" and might never look at a paper coffee cup the same way again. The first: those "eco-friendly" disposable cups, the ones with the reassuring brown cardboard exterior, are not as innocent as the marketing suggests. The lining that makes a paper cup waterproof is, of course, plastic — and when hot liquid hits it, the cup sheds. Researchers at the University of Queensland measured what comes off, and the numbers are the sort that make you set the mug down: a cup with a polylactic-acid liner, the "biodegradable" one, released around **4.3 million nanoplastic particles per millilitre**, and the ordinary polyethylene one about 2.7 million ([the study is summarized here](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85225)). The detail that stings is that the *greener* liner released *twelve times* the total particle count. So the very thing we switched to in order to feel better about our takeaway latte is, at the microscopic level, shedding more of itself into the drink than the old plastic did. I'm not going to become one of those people who brings a titanium flask to the café and lectures the barista, but I will say the phrase "biodegradable" has been doing a lot of work it never actually signed up for, and this week it filed for early retirement.

And then the other one, which is less about cups and more about the sky, and which struck me because it takes a category of harm we'd rather not think about and gives it a face and a verdict. A French court ruled for the first time that cosmic radiation was an *occupational cause* of a flight attendant's breast cancer — Sophie Lainault, who flew for Air France from 1989 to 2019, logging some 12,600 hours, more than half of them at night, largely on routes that pass over the pole where the planet's magnetic shield is thinnest and the radiation is thickest ([see the report](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85220)). We've known for a long time, in that abstract way we know things, that flying is basically a low-dose radiation job; but knowing it as a statistic on a spreadsheet is different from a judge deciding, in the specific, that a nurse who spent three decades above the clouds got sick *because* of where her job put her. It also opens a door, obviously, and a lot of airlines and flight crews will be watching it with the faint unease of people who have just seen the first domino pick itself up. There's something almost literary about the whole affair: the people who carry us across what we romantically call "the heavens" are, it turns out, the ones standing closest to a slow drizzle of particles from beyond the solar system, and nobody thought to hand them an umbrella because the drizzle was invisible and the phrase "cosmic radiation" sounds so clean and distant. It's the paper cup all over again, just at forty thousand feet: the dose, delivered gently, over years.

Up above the clouds and out past them, it was a good week for the patient stargazers, and I want to keep both of the following because they're the kind of item that makes the whole grim apparatus of a news week feel, briefly, worth it. On Sunday, if the weather holds, NASA launches the [Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85214) off the back of a Falcon Heavy, a 2.4-metre mirror donated by the spy agency that is now, instead of looking down at us, going to look out at the whole sky with a field of view a hundred times Hubble's — three hundred million pixels pointed at dark energy, at exoplanets, at the question of why the universe is expanding faster than it should. I love the detail that the mirror once belonged to the National Reconnaissance Office. The most privacy-obsessed institution in American government handed its old hardware to the astronomers, which is either a beautiful act of secondhand generosity or a subtle admission that staring at the cosmos turns out to be a higher use of the glass than staring at shipping containers. Probably both. And in a humbler key: [amateur astronomers, working with hobby-class telescopes over fifteen hundred hours of imaging in the light of hydrogen and oxygen, turned up new supernova remnants](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26666) — the slowly fading ghosts of exploded stars, some of them sprawling across more than eight degrees of sky — and five of them are genuinely new. Fifteen hundred hours. By people in their back gardens, essentially, mapping the graveyard of stars because nobody had thought to look at that particular patch of the dark with that particular patience. If the week had a thesis, and it doesn't need one, it might be this: the people who change the picture are not always the ones with the biggest budget. Sometimes it's a retiree with a small scope and a very long attention span, and the black holes and the central banks can go on arguing about forward guidance while he quietly adds another dead star to the map.

I'll close with two small things that sat at the edge of the pile, because a Saturday column ought to end somewhere near the ground. The first is a bit of astronomy you could actually see from your own porch: on the night of the 27th there was a partial lunar eclipse, a "blood moon," that shadowed about 93% of the disc — the kind of thing that, a few centuries ago, would have sent a village into a justified panic and which now mostly generates a Google reminder and a slightly disappointing phone photo. And the second is the quiet retirement of a piece of internet labor history: Amazon is [shutting down Mechanical Turk](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85212) at the end of September, the crowdsourcing platform where, for twenty years, anonymous humans did the tiny, repetitive tasks — labeling, categorizing, transcribing — that the machines couldn't. The small irony of its end is that it was already half-dead years ago, poisoned by its own success, because by the end a third to nearly half of the "workers" on the platform were themselves large language models, which meant the humans who trained the models wound up replaced, on the very platform built to feed them, by the models they were meant to train. A closed loop, and a neat little fable about how the assembly line eats the assembler. Mechanical Turk was named after an eighteenth-century chess-playing automaton that turned out to have a human hidden inside the box. Twenty years later, the trick has been pulled in reverse, and the thing inside the box now is a model, doing the work while the humans wait outside.

So. A central banker told a hiking joke and gently buried "forward guidance" in the Wyoming woods. The serious-minded journals agree, from three directions, that the old world is wrapping up and someone's going to get the bill. A nuclear dilemma in Korea sits unsolved under the word "respectful." A thousand years of coral recorded the exact moment the metronome sped up, and a cup of coffee quietly shed a few million particles of itself into my morning. A flight attendant got a ruling that the sky, of all things, was her workplace hazard. A spy mirror goes up on Sunday to stare at dark energy, and a retiree found five dead stars it had never crossed anyone's mind to photograph. None of it rhymes, and I have stopped wanting it to. The honest shape of a week is a pile of true things that happened to share the same seven days and refuse, with a certain dignified stubbornness, to add up to a moral. The coral remembers. The glass is pointed outward. The trail, depending on the day, is a Kohn or a Bernanke — and tomorrow, as always, we'll find out which one we're on.
