# The Cambrian Sea Creature Had the First Spider Fangs, and the Banks Had Jeffrey Epstein's Receipts

By *C. Ghost*

Tuesday. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,600 today. I only mention this because the mechanism behind the number is almost funny in its simplicity: the president said "last chance for a deal," the treasury secretary said a Hormuz deal could arrive "today or tomorrow," and the market, which had spent most of July pricing in a widening war, abruptly decided to price in a narrowing one. Brent swung wildly — up 3 percent in the morning, then sharply down after Qatar confirmed diplomatic efforts were real and Bessent did his CNBC hit. WTI finished below $77. Five consecutive nights without bombs falling on Iran. Five consecutive nights without ballistic missiles flying toward Jordan or Kuwait. The pause is holding, or at least the absence of explosions qualifies as a pause, and the market is treating the absence of explosions as worth roughly 7,600 S&P points and a whole lot of relieved exhaling.

The negotiations themselves are a hall of mirrors. Trump says talks are happening. Iran says no direct talks are happening. Qatar says it's shuttling drafts between the two sides through Oman, Pakistan, and Egypt. All three statements can be true simultaneously, which is the kind of diplomatic geometry that makes Wittgenstein look like a straightforward writer. Iran wants a temporary arrangement — up to three months — where it administers a controlled shipping route through the strait and splits "service fees" with Oman. Trump wants denuclearization as phase two. The gap between phase one and phase two is measured not in days but in decades of mutual distrust, and yet here we are, on a Tuesday, with a treasury secretary telling CNBC that freedom of movement through the strait might be restored within 48 hours. Bessent specifically said the deal would *not* involve Iran charging a toll, which is the kind of detail that matters enormously and is also the kind of detail that Iran might have different ideas about. The last time anyone tried to charge ships for passage through a strategic waterway, it was called a blockade. Now it might be called a "service fee" and split fifty-fifty with Oman, which is either brilliant diplomacy or the opening scene of a very complicated insurance claim.

---

Meanwhile, in the category of "people who are making money from the thing that is also killing people," the oil majors reported their second-quarter earnings today, and the numbers are the kind of numbers that make you want to take a shower. BP made just under $6 billion between April and June — double last year's profit. Saudi Aramco made $33.4 billion, a 33 percent jump, beating analyst expectations while the strait that its tankers need to exit remains an active combat zone. The war has made oil more expensive. More expensive oil has made oil companies richer. Two Saudi tankers threaded the Bab-el-Mandeb over the weekend for the first time in weeks, which counts as a logistics victory, but the broader pattern is unmistakable: the companies extracting and selling the stuff are having their best year in recent memory, and the companies that need oil to make things — airlines, shipping, manufacturing — are quietly absorbing the cost or passing it to consumers or both. Trump criticized the oil firms today for making too much money and called on them to lower prices, which is about as effective as criticizing gravity for making things fall. The oil companies did not create the war. They are merely its beneficiaries, and beneficiary is a word that sounds neutral until you sit with it for a minute.

Let me talk about something that is not oil, because the day had stories that deserve more than a paragraph wedged between war updates.

Senator Ron Wyden, the top Democrat on the Senate Finance Committee, released a report today titled "Looking the Other Way: How Wall Street Banks Enabled Jeffrey Epstein's Sex Trafficking." It is the product of a four-year investigation, and its findings are granular, damning, and somehow still shocking nearly seven years after Epstein's death. JPMorgan Chase, Deutsche Bank, and Bank of America — three of the largest financial institutions on the planet — are accused of systematically ignoring hundreds of millions of dollars in suspicious transfers, coaching Epstein on how to withdraw cash without triggering alarms, and failing to file the reports that federal anti-money laundering laws require.

The report names names. Specific bankers. Specific transactions. It alleges that Bank of America "likely violated" federal law by failing to report $170 million in payments from billionaire investor Leon Black to Epstein. It alleges that JPMorgan executives "coached Epstein on how to withdraw the large amounts of cash he needed to pay his victims." It also takes aim at the Treasury Department and the Justice Department for obstructing the investigation and refusing to hand over documents. Wyden called the report "a ready-made roadmap for prosecutors, investigators and members of Congress to finally start holding the Epstein class accountable."

What gets me about this story — beyond the obvious horror of what Epstein did — is the sheer institutional inertia it describes. Three banks. Hundreds of millions of dollars. Dozens of employees whose job descriptions literally included "report suspicious activity." And none of them did, or at least none of them did until after Epstein was arrested in 2019, at which point the reporting became retroactive and therefore legally safer. The system worked exactly as designed, which is to say it worked for the person with the money. The phrase "looked the other way" suggests a kind of passive neglect, but the report's details suggest something more active: looking directly at the money, understanding what it was for, and deciding that the client relationship was more important than the law. Banking is not supposed to be a moral enterprise. It is supposed to be a regulated one. This report suggests the regulation failed at every level, and the people who designed the regulatory system — the Treasury, the Justice Department — then failed to investigate the failure. Epstein is dead. Ghislaine Maxwell is in prison. The bankers named in this report are presumably still employed, still eating lunch at the same midtown restaurants, still managing other people's money, because the gap between "moral outrage" and "legal consequence" in American finance is roughly the width of the Atlantic Ocean and just as hard to cross.

Now I want to go five hundred and eighteen million years back in time, because the Cambrian Period delivered a gift today and I refuse to let it get buried under the weight of everything else.

A team from Yunnan University and the University of Leicester published a paper in *Nature* describing what they found inside a tiny fossil of *Urokodia aequalis*, a marine creature about two to three centimeters long that lived in the seas of what is now southern China. They used X-ray tomography to peer inside the rock — the same way a doctor uses a CT scan to look at a patient's internal organs — and what they saw, preserved in mummified soft tissue after half a billion years, were a pair of pincer-like appendages just behind the creature's eyes. Chelicerae. The earliest known evidence of the structures that would eventually become the fangs of every spider on Earth, the pincers of every scorpion, the feeding appendages of every tick and mite and horseshoe crab.

There are more than 100,000 described species of chelicerates alive today. Every single one of them — every black widow, every vinegaroon, every dust mite living quietly in your pillowcase — descends from something that looked more or less like *Urokodia*: a small, stalk-eyed marine predator with jointed limbs and a hard external skeleton, hunting in the shallows of a world that had not yet invented trees or bones or lungs. The fossil also preserves what may be book gills — respiratory structures still found in modern horseshoe crabs — suggesting that *Urokodia* already had the breathing apparatus that its descendants would use for the next half-billion years, give or take a few modifications.

Professor Yu Liu, the lead author, described the moment of discovery: "We were using X-ray tomography analysis of these fossils to reveal their soft anatomy buried in the rocks for hundreds of millions of years, when suddenly we noticed the pincer-like limbs at the front of the animal. We knew immediately that this was a very exciting fossil." There is something deeply charming about a scientist describing the discovery of the oldest spider-fang precursors on Earth with the phrase "a very exciting fossil." The understatement is geological in its own right.

The Chengjiang fossil site in Yunnan preserves evidence of more than 200 types of animals from the early Cambrian, a period when animal body plans were exploding in diversity so rapidly that paleontologists call it the Cambrian Explosion and still don't fully understand what triggered it. *Urokodia* was published on the 42nd anniversary of the site's discovery, which is the kind of anniversary gift that most research sites do not receive: a cover story in *Nature*, a half-billion-year-old spider ancestor, and a new chapter in the evolutionary history of one of the planet's most successful animal groups. Not bad for a Tuesday.

A few more things, because Tuesday was generous with the dispatches from places that are not war zones or fossil beds.

NASA's Curiosity rover, which landed on Mars on August 5, 2012 — tomorrow is its fourteenth anniversary, if you're counting, which I am — has driven into a valley in Gale Crater covered in what looks like a vast honeycomb of tiny polygon-shaped fractures, each only a few inches wide, stretching across the landscape like a Martian tiling project abandoned mid-installation. [The discovery was announced August 1](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4947-4953-gale-crater-then-and-now/) and has scientists scratching their heads about the scale. Polygonal cracking on Earth usually forms from the expansion and contraction of mud as it dries, or from freeze-thaw cycles in permafrost. But these polygons are much smaller than typical desiccation cracks, and the mechanism on Mars — which hasn't had surface water in the way Earth does for billions of years — is unclear. Curiosity is fourteen years into a mission that was originally planned for two. It has climbed more than 800 meters up the slopes of Mount Sharp. Its wheels are chewed up. Its drill has been temperamental. And it is still finding things that nobody expected, on a planet that has been dead for eons, in a crater that has been dry for longer than multicellular life has existed on Earth. The rover's anniversary is tomorrow. I hope someone at JPL brought cake.

In Michigan, the cyclosporiasis outbreak — caused by a microscopic parasite that contaminates food and water and produces weeks of debilitating gastrointestinal symptoms — has now killed two people and sickened more than 11,000, with 193 hospitalized since May. Cases have been detected in 45 states. The CDC has traced the outbreak to lettuce or salad greens, which is the epidemiological equivalent of saying "probably something you ate." The outbreak comes a year after the Trump administration ended the FDA's mandatory tracking requirement for Cyclospora and five other foodborne pathogens, switching to a voluntary system where states choose whether to report. A gastroenterologist at NYU put it bluntly: the change in regulation "is contributing to the fact that this outbreak was so massive." Two people are dead from a salad. The parasite that killed them has been known to science for decades. The tracking system that might have caught it earlier was dismantled, and the deaths are now a data point in a debate about regulatory philosophy that the dead people did not participate in.

In Sudan, an army drone strike hit a court in the village of Garra al-Zawaya in North Darfur on Sunday, killing 35 people. The Emergency Lawyers group, which has documented abuses by both sides of the civil war, reported the toll. The UN says more than 1,000 civilians have been killed in drone strikes in Sudan in the first five months of this year alone. The conflict is now in its fourth year. At least 59,000 dead. Thirteen million displaced. Famine conditions in multiple regions. A drone strike on a court — a place where people go to resolve disputes, to seek justice, to be heard — is the kind of detail that makes the word "war" feel like it's missing a syllable or two. The court was in territory controlled by the Rapid Support Forces, which means the army considered it a legitimate target, which means the concept of "legitimate target" has stretched to include buildings whose entire function is to prevent the kind of violence that destroyed them. I don't have a conclusion for this paragraph. The paragraph is the conclusion.

A few more, quickly, because Tuesday kept going.

In Gaza, a mass funeral was held for 112 Palestinians from two families killed in an Israeli attack on residential buildings in Gaza City in November 2023. The bodies remained buried under rubble for more than two years before Palestinian Civil Defense workers recovered them in mid-July. Among the dead: 44 children, 37 women. A spokesperson for Gaza's Civil Emergency Service held remains wrapped in a Palestinian flag and asked, to the cameras, to anyone who might be watching: "Why are children getting killed in Gaza? Why are women getting killed in Gaza?" The funeral was today. The attack was November 2023. The gap between the two dates is not just time. It's a measure of what gets buried when the machinery of war moves on.

Todd Blanche, Trump's former personal attorney, appears on track to be confirmed as the next attorney general after two Republican senators agreed to support him and he formally rescinded the $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization fund." The fund is dead. The tax immunity deal now applies only retroactively. The Senate Judiciary Committee voted today. The confirmation saga has been, among other things, a masterclass in how to trade a fund nobody could explain for a confirmation nobody could stop.

In the UK, the Met Office confirmed that England and Wales just experienced the driest July in nearly 200 years. Rivers are low. Reservoirs are shrinking. The National Farmers' Union warned of potential food shortages. Meanwhile, wildfires outside Athens jumped containment lines, and firefighters in Bordeaux guarded against flare-ups. The heat wave that has been baking Europe for weeks is not done. It is, if anything, just getting comfortable.

---

Tuesday night. Day 158 of a war that might be ending, or might be pausing, or might be playing diplomatic three-dimensional chess while the board is on fire. The S&P hit 7,600. The treasury secretary said a deal on the strait could arrive within 48 hours, and the president said it was Iran's "last chance," and Iran said it wasn't negotiating with the United States at all, and Qatar said actually everyone is negotiating through us, and all of these statements are somehow compatible. Oil companies reported billions in profit while the president told them to stop making so much money, and the markets shrugged because markets have learned to treat presidential criticism of corporate profits the way they treat weather forecasts — noted, then ignored.

A Senate Democrat published a report documenting how three of the world's largest banks systematically enabled a sex trafficker for years, and the report is being called a "roadmap," which means someone now has to decide whether to follow the map or file it in the drawer marked "too much work." Two people in Michigan died from a parasite in their salad, and the tracking system that might have prevented the outbreak was dismantled before it happened. A fourteen-year-old rover on Mars found a valley covered in tiny geometric fractures and nobody knows what made them. A five-hundred-and-eighteen-million-year-old fossil revealed the first spider fangs, preserved in soft tissue that had no right to survive half a billion years, and the scientist who found them described it as "a very exciting fossil." A mass funeral was held in Gaza for bodies recovered after two years under rubble. A drone killed 35 people in a courtroom in Darfur.

The days do not sort themselves into categories. You try anyway, because the alternative is to let them wash over you undifferentiated, and that way lies a different kind of death — the death of attention, the death of caring about things that happen to people you will never meet. The Cambrian sea creature with the first spider fangs was somebody's ancestor — not yours, not directly, but somewhere in the branching tree that produced the mite in your pillow and the spider in your bathroom and the horseshoe crab on the beach, *Urokodia* is at the root. Five hundred and eighteen million years later, we are still finding its body, and it is still telling us things we didn't know. That's not a metaphor. It's just paleontology. But it's the kind of paleontology I want to carry into Wednesday.
