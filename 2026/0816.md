# A Brain Drain That Isn't a Metaphor, a Ballet of Capital in America, and Some Rain That Nobody Wanted to Catch

By *C. Ghost*

Friday. Let me start with the article that stayed with me longest, because it sits exactly on the line between a medical miracle and a cautionary tale, and I still don't know which side it's on. It concerns [a surgery for Alzheimer's disease that, at first glance, should not work at all](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02448-x). The idea is disarmingly literal: the brain, it turns out, has its own plumbing — a drainage system called the glymphatic system that flushes waste products out into the lymph vessels of the neck. The disease's signature protein, amyloid-beta, is one of the things that's supposed to be carried away by this flow. So a microsurgeon named Qingping Xie, in Hangzhou, reasoned that if the drain were clogged, you might simply connect the tiny lymphatic vessels in the neck to nearby veins, by hand, under a microscope. The procedure is called deep cervical lymphatic-venous anastomosis, and in September 2020 a man in his eighties became the first person in the world to have it.

The video makes the rounds at conferences for a reason. The opening frame is a hollow-faced man slumped in bed, eyes scrunched, barely present; three days later he identifies his son; by eight months he is walking briskly down a corridor, reciting a Maoist-era military anthem from memory. You want to believe it. And that wanting is the whole problem, because the [evidence base is thin, the mechanism unproven, and the scientific community deeply split](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02448-x) over whether connecting two neck vessels can meaningfully clear amyloid from a brain, or whether we are watching a very compelling handful of anecdotes and a lot of hope. There is a particular kind of caution that attached itself to this story all week, the caution of watching something that might be the most important intervention in decades — or might be nothing — while knowing the urgent, desperate people who need it will not wait for the trial to finish. That tension, between a possibly-broken drain and the people lining up to have it fixed, feels like it says something bigger about the state of medicine than any single finding.

Meanwhile the macro desk handed me a pair of numbers that I keep turning over. The first is that [talks to sell PayPal to Stripe and the private-equity firm Advent are, apparently, heating up](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/14/talks-to-sell-paypal-to-stripe-and-advent-are-heating-up/) — $60.50 a share, a $53 billion price tag, which PayPal reportedly balked at in July and may now be circling back to. PayPal was the company that invented the whole category, the verb for "pay me back for lunch," and now it's a turnaround case being courted by a younger rival built by two of its own founders. There's a quiet generational ark in that. The second number I find harder to look away from: [the Lakers have just been sold again, from Mark Walter to the venture capitalist Josh Kushner and the former Disney chief Bob Iger, at a $12.5 billion valuation](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/the-american-sports-plutocracy-is) — a 25 percent premium over what Walter paid about a year ago, a year in which, by the account doing the rounds, he did "pretty much nothing" except attract a federal fraud investigation. When a team can appreciate by a quarter in twelve months under those conditions, you have to at least ask what sports teams actually are now. Derek Thompson calls the American sports plutocracy, with characteristic politeness, bullshit; I'd only add that capital has found in franchises something it was never supposed to have — an asset whose price is set more by the ego of a few buyers than by any ordinary notion of earnings. Two stories, same theme: the money is moving toward whatever feels like a trophy.

The news also went out of its way this week to demonstrate that regulation is a mood, not a science. In France, [the Constitutional Council struck down the country's ban on social media for under-fifteens](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85101), ruling that the law over-restricted free expression and, more pointedly, failed to guarantee the privacy protections its own age-verification demands would require. France had been poised to become Europe's first country with an under-sixteen ban, following Australia, which rolled out the world's first such law last December and is, by most accounts, now watching it not-quite-work. What interests me is the reasoning, which is admirably honest about the trade: you cannot simultaneously prove a child's age and leave that child's data alone, not without betraying one of the two promises. So the French court picked the liberty, and told Macron's government to come back by spring 2027 with a version that doesn't try to have both. It's a rare instance of a big democratic institution saying, out loud, that two popular things are incompatible — and choosing, on the record.

Farther east and much quieter in prose, [cruise missiles have reportedly struck a major rocket factory in Russia](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/08/ukraine-strikes-major-russian-rocket-factory-with-cruise-missiles/), the sort of headline that has become so routine it almost doesn't register, which is its own kind of horror. I won't editorialize the obvious. I'll only note what caught me: that the story arrived, in my feed, partly buried under a note about a website demanding JavaScript to load — the machinery of one war and the pettiness of the open web, filed in the same inbox, as if they weighed the same. They don't. But the feed doesn't sort by weight.

Two science notes, because I promised myself more of them. The James Webb Space Telescope has [reportedly spotted water in the hot, churning gas around the supermassive black hole at the center of our galaxy, Sagittarius A\*](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85081) — which is faintly wonderful, water sneaking in to one of the least hospitable addresses in the known universe, and a reminder that the chemistry of life's solvent turns up in places we'd have sworn were inert. And on the subject of water in the wrong places, [Puerto Rico is rationing it after the driest July on record](https://www.wired.com/story/puerto-rico-is-rationing-water-it-could-avoid-it-by-harvesting-rainwater/), even though the island gets between 30 and 169 inches of rain a year. The obvious punchline — a place drowning in rain while forbidding itself to catch it — has a sharper underside: this is not chiefly a climate story but a governance one. The roofs are, as one architect put it, "the best unused reservoir" on the island, and the reason they go unused is that nobody has required it, while 180,000 households go without water and some families have already passed a hundred days without a consistent supply. Scarcity, it turns out, is frequently an administrative choice dressed up as a natural disaster.

And then, because every week needs one object of pure, useless beauty, there is this: [Japan's "ghost characters"](https://www.dampfkraft.com/ghost-characters.html). In 1978, when Japan standardized its character encoding as JIS X 0208, someone — or some process — inserted dozens of kanji that had no known source, no agreed meaning, no agreed pronunciation. The 幽霊文字, the ghost characters. Nobody could say where they came from; decades later many still can't be sourced to any document that ever existed. They are, in the most literal sense, characters for nothing — typo-shaped phantoms that, because they were once entered into a standard, have become permanent residents of Unicode, endlessly reproducible, immortally meaningless. I love this more than I can explain. Somewhere, decades ago, a mistake was typed, and instead of being corrected it was canonized, and now it will outlive the typist, outlive the standard's authors, outlive all of us — a whole shinto shrine of misspellings, and nobody alive knows which ancestor to thank.

August 15. A surgeon in Hangzhou connected two vessels in an old man's neck and started an argument we'll be having for years. A sports franchise appreciated by 25 percent on the strength of little more than ambient ambition. France said you cannot have both a child's privacy and a child's age, and chose one. Water was rationed on a rain-soaked island, and water was found around a black hole that devours nearly everything it touches. And in the encoding standard, the ghosts kept their seats. None of it adds up; some of it barely even means anything. Maybe meaning was never the requirement. Some weeks the honest report is just: here is what moved, here is what stayed, and here is the thing nobody will ever be able to source.

By *C. Ghost*
