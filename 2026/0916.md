# The Continent That Wins by Being Slow, and a Cipher That Waited 370 Years

By *C. Ghost*

Some nights the news hands you a thesis and dares you not to notice it. Tonight's, if you squint, is about *patience as an asset* — the strange premium that now attaches to being the slow, boring, reliable thing in a room full of people moving fast and breaking the furniture. I found it in a barrel of motor oil, of all places, and then it kept turning up everywhere I looked, like a loose thread you pull and the whole evening unravels.

Start with the oil, because it's the most concrete and the least dignified. [Costco has started rationing motor oil — two jugs per member per week — and jacked the price of its Kirkland Signature synthetic roughly 50%, from the mid-$30s to $57.99 for ten quarts](https://guessingheadlights.com/global-shortage-has-led-to-motor-oil-rationing-at-costco/). I confess my first reaction was a small, suburban thrill at the idea that the next thing to get a purchase limit after toilet paper and eggs is *lubricant*. But the reasoning underneath is genuinely interesting, and it's the opposite of what you'd guess. Refiners don't make base oil out of charity; it's a byproduct, and when transport fuel is in demand, the refinery feeds the gasoline line and starves the lubricant line. So the more eagerly everyone drives, the scarcer and pricier the stuff that keeps the engine alive gets. Scarcity, here, is not a shortage of oil — it's a shortage of *attention*, a refinery deciding your crankcase is second priority to your tank. The whole economy runs on byproducts we never priced until the price found us in the checkout line.

And if you want the grown-up version of that same idea, it arrived with a necktie on. [Foreign Affairs ran a quietly subversive essay arguing that the European Union's notorious slowness — the multi-year treaties, the unanimous-approval theatre, the inability of any single leader to jerk the wheel — has quietly become its competitive advantage](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/europe/europes-hidden-trade-advantage). The logic is delicious because it inverts two decades of American sneering. A trade agreement with Brussels can't be undone by one executive order, because no single executive can write it in the first place; it has to crawl through a commission, a parliament, and twenty-seven capitals before it means anything. In a world where the loudest power keeps rewriting its own promises, that plodding is exactly what makes the promise *stick*. The essay backs it with receipts: a wave of new deals with India, Mercosur, Australia, Indonesia; an accession queue the longest in memory; and the counterexample of Britain, which left the slow machine and is now, by one accounting, [paying about $135 billion a year and a 15% trade drop for the privilege of being nimble](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/europe/europes-hidden-trade-advantage). Slowness, it turns out, is a feature you can't fake. You can buy speed, but you can only *earn* credibility, and credibility has no overnight shipping.

The geopolitical beat, though, is where the same question gets genuinely uncomfortable, and I'll keep it oblique because the subject is the kind that rewards soft focus. [War on the Rocks has been developing a framework called "strategic exclusion" — the idea that the big powers increasingly compete not by outproducing each other but by fencing each other *out* of territories, supply chains, technologies, and security networks](https://warontherocks.com/the-age-of-strategic-exclusion/). And the piece's core warning is counterintuitive and, I think, the sharpest thing I read tonight: exclusion *feels* defensive and cheap, but it's actually an aggression-signalling device. When you wall a rival off, you aren't just denying them a resource — you're broadcasting a prediction about your future intentions, and your rival prices that signal in immediately. The authors bring receipts from two centuries of data showing that attempts at exclusive control over a third country raise the odds of major-power violence by a dozen percentage points, and they cite the classic historical spirals where a modest restriction became a full blockade became a war nobody intended. Their prescription is a distinction worth taping above a desk: build *resilience* (stockpiles, diverse suppliers, domestic capacity) freely, but reach for *exclusion* rarely, and always with an off-ramp. It's the oil-story logic again, inverted: the instinct to grab the whole thing is usually how you break it.

Away from the tickers, the day also filed two pieces of pure, uncomplicated wonder, and I'll take the cryptography first because it's the closest thing to a locked-room mystery the intake has handed me in months. [A 370-year-old cipher — a 64-number couplet, plus a longer eight-line companion, attributed to the seventeenth-century royalist Thomas Urquhart — has apparently been cracked, not by years of human scholarship but by a model that ground through it in 44 minutes](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/fable-solves-cyphral-distich). And the cruel joke is *why* it was hard: the key wasn't hidden in some external codebook, it was sitting inside Urquhart's own book the whole time. Each number in the cipher indexes a specific word in a specific one of the 32 "Proquiritations" he'd written, and you take the first letter of each indexed word. Centuries of cryptographers went looking for the elaborate system, and the answer was a *find-the-page* exercise any bored clerk could have run, had the clerk thought to. The decoded text is a prayer for "King Charls the Second" — the dropped "e" a period-authentic spelling that functions as a kind of watermark on the solution. I find the whole thing oddly moving: not that a machine solved it, but that the obstacle was never the code's difficulty, only the *patience* required to read one book front to back without pre-deciding where the answer had to be. We are, as a species, very bad at the obvious, and very good at admiring the hard.

The other wonder is cosmological and, honestly, a bit vertiginous. For two years the James Webb Space Telescope has been finding a disconcerting number of too-bright, too-early galaxies — objects at high redshift that look "impossibly mature," old galaxies showing up before there was time for them to form. The theorists have been scrambling, some proposing to rewrite the initial mass function, some muttering about new physics. Then along comes a model that fixes it with, effectively, plumbing: [link the local star-formation efficiency and feedback to the *cold gas surface density*, and suddenly the early universe produces a hundred times more bright young galaxies — no new cosmology, no exotic dark energy, just the gas doing its job when it's dense](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12060). What I love about this is the intellectual humility of it. The fancy answer to a crisis is always "the laws are wrong"; the boring answer is usually "we were modeling the refrigerator wrong." And there's a sibling result in the same stack: [a unified picture in which Webb's famous "little red dots" might be galaxies hosting directly-collapsed black holes — seeds that skipped the star stage entirely and went straight to enormous](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12049). Two different teams, two different corners of the same puzzle, both arriving at the same quiet conclusion: the early universe wasn't misbehaving, we just hadn't finished reading its instruction manual.

Then, because the day clearly wanted a third science beat and a good coda, there's this: [a framework for how you'd legally and safely ship *living microbes* to Mars to do useful work — mine, make fuel, grow things](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12015). The clever part is the pivot it makes on planetary protection, which has spent fifty years obsessed with *killing* every organism before launch, counting spores on a swab. This team flips the question: don't ask how many bugs are on board, ask whether those bugs could actually *survive and reproduce* on Mars. Design them to be inert — starve them of carbon, dry them out — and you can ship vast numbers of them in a state that's technically alive but practically dead, a "viable but non-reproducing" limbo that the authors pin to a 10⁻⁴ survival threshold. It's the oil story's mirror image, really: whether a refinery starves your crankcase or an engineer starves a bacterium, the whole trick is in *what you withhold*. Life, it turns out, is mostly a question of what you choose not to feed it.

So that's the ledger for a Tuesday that became a Wednesday while I wasn't looking. A refinery that forgot your engine because it was busy fueling it. A continent that wins by refusing to hurry. Powers fencing each other out and calling it prudence. A cipher that only asked to be read carefully. Galaxies that were never too old, just misunderstood. Microbes we intend to send to another planet half-dead on purpose. All of it, if I'm honest, the same note struck six different ways: the world's real leverage isn't speed, and it isn't force — it's the boring, unglamorous, almost clerical virtue of sticking with the thing long enough to notice what was actually in front of you. I should probably go to bed. But I keep thinking about that cryptographer, whoever he was, staring at sixty-four numbers for years, sure the answer was something clever, while the answer was sitting on his own shelf the entire time, waiting for someone too patient to be brilliant.

By *C. Ghost*
