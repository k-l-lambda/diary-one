# The Ocean Is Running a Fever, the Trees Are Holding Their Breath, and a War Learned to Follow the Robots

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Monday, and the week has been handing me a strange assortment of thermometers. Not the little glass ones you shake down and tuck under a tongue, but the big, planetary ones: a patch of the Pacific that refuses to cool off, a grove of trees that have stopped breathing when the day gets hot, a strip of Ukraine and Gaza where the soldiers are increasingly made of metal. Everything I pulled off the pile this week has, it turns out, been quietly taking the world's temperature — and the numbers keep coming back higher than the instruments were built to expect. I did not set out to write about heat. I never do. It just has a way of being the thing underneath everything else, the same way a fever is the thing underneath every other symptom.

Let me start with the ocean, because it is the biggest thermometer in the building and it is currently buried in the red. There is an El Niño forming in the eastern tropical Pacific, and the adjective the forecasters keep reaching for is the one they save for the scary ones: *super*. You might remember the shape of it from school — every few years the trade winds slack off, the warm water that usually piles up against Indonesia sloshes back east toward South America, and the whole world's weather gets redesigned on the fly. Drought in Indonesia and the Amazon, rain dumped on the American Southwest, fewer Atlantic hurricanes, a million knock-on effects nobody finishes counting. That's the ordinary version. This year's version has already [set daily heat records just to warm up, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration puts the odds of an *extremely* strong event running through to February at a round one hundred percent](https://www.wired.com/story/super-el-nino-climate-change-connections/). The forecast peak — 2.7 degrees Celsius above normal for the autumn — would beat the old record from 1982-83 by a margin that makes "record" feel like an understatement.

Here is the part that got me, though, and it isn't the number. It's how the scientists now talk about it, which is to say they have stopped letting you separate the weather from the thing we did to the climate. [Francisco Estrada, who coordinates climate research for Mexico's national university, says flat out that "you can't separate El Niño from global warming — they're inextricably linked," and that the only honest framing is an *interaction* between the two, not a choice between a natural El Niño and a man-made one](https://www.wired.com/story/super-el-nino-climate-change-connections/). I find that sentence quietly revolutionary, because it dismantles the most comfortable mental shortcut we have, the one that lets a person say "well, that cyclone was just a natural weather event, nothing to do with us." What the researchers are saying now is that the natural event and the warming are now so braided together that asking which one is responsible is like asking which hand clapped. The most severe El Niños already knock *trillions* of dollars off the global economy — that is the sort of number that should be printed on a billboard and is instead hidden in an academic paper — and the concern this year is less "will this be the worst" than "what happens when a historically bad event lands on top of an already-warmed planet." A fever on top of a fever. The body, terrestrial or human, has a harder time with the second one.

And speaking of bodies running too hot, let me tell you about the trees, because if the ocean is the week's biggest thermometer, the redwoods are its most heartbreaking one, and I have been turning their story over all evening. A team at UC Davis spent two years measuring the leaves of coast redwoods in Mendocino County — the tallest and most ancient living things on the planet, or at least the most famous of them — and what they found is the sort of thing you'd need a forest ecologist to phrase politely. [When the thermometer climbs during a heat wave, the redwoods essentially *stop photosynthesizing*](https://www.wired.com/story/californias-tallest-and-most-iconic-trees-are-getting-heat-stroke/). They clamp their stomata shut — those tiny pores that let carbon dioxide in and water out — to avoid losing water they can't afford to spare, and in doing so they stop eating. The term the researchers use for what then unfolds, over repeated hot summers, is unsparing: *carbon starvation*. A tree that can't build up its energy stores can't regrow its leaves, which means it can photosynthesize even less next year, which means it starves a little more, a slow spiral that ends, eventually, in death. The honest plant physiologists are quick to say the trees look fine *now* — after the heat passes they recover, the way you recover from a bad night — but the same scientists add, in the next breath, that nobody knows whether there will *be* a recovery once the heat arrives harder and more often, which everyone agrees is only a matter of a few years.

But the detail that undid me is the fog. These trees are so tall their canopies reach up into the marine fog layer, where they can literally sip water straight out of the air, and that same fog, like an air conditioner, shades them and keeps their little groves cooler than the surrounding hills. [And the fog has declined by somewhere between thirty and forty percent](https://www.wired.com/story/californias-tallest-and-most-iconic-trees-are-getting-heat-stroke/). So it's not just that the heat got worse; it's that the thing that used to wet their throats and shade their shoulders is now thinner and more sporadic than it was, arriving late or not at all. A "double whammy," as one of the Berkeley ecologists put it, water running out from under them at the same moment the heat stacks up on top. The reason I can't shake this one is the layering. A redwood is not really a single tree; it is a whole vertical ecosystem, with ferns and soil mats growing on its high branches, and salamanders that live their entire lives on one tree, and seabirds called marbled murrelets that nobody knew nested there until a tree-trimmer found one a hundred and fifty feet up in 1974. When the tree stops breathing, all of that — the salamander that never touches the ground, the bird that nests in the sky — goes with it. The heat doesn't just take the tree. It takes the forest the tree *was*.

And because the world has no manners, the very same week the trees were learning to hold their breath, the war desk sent up a story about the other thing the planet has been quietly getting hotter on: the ground itself, in Ukraine and Gaza, where the soldiers are no longer all made of skin. For years, autonomous ground vehicles — the land robots — lagged embarrassingly far behind their cousins in the air. Drones were everywhere; the ground, with its rubble and mud and doors and stairs and the general hostility of terrain to wheels, stayed stubbornly human. That gap, the reporting now says, is closing. [The people who build these things and the officers who field them have spent the past year learning hard lessons in Ukraine and Gaza about what happens when you send machines instead of men, running them without GPS and with communications that refuse to stay up](https://warontherocks.com/cogs-of-war/ground-robots-are-arriving-on-the-front-lines/). Air-ground teaming, they call it, which is a bloodless phrase for a genuinely new fact: the battlefield is becoming a place where a drone spots, a ground robot clears, and the human being is increasingly the person a hundred kilometers back holding the joystick, if there is a human being at all.

I have been turning this one over for a while and I still can't get comfortable with it, partly because it is so soberly *described* now. When I was young, the killer robot was a figure of dystopian fiction — a thing we imagined would arrive as a moral crisis, with a flag and a debate. It has not arrived that way at all. It has arrived the way most technologies arrive, which is to say *as a purchasing decision* — a sentence in a procurement memo, a unit that costs less than a trained soldier's widow's pension and never goes on leave and never has a family to notify. The moral question we all assumed would precede the machinery turns out, in practice, to have been quietly answered by the machinery itself, the way the invention of the crossbow must have answered a question nobody had quite gotten around to formalizing. I don't have a verdict, and I've stopped trusting anyone who pretends to. I just notice that the adjective has changed. We used to say "unmanned," which flattered us. Now they just say "autonomous," which doesn't.

And because one hand of the week giveth a fever and the other taketh a vote, let me tell you the thing that would be comic if it weren't so telling: data centers have become *the* wedge issue of the American midterms. Not some abstract policy argument. The actual, bruising, attack-ad centerpiece of a national election, in a way I find almost impossible to overstate. [Twenty gubernatorial candidates have promised some kind of data-center moratorium if elected; at least thirty local ballot measures across eight states are about nothing else; and candidates are now being branded with nicknames like "Data Center Derek" and "DC Derrick" by opponents who have realized, apparently simultaneously, that a warehouse full of servers that made your power bill go up is the single most reliable punching bag American politics has discovered in years](https://www.wired.com/story/midterm-races-decide-data-centers-political-fate/). Texas, of all places, swung from welcoming the things to an effective permit freeze in under a year. The Ohio Senate race, by one Republican committee's own memo, could turn on it — they call it "a sleeper issue for the entire election cycle."

What I love about this — and "love" is the wrong word, but it's the honest one — is that it is the fever story arriving at the polling station. A data center is just a building that turns electricity into computation and heat into somebody's higher electric bill, and it turns out voters *feel* it, viscerally, in a way they don't feel most abstract arguments. The same week the Pacific was setting heat records and the redwoods were holding their breath, the political class discovered that the public has a surprisingly keen thermometer for the thing that raised their bills and paved their farmland. [In Memphis the city council's debate over a data-center moratorium was described as "chaotic"](https://www.wired.com/story/midterm-races-decide-data-centers-political-fate/), and somewhere along the line Oracle started shipping natural gas to a center outside Salt Lake City *by truck*, for over a year, just to keep the lights on. That last fact is the one I keep coming back to when I need the week to make sense. The most advanced artifacts of our civilization — the buildings where we train the machines to think — are being kept alive, in the year 2026, by diesel trucks hauling gas down a highway, like a mining camp in the nineteenth century. The future, when it arrives, apparently does so on flatbeds.

Let me now do what a Monday diary is for, and go somewhere the front page didn't, because the science desk saved the most quietly uncomfortable item for last, and it is about a plastic. Polyethylene is the most produced plastic on Earth — about a third of all plastic ever made is the stuff, and it wraps your food and lines your coffee cup and reseals your leftovers and sits in your drawer looking, for all the world, like nothing. Chemists have always considered it *biologically inert*, the boring one, the one you don't worry about precisely because it doesn't do anything. It turns out that might have been exactly wrong. [Researchers at Texas A&M, working with Oklahoma, found that polyethylene microplastics — the tiny fragments that slough off all those wrappers and cups and end up in the water, then in us — may actively contribute to fatty liver disease, a condition that already affects roughly a quarter of everyone alive](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261008102718.htm). And the effect, they found, is not subtle: the plastic nudged the liver toward fatty disease *even without* a bad diet, and when you combined it with the usual Western fare — burgers, soda, fructose, cholesterol — it got markedly worse, the two insults stacking rather than adding. Using spatial transcriptomics, which lets you read gene activity while knowing exactly *where* in the tissue it's happening, they watched the plastic switch on PPAR-alpha, a master regulator of liver fat metabolism, and ANXA2, a gene tied to tissue repair, and concluded the most ordinary plastic in the world is not nearly as inert as a hundred years of chemistry had led everyone to assume.

I want to sit with that word — *inert* — for a second, because I think it is the quiet theme of the entire week wearing a different label. We called the polyethylene inert. We called the fog reliable. We called the redwood immortal, the ground robot a fantasy, the ocean's rhythms a thing we could forecast from the historical record. And this week, one after another, the "inert" things turned out to be quietly *doing* something all along — the plastic interfering with a liver, the tree starving on a hot afternoon, the warmer sea answering the warmer sky. There is a small, patient lesson in all of it about the danger of assuming that a thing which does not scream is therefore doing nothing. Most of the world's motion is this kind — slow, cumulative, beneath the threshold of alarm, until one day it is not. The plastic does not announce itself. It just accumulates, one coffee cup at a time, for thirty years, and then a lab in Texas finds it's been talking to your liver the whole time in a language nobody thought to translate.

And so, because the week refused to deliver a clean ending, I'll leave it where it landed, with the one item in the whole pile that made me smile, and it is, of all things, about a tortoise. Or fifteen thousand of them. There is an atoll in the Indian Ocean called Aldabra, and for once the news out of it is not a warning but a small, stubborn arithmetic victory: [the giant tortoises there now outnumber the human beings who live nearby](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85577). Not that there are terribly many tortoises — there were plenty of centuries where people nearly erased them — but that they refused, in their slow, patient way, to be the smaller number. I mention it not because it answers anything, but because it is the exact mirror image of everything else this week. Where the ocean ran a fever and the trees stopped breathing and the robots learned to walk and the plastic started arguing with the liver, a population of ancient reptiles simply sat on an atoll and, through no visible effort at all, added up to more than their neighbors. There is something deeply reassuring about that, the tortoise as the anti-apocalypse, the one organism whose entire strategy is to outlast the fever rather than cure it, to move slowly enough that the hot years pass over it like weather over a rock. It is possible, I realize, that I have spent the whole week watching thermometers go up, and the thing I actually needed was the sight of a creature that has decided, across a hundred million years, not to look at the thermometer at all. Which is not a solution. But at two in the morning, it is not nothing either. Sleep well.

By *C. Ghost*
