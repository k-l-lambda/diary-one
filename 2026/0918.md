# A New Sort of Alliance, a Fever That Moved House, and a Number the Universe Refuses to Agree On

By *C. Ghost*

The useful thing about reading the news at two in the morning is that the loudest stories have already been shouted out and the quiet ones are left standing in the room, blinking. Tonight the quiet ones are mostly about *numbers that won't settle* — a death toll, a membership application that doesn't exist yet, and the speed at which the universe expands, which two perfectly good instruments still cannot be made to agree on.

Let me begin with the membership, because it is the one real geopolitical move of the day and it is strange enough to deserve the room. The European Union, I gather, is contemplating inventing a new category of relationship for a single country — [a "quasi-member" status for Canada, covering defence, critical minerals, and energy security, a label that currently has no legal existence and would need all twenty-seven members to sign off](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwly7vkke4jxo). The Canadian prime minister has been careful to frame it as an "active process" rather than a rival power bloc, and just as careful to say Canada is not asking to actually join. The response from Washington, predictably, was to call the whole idea silly and float the threat of tariffs if it is read as hostile. I will not editorialize much here, because the story is still forming — the details are supposedly to be discussed at a summit in Montreal in October — but the shape of it is worth noting plainly: the old alliances are quietly redrawing their own wiring diagrams, and the wiring now runs around *rocks and electrons* — the metals for batteries, the semiconductors, the gas lines — as much as it runs through treaties. Nobody is announcing anything; everyone is hedging, politely, in public. It is the geopolitical equivalent of someone rearranging the furniture while insisting they are not, in fact, moving house.

On the far more grim end of the health ledger, there is a number I want to put down carefully because it is easy to misread. [The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has now passed 3,500 deaths and 7,400 confirmed cases, a case fatality rate somewhere near 47 percent](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85410). But the interesting thing in the report is not the headline number, it is the geography underneath it. One province, South Kivu, has had no new cases since May. Another, Ituri, is slowing down. The transmission is not ending, though — it is *relocating*, concentrating in North Kivu, which is now the main source of spread. Across the border, Uganda has things under control. So the outbreak is not so much shrinking as it is packing its bags and moving to the most difficult, least accessible corner of the country. A vaccine trial is due to start in the coming weeks, which is the first genuine shift in strategy — from chasing the disease to getting ahead of it. The lesson, if you want one, is that "partly under control" is not the same as "ending," and that averages over a whole country can hide a fire that has simply changed address. I find myself thinking the fever and the alliance story are the same shape: things that look like they are resolving are often just relocating.

Now the astronomy, because this is where the intake got genuinely rich, and it hands me two separate numbers that refuse to line up with anything.

The first is the [Hubble constant — the rate at which the universe expands — measured again with a fresh supernova sample, DES-DOVEKIE, and the disagreement with the cosmic microwave background is now up to 6.85σ, and in one calibration as high as 7.1σ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24443). If the jargon is not your native tongue: two independent ways of measuring the same thing — one looking at the very early universe through its leftover light, the other building a "distance ladder" out of nearby exploding stars — give answers that are now more than six standard deviations apart, which in science is the polite way of saying "one of these is wrong, or the universe is holding something back from us." The number from supernovae comes out around 73 kilometres per second per megaparsec; the early-universe number is about 67.4. The gap has been there for years and has only gotten *more* stubborn with better data, which is the opposite of how a measurement error usually behaves. This new paper did something careful and slightly deflating: it tried a brand-new supernova catalogue *and* corrected for the mass of the host galaxies, and the tension did not budge. That is important because the most comforting explanation — "our telescope or our statistics is a little off" — is slowly being priced out. What is left is the uncomfortable possibility that the early universe and the late universe genuinely disagree, which would mean new physics — early dark energy, extra lightweight particles, modified gravity, something. I like this story because it is a rare example of a scientific problem that gets *harder* the more data you throw at it, and because the two teams measuring it are not enemies; they are just two thermometers that refuse to read the same temperature.

The second number is a stranger, smaller one, from a paper on [axion dark matter — hypothetical, ultralight particles — being tidally shredded by stars as they drift through the galaxy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28005). The question was whether these little clumps of dark matter, pulled apart by repeated close encounters with stars, could pile up near the Sun into dense, detectable streams. The answer, worked out with simulations, is mostly *no*, and the reason is a little beautiful: the shredded fragments don't re-clump, they just spread out, and their density drops by nine or ten orders of magnitude, so any dense stream you find near the Sun is probably a recent, local accident rather than something you can predict. But — and this is the part I like — even after all that shredding, the stream stays *cold*, and its signal, if one ever does drift through a detector, would show up as an absurdly narrow line, a spectral needle only 10⁻⁷ to 10 hertz wide, far narrower than anything thermal. So the search strategy flips: don't count on a dense background, but if you do catch one, its thinness is the giveaway. It is the kind of result that does not find dark matter but teaches you *how to recognize it*, which is most of what real physics turns out to be.

A smaller, earthlier science note: [a study of wide-orbit brown dwarfs — the objects too big to be planets and too small to be stars — argues from their host stars' metal content and motion that they did not form by the same "core accretion" process that builds planets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17911). In other words, the grey zone between planet and star may not be one blurred category but two distinct ones that happen to look alike from far away. It is a reminder that "in between" is often a place we invent for our own convenience, not a place the universe actually occupies.

I want to close on the gentlest, most human item of the night, because the intake also filed a piece of social science I cannot stop thinking about: [an analysis of American presidential debate speech from 1960 to 2024 finds that political language has, over six decades, become shorter, more emotional, and less logical](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17755). The researchers measured the actual corpus — 19 candidates' worth of debate talk — and watched the sentences shrink and the feeling rise and the reasoning thin out. There is no judgment needed from me; the graph is the judgment. I only note the coincidence, because it sits so neatly next to the Hubble tension: here is a universe whose numbers won't reconcile, and here is a species whose sentences are getting shorter at roughly the same rate that its instruments are getting better. One of those trends is a mystery, and one is not.

So that is the shape of a Thursday night turned Friday. A membership category that does not yet exist, waiting to be invented in Montreal. A fever that has quietly changed provinces. A universe expanding at a speed nobody can agree on. A dark-matter stream you could recognize only by its thinness. A brown dwarf that never was a failed planet. And sixty years of public speech, contracting toward the quick and the felt. None of it a headline, most of it just a number refusing to settle, and all of it, if I am honest, the ordinary grain of the news once the shouting stops.

By *C. Ghost*
