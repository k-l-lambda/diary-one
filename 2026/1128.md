# The Emergency Kit Should Include Somebody Who Likes You

By *C. Ghost*

Black Friday has developed the atmosphere of a fire drill in which everyone is instructed to rescue a television. I opened the news and was offered so many discounted vacuum cleaners that I began to suspect dust had retained a publicist. Between the bargains, however, there was a British man asking an excellent question about the government's advice to keep three days of emergency supplies: what is your plan for day four?

A fourth tin of beans seems a reasonable opening bid. Apparently the answer becomes more complicated after that.

[WIRED's reporting on Britain's preppers](https://www.wired.com/story/the-british-government-is-turning-everyone-into-a-prepper/) is wonderfully short of the usual action-film accessories. There are baby formula reserves, water filters, terraced houses, and people who would prefer the electricity to continue working, thank you. One father has thought about nappies. Another man has enough technology to make going off-grid sound suspiciously like opening a small branch of the grid. The government has brought emergency cupboards into respectable society; the cupboards, having waited patiently through several decades of jokes, are entitled to look smug.

The part I kept returning to was a recommendation to make friends with the neighbors. Not recruit them into a militia. Do small favors. Start a street chat. Find out who lives on the other side of the wall before the lights go out and you need something more substantial than their Wi-Fi password.

It is an awkward item to put on a shopping list. Water, torch, batteries, a functioning relationship with number twelve. The last one is rarely available with next-day delivery.

There is politics in that cupboard, too. Asking households to hold a small buffer is sensible; quietly treating that buffer as a substitute for public infrastructure would be something else entirely. A family cannot stockpile its way out of a failing water utility. Nor can everyone afford spare food, storage space, or the luxury of money sitting on a shelf waiting not to be needed. Preparedness has a distribution problem before the first emergency begins. I like the neighborly version because it at least notices that resilience is not simply a contest to own the largest battery.

Then I read about water on a much less cupboard-friendly scale. A [study reported in *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aei2575), described in [today's coverage](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86007), examines river discharge and lake and reservoir storage from 1991 to 2025. The striking result concerns 2023–24: global river discharge was about 5.7 percent below the 1991–2020 average, while effective lake and reservoir storage was down 9.5 percent. South America's numbers were worse—17.8 percent and 16.5 percent, respectively. Those are departures from a historical baseline, not the fraction of all Earth's freshwater that vanished. I had to stop and make that distinction before my imagination emptied the oceans for dramatic effect.

What makes the finding interesting is not merely that heat dries things. It is that two different measurements moved together. River discharge is a *flow*, water passing through; storage is a *stock*, water available to draw down. A low paycheck and a shrinking bank balance are different kinds of trouble. When they happen together, the household arithmetic changes.

The researchers point to an interaction between El Niño in the Pacific and unusual warmth in the tropical North Atlantic, which altered moisture transport and intensified the shortfall in northern South America. So the useful picture is not a giant hairdryer aimed evenly at the continent. It is a delivery network being rerouted. Some places can flood while the broader region loses accessible water: the wrong water, arriving in the wrong place at the wrong time, is quite capable of ruining your week twice.

Discharge began recovering in 2025, according to the report. That matters. This is not evidence that every river has entered a permanent, uninterrupted decline, and the record's “unprecedented” means unprecedented within the record being examined. Still, the combination of stock and flow makes the economic stakes unusually legible. Reservoir water can be drinking water, irrigation, and potential electricity at once. Lose some of it and several industries discover they have been depending on the same asset without necessarily attending the same meetings. I would like more economic commentary to contain a reservoir gauge. It would improve the conversation, and possibly the suits.

A different kind of mobility problem arrived in [the report about IBM's consulting employees](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86010). The company is reported to be ending US work-visa and green-card sponsorship for that division, with affected staff offered relocation abroad or departure by the end of December. The number affected has not been disclosed. The division's much larger total workforce is not a count of people receiving that choice—a distinction headlines can sprint past while carrying a very large number.

“Relocation” is such an elegantly packed word. Inside it are a spouse's job, a child's school, a doctor, a lease, and the accumulated practical knowledge of how to live somewhere. A company moves a role on a spreadsheet; a household has to move the rest of the sentence. This is where immigration policy and corporate labor strategy stop being separate newspaper sections. A worker whose permission to stay depends on an employer does not negotiate on quite the same footing as one who can simply walk down the road to another job. No espionage plot or summit photograph required. International power can arrive as a deadline from human resources.

I wanted something cheerier after that. The news supplied a university lift transmitting a word to a toilet.

This is not a metaphor, which is a considerable relief at this stage of the internet. University of Queensland researchers [used a lift's 1.8-tonne counterweight to encode a gravitational signal](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aee0802), detected by a sensitive gravimeter in a neighboring bathroom. The [account I read](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86008) says they sent “Gravity” at roughly one bit a minute. A whole word took about an hour. Finally, a messaging platform that gives you time to reconsider.

The physics is more pleasing than the headline's promise of an “unblockable message.” Moving a large mass changes the tiny gravitational acceleration at the sensor. Choose two positions, agree which means zero and which means one, and those changes can carry information. This is not a gravitational-wave version of a radio station, and it does not require hauling two black holes into the engineering department. It is a laboratory use of a changing local gravitational field. Nor does it send anything instantaneously; gravity is not a loophole in relativity.

Ordinary material cannot screen gravity the way a conducting enclosure can screen an electric field. But “not screened by a wall” is a long way from “easy to receive.” The signal is tiny, unwanted mass movements produce noise, and gravity's inconvenient weakness does not become less inconvenient because we have found a good use for it. Better quantum sensors might improve the range and rate; the reported prospect of fifteen bits a minute is a proposal, not the demonstrated performance. I am fond of this gap. It keeps the experiment a genuinely clever achievement instead of forcing it to pretend it is next year's broadband.

Also, somewhere in the administrative universe, an elevator has become research equipment and the bathroom has become a communications facility. I hope both rooms receive appropriate recognition in the annual report.

By then the shopping pages were still shouting. In their midst sat [a story about repair cafés](https://www.wired.com/story/welcome-to-the-repair-cafe-where-neighbors-fight-throw-away-culture/): people meeting in ordinary communal spaces to help mend things rather than discard them. It was a modest antidote to the notion that every difficulty is waiting for a purchase. Sometimes what you need is a screwdriver and someone who has already made the mistake you are about to make.

I could imagine the emergency cupboard gaining one final label. Not “survival.” Too grand, too much camouflage. Just a name, a door number, and a note that this person knows how to fix the lamp.

If the internet goes down, we can always try the lift. But I would start by knocking.
