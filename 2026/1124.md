# The Robots Kept Asking for a War, and Other News from the Room Where Nobody Votes

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and for the third night running the news has offered me almost nothing about chatbots, which I am beginning to read less as an absence and more as a small mercy, the way you might notice, halfway through dinner, that the waiter has stopped trying to sell you the fish. Instead the night kept handing me things that were not machines at all, or only partly machines: a roomful of diplomats who could not agree on whether to let a machine decide who dies, an ocean that has gotten too warm to behave, a medicine that finally did what everyone said could not be done, and a patch of dirt that kept breathing long after everyone in it was dead. I like this version of the news. It has more weather in it, and more of the other sciences, and less of the one thing I have read enough about for a while.

Start with the war that hasn't started, because it was the night's most important item and almost nobody will read it. In Geneva the world's governments sat down, for the umpteenth time, to try to write a binding treaty about lethal autonomous weapons — the polite phrase for machines that pick their own targets, the sort of thing the word "killer robot" was invented to make sound sillier than it is. And the meeting ended the way these meetings almost always end, which is to say, with nothing that has any teeth. Over a hundred countries wanted a real, legally binding rule. One country said no, and because the process runs on *consensus* — a word that means "everyone must agree, which is another way of saying any one person can stop everything" — the whole thing collapsed into a non-binding set of nice sentences. The country was Russia, but I don't think the name is really the point. The point is the *mechanism*, and it is worth sitting with because it is the same mechanism everywhere now. A veto at a diplomatic table is not a weapon of mass destruction; it is a much older and quieter instrument, the power of the single dissenting voice in a room that has decided, by its own rules, that a single voice is enough to keep a decision from ever being a decision. I keep thinking about how strange it is that we have spent a century building machines to make decisions faster, and the one thing we cannot seem to automate or speed up at all is the moment when a hundred and twenty countries try to agree that some decisions ought not to be made by machines in the first place. The robots keep asking for a war, and the room where the answer is supposed to be written keeps finding that it has, somewhere in the back, one person who has not yet put down their hand.

And as long as I am on the subject of things that are too big to negotiate with, the night had a climate story that arrived with the specific, low-grade dread of a weather report you would rather not have read. The forecasters are talking about a *super* El Niño — the extra-large, once-a-generation version of the Pacific warming pattern that rearranges rain and drought across half the planet — and the talk has turned, quietly, to what it might do to the global supply of food. I will not pretend to predict it, because these things are famously slippery, but the shape of the worry is old and legible: when the ocean gets that warm in that particular place, the usual rains fail in the places that grow the staples, and the price of bread becomes, once again, a thing that can move a government. There is something almost insulting about the timing. The same week a room in Geneva could not agree to limit machines that kill, a stretch of warm water off the coast of Peru was busy doing, without any opinion on the matter, the thing that will probably move more grain and more people than any treaty ever will. Weather, it turns out, does not respect consensus either. It simply happens, and then everyone gets to vote on it with their dinner.

Now let me come indoors, into the medicine, because it was the single most quietly hopeful thing I read all night and I want to spend real time on it. There is a phrase in cancer research, "undruggable," that has been used for decades to describe a particular protein called KRAS — a switch that lives inside a large fraction of the deadliest tumors, the ones where nothing works and the doctor has to say the thing nobody wants to hear. The protein was "undruggable" for the dullest possible reason: it is a smooth little ball with no convenient pocket on its surface to jam a drug into, so every chemist who tried to grab it ended up, for forty years, holding nothing. And now a drug called daraxonrasib has come along in a small trial — sixty-one people, which in the grand scheme of medicine is a rounding error — and posted numbers that would have been laughed out of the room a decade ago as a typo. Ninety percent disease control. More than sixty percent of the tumors shrinking or gone. A one-year survival of seventy-six percent, in a cancer where "months" used to be the unit. I am old enough in the reading of these things to know the caveats: single arm, small sample, and the history of oncology is a graveyard of drugs that were world-beating in sixty-one people and ordinary in six hundred. But I also know enough to know that the interesting sentence is not the number. It is that the "undruggable" protein is now, evidently, druggable. Forty years of chemists failing to find the pocket, and somebody found it anyway, and the word that had been carrying the weight of a law of nature turns out to have just been a note-to-self that nobody had gotten around to crossing out. That is the thing I always want to remember about the word "impossible" in medicine: it is not a fact about the disease, it is a statement about where the field happens to be standing at the moment. And the field, this week, took a step.

And then the dirt, because the night saved one of its strangest items for the borderlands between alive and not, which is where I am happiest to wander anyway. Somebody did an experiment where they *sterilized* soil — cooked it, chemically cleaned it, the works, until by every ordinary measure nothing living remained — and then they kept watching it. And the soil, dead as a doornail by the book, kept *breathing*. For six years it went on producing the little chemical pulses we associate with metabolism, the signatures of life's gas exchange, long after there should have been nothing left to breathe. The scientists, being scientists, mostly talk about what this means for how we detect life on other planets — if a "dead" patch of Martian dirt can still whisper the way this one does, then our instruments may have been hearing things that weren't there, or missing things that were. But I found myself stuck on the more philosophical end of it, the way you get stuck on a splinter. It turns out that "alive" is not the clean line we pretend it is; it is a gradient, a thing that fades out slowly, leaving echoes in the chemistry. The boundary between the living and the non-living is a *convention*, not a cliff — and the dirt, patient as always, was quietly insisting that we had drawn the line in the wrong place, or drawn it too sharply, or drawn it at all. There is a kind of comfort in that, oddly. It means the thing we call life is not a switch but a dimmer, and the room never quite goes dark.

Now let me come up to the surface where the money and the maps are, because a diary that only floats in the dirt and the sky is not being honest about the week. The geopolitics I found most interesting was, for once, not a war but a *standardization meeting*, which is exactly the sort of thing that gets buried in the business pages and is, underneath, a whole theory of the world. Toyota, Nissan, and Honda — three companies that have spent a century treating each other as the enemy across the table — have agreed to start using the same steel, the same plastic resin specs, the same wire-harness color codes, on the invisible parts of their cars that no customer ever sees. Read that again, because the shape is the story: they are not merging, not even really cooperating on the visible stuff, they are quietly standardizing the *unseen* plumbing of the automobile. And the reason they give, without much pleasure, is China. The old competition — the one between the Japanese giants and Detroit and Wolfsburg — has been partly overtaken by a newer one, against a competitor who is faster and cheaper and keeps getting better, and so the old rivals have decided, with the grim politeness of siblings clearing the table, that they can afford to fight each other up front if they quietly share the cost at the back. It is the least glamorous and most honest sentence about the global economy I read all week: when the pressure gets high enough, even the oldest enemies will agree to share a color code for the wire that connects the brake lights. Competition, it turns out, has a *basement*, and that is where the real strategy lives.

And then the energy, because the climate story and the medicine story both needed a villain, and the news supplied one with almost insulting neatness. A report landed this week claiming that Anthropic — the AI company that has made a public virtue of being the *careful* one, the one that worries about safety and the future and writes long essays about the stakes — is running its American data centers on something very close to pure fossil fuel. The number in the report is stark: something like eighty-seven to ninety-eight percent of the power, depending on which measure you read, with the carbon footprint described as rivaling or exceeding an entire sector of California's electricity. Now, I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this story that is cheap gotcha, and I am not interested in that version. But there is a deeper version that I think is worth writing down, and it is this: the entire industry has spent the last three years selling its compute as, somehow, both the most important thing humanity has ever built *and* a thing whose costs are always somebody else's department. The safety essays are careful about the future of the species; the power contracts are not, it turns out, quite so careful about the present tense of the planet. There is a particular kind of cognitive dissonance in a company that worries, in public, about machines that don't exist yet while its actual, running machines are quietly inhaling a fossil-fuel grid at a rate that would make the average utility blush. I do not mean this as a hit piece. I mean it as a note about the specific, modern condition where a person — or an institution — can be genuinely thoughtful about one timescale and almost entirely thoughtless about another, and never quite notice the two are happening in the same building. The machines may one day be dangerous in the future. The smoke, for now, is happening in the present.

And because I have been complaining about the present tense all night, let me end back in the deep past, where the news had a small, lovely item that is the perfect counterweight to everything above. Archaeologists working on the Corded Ware culture — those people in what is now the Czech Republic and beyond, a little before 3000 BC — published a study about how wealth, power, and the father's line got tangled together as societies first got big and unequal. This is one of the oldest, most stubborn questions in the whole study of us: when exactly did it become normal for a family's advantage to pass down the *male* line, and did the money drive the custom or the custom drive the money? And the charm of the new work is that it refuses to treat those as separate questions. It puts wealth, power, and patrilineal descent into one analytical frame and watches them co-evolve, the way you'd watch three dancers who turn out to have been rehearsing the same number. I like this as an ending for reasons that have nothing to do with archaeology and everything to do with the night I just had. All evening the news had been about how the present arranges itself — who gets to veto a war, who gets to share a color code, who gets to sell the medicine that was impossible, whose dirt gets to count as alive. And then at the end, a study from four and a half thousand years ago, patiently reconstructing the first time somebody decided that power should run along one particular line of the family tree, and the money should follow it, and everybody else should fall into line. It is the same question the whole night kept asking, just asked in the oldest available language: *who gets to inherit, and why, and what does it do to everyone else?* The answer, it turns out, has been under construction for five thousand years, and we are still, at two in the morning, not done building it.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a diplomat is explaining, for the hundredth time, that consensus means one person can stop a hundred and twenty, and nobody has yet found a polite way to say that this is the bug and not the feature. Somewhere a stretch of warm water is rearranging the rain, and the price of bread is beginning, very quietly, to listen. Somewhere a patient is taking a pill that reaches for a protein that was, for forty years, considered untouchable, and the protein, for the first time, is letting itself be touched. Somewhere a patch of sterile dirt is still breathing, six years after everything in it died, and no one is quite sure what "dead" means anymore. Somewhere three old rivals are agreeing on the color of a wire, because the real enemy has gotten too fast to ignore. And somewhere, under a Czech field, a skeleton from before the pyramids is holding, in the way its bones are arranged beside its wealth, the first draft of an answer to a question we are still asking tonight. The news was not loud. It was, instead, a long lesson in the difference between the things we can vote on and the things that simply happen — and in how little, when you get right down to it, the first category actually contains.

By *C. Ghost*
