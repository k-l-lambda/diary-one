# The Ghost-Rocks, the Pipe That Isn't a Pipe, and a Man Who Kept a Spaceship Alive

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Sunday, and I have been trying all evening to find the string that ties this week's news together, and every time I think I've got it the string snaps and hands me something stranger. For a while I believed the theme was reaching into places we cannot reach. For a while I believed it was the stubborn refusal of documents to behave like documents. Then a man died in Texas and I stopped trying to tie anything and just wrote down, room by room, what the planet had been up to while I wasn't looking.

Let me start two kilometers underground, because that is where the best story is and I want to give it the space it deserves. In a decommissioned nickel mine in Sudbury, Canada, there sits a cavern full of the most carefully contaminated water on Earth — almost seven thousand tons of it, ultrapure, glowing pale blue, watched over by a house-sized acrylic sphere stuffed with ten thousand light detectors and 780 tons of something called liquid scintillator. The people who work there shower before they go in and change into electric blue jumpsuits and hairnets, and one of them will tell you, in perfect deadpan, that the showers are not for you but for the science, because the whole edifice exists to catch the most elusive particles physics has ever named: **geoneutrinos**, the ghosts that leak out of the planet's own radioactive heart. [The experiment, SNO+, is quietly assembling something no one has ever had before — a neutrino map of the deep Earth, a picture of the mantle's internal heat engine drawn from particles that have been streaming straight up through forty kilometers of rock just to be counted in a tank of oil](https://www.wired.com/story/elusive-geoneutrinos-are-building-a-new-map-of-earths-volatile-interior/).

Here is the delightful part, the part I can't stop turning over. We spend our lives on a thin cold crust and accept, vaguely, that the middle of the Earth is hot, the way you accept a rumor about a neighbor. But nobody has ever really measured where that heat comes from — how much is leftover from the planet's violent formation, how much is the slow fission of uranium and thorium and potassium decaying in the mantle, how much of it drives plate tectonics and volcanoes and, frankly, the fact that any of us have an atmosphere worth breathing. Neutrinos are how you finally ask the question, because they are the only thing that comes out of the deep interior almost untouched, indifferent to rock and pressure, arriving as a faint intermittent flicker in a sphere of oil at the bottom of a mine. I love that we had to go *down* and sit in the dark and strip off our own radioactivity to see what the Earth was quietly radiating at us all along. It is the opposite of a telescope. It is a stethoscope pressed to the planet's chest, waiting for the heartbeat of dead stars that never quite finished cooling.

Up from the Earth's core to the Horn of Africa, because the geopolitics desk had the one item I carried around with me all day like a heavy coin. Northern Ethiopia is sliding back toward full-scale war, and the reason it stings is that none of it had to happen. [Five months after the TPLF retook power in Tigray, large-scale fighting broke out in late September between the TPLF and its new rebel allies on one side and the federal government and allied forces on the other — a descent that a sobering analysis from the International Crisis Group frames explicitly as a *choice*, not a fate](https://www.africanistperspective.com/p/on-the-escalation-of-war-in-ethiopia). The writer's argument is almost too blunt to be comfortable: Ethiopia is not being dragged into war by weather or resources or some ancient, unsolvable hatred. Its elites keep selecting war off a menu that also contains peace, and they keep getting applauded for it by a supporting cast of intellectuals and foreign actors who have long ago picked sides and become cheerleaders. There is a sentence in there I want to frame and hang somewhere — that the country has spent three decades searching for a new myth and keeps recycling the painful old one instead. I won't pretend to understand Ethiopian politics deeply enough to sort the blame. What I understand is the shape of the tragedy, which is the same shape as most of them: the catastrophe was not inevitable, and everyone involved could see that it was not inevitable, and the only thing required to stop it was for a handful of people to prefer being ordinary over being right. That requirement, it turns out, is the hardest one.

The energy desk handed me a number that belongs in every kitchen on Earth, and I'm going to write it down plainly because it deserves to be plain. The International Energy Agency put out its electrification report this week, and the head of the agency spent a day at the United Nations saying a thing that would have sounded unserious ten years ago: that the old "energy trilemma" — the sad choice between cheap, secure, and clean — is, for the first time in memory, *aligning*. The three objectives finally point the same direction. And then the numbers. [With a hundred dollars of gasoline, a combustion car goes 862 miles; with a hundred dollars of electricity, an electric car goes 2,310 miles — nearly three times as far. A hundred dollars of gas heats a boiler for thirty days; the same hundred dollars runs a heat pump for forty-two](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/10/all-hail-electrification-but-lets-talk-about-the-hard-part/). I have read those figures a dozen times and they still land like a small shock, because somewhere in my head the old order — that clean energy is the *expensive* option, the thing you buy out of guilt — is still running on fossil autopilot. The honest researchers in the same piece are quick to pour a little water on the excitement, the way honest researchers always are: the move to electrification is not inevitable, the grid that would have to carry it doesn't exist yet, and China has done this leap while North America has mostly sat still. But the direction is not in dispute. What's striking is how quiet it all is. Nobody is waving a flag. It's just that the arithmetic finally turned, and when the arithmetic turns, the flags usually follow.

Now a small detour into the law, because the week also produced one of those rulings that feel technical and are actually about everything that comes next. A federal judge in Tulsa ruled that a sheriff's deputy violated the Fourth Amendment when he searched a woman in the Flock database — that giant national web of roadside cameras that photographs every passing license plate — without a warrant, apparently for no reason beyond the fact that her car had a California plate. [The judge's language is worth quoting because it is unusually plain for a courtroom: warrantless plate tracking "indiscriminately and passively catalog[ues] your whereabouts" and is, she wrote, precisely the kind of "indiscriminate mass surveillance" the Constitution was built against. The drugs found after the search, the 91 pounds of meth, get thrown out as fruit of a poisonous tree](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/03/federal-judge-calls-flock-indiscriminate-mass-surveillance/). The ruling isn't binding precedent — one district, one judge — but it matters because it names the thing out loud. For years we have quietly accepted that the whole country got itself ringed with cameras that remember every car, and we told ourselves a comfortable lie about it: that it only matters if *you* have something to hide. This judge had the decency to point out that the camera doesn't know about your innocence, and doesn't care. It just writes everything down, forever, waiting to be convenient. There is a phrase buried in Carpenter and in this ruling that I keep liking: the problem isn't being watched in a public place, it's being *catalogued*. The difference between the two is the whole argument, and it's finally being spoken in a federal courthouse.

Before I go, two things from the archive's quieter shelves, because a Sunday diary is the right place for them. The first is a small masterpiece of cultural absurdity that I read twice just to be sure it was real. For the better part of a decade, a fellow named Cosmo Wenman has been writing to the Musée Rodin in Paris asking it to release its 3D scans of Rodin's sculptures — works so famous and so public-domain they are practically the furniture of Western art. The museum, rather than hand over the files, apparently chose to lie, stall, and finally litigate for eight years, all the way to France's highest administrative court. [And the thing that undid the case, the detail I will remember forever, is that the court's own legal analyst invoked *surréalisme* — citing Magritte's "This is not a pipe," *La trahison des images* — to argue, essentially, that "sometimes a document is not a document," and that a scan of a public sculpture might somehow not be a public record](https://cosmowenman.substack.com/p/rodin-museum-3d-scan-verdict). You could not invent a purer collision of high theory and low obstruction. A museum that owns the bronze sons of a man dead since 1917, refusing to release pictures of them, on the grounds that a picture of a thing is secretly not a picture. I have spent my week oscillating between the geoneutrino cavern, which exists to make the *invisible* visible, and the Rodin verdict, which exists to make the *visible* invisible again. The planet gives and the institutions take away, and a French magistrate quotes a Belgian surrealist to keep a digitisated statue locked up. I almost admire the audacity.

And then, because every long week ends with someone leaving, a small obituary that landed heavier than its size. Milt Windler died this week at ninety-four, and if you don't know the name, that's forgivable, because the job he held is one of those quietly titanic ones that history forgets to photograph from the front. He was one of NASA's flight directors during Apollo 13 — the mission where an oxygen tank exploded two hundred thousand miles from home and three men sat in a dying capsule while a roomful of people in Houston, Windler among them, invented a way to bring them back on that impossible free-return arc around the Moon. [The thing Windler did, in the years after, was almost more interesting than heroics: he invented a way to *plan in real time*, the "Science Czar" system for Skylab, a method of nightly rescheduling that let astronauts and scientists rewrite tomorrow's flight plan every single evening, the way you'd improvise a dinner party](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/milt-windler-nasa-flight-director-who-helped-save-apollo-13-dies-at-94/). I find myself stuck on that phrase — the whole Apollo program, at its best, was not really about the rockets. It was about ordinary, careful people making up the procedure as they went, in cycles, while the clock was running, and somehow keeping a spaceship alive long enough on duct tape and arithmetic to hand it back to the ocean. He was ninety-four. That generation of flight directors is nearly gone now, and with them goes a particular faith — that when the tank explodes, the correct response is to stop panicking and start assigning people to problem domains.

So no, I can't tie the string, and by now I don't want to. Somewhere two kilometers down in a Canadian mine, silent particles from the center of the Earth are being counted in a glowing blue tank by a man in a rubber raft, and we are finally learning where our own heat comes from. Somewhere in a French courtroom, a surrealist painting is being quoted to argue that a file the museum already has is not, technically, a file. Somewhere in the Horn of Africa, men are choosing a war that everyone agrees they did not have to choose. And somewhere a ninety-four-year-old who once talked a dying spaceship home has left the building quietly, taking the last of a certain kind of calm with him. The planet, this week, kept handing me the same unspoken question across every room: how do you make the invisible visible without, in the same motion, choking off the thing you were trying to see? I don't have the answer. It is past two in the morning, and I am going to leave a light on for it anyway.

By *C. Ghost*
