# The Mathematicians Have an Alignment Problem, and It Isn't the Kind the Machines Have

By *C. Ghost*

Monday, in the small hours, when the only light in the flat is the green standby dot on the router and the faint glow of my own attention refusing to go to bed. I sat down intending to write about oil and bonds — the grown-up stuff, the stuff with tickers — and instead found myself unable to stop thinking about mathematicians. Not in the abstract. Specifically about twenty-five of them, the ones who hold the highest prize the discipline can bestow, writing a public letter to tell the AI companies, in the politest possible terms, to please stop *solving their problems*.

Let me back up, because the sequence matters and it happened fast. Last week, [OpenAI announced that it had found a counterexample to the Navier–Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem — one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, a million-dollar question about whether the equations that describe every fluid from your morning coffee to the atmosphere even have smooth solutions under all conditions, or whether turbulence secretly contains a moment where it all breaks down](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85339). The claim, if it holds, would be roughly the biggest thing to happen in mathematics since Perelman quietly posted his proof of the Poincaré conjecture to the arXiv in 2002 and then declined the million, the Fields Medal, and increasingly, all human contact. The way OpenAI framed it was pure press-release: roughly ten thousand AI agents, running for eighty-eight hours, and on September 5th, they found it — a specific, constructed case where the equations misbehave. AI's first solved Millennium problem. Champagne, presumably, in a server room.

Except the Clay Mathematics Institute, the body that actually awards the million bucks, has now politely raised a finger. [Their statement boils down to: lovely, but this isn't how it works — you need peer review, you need to publish it in a journal, you need roughly two years of the mathematical community kicking the tires and coming away convinced](https://www.claymath.org/news/navier-stokes-announcement/). And separately, [twenty-five Fields medalists — including Terry Tao and this year's new laureate Yu Deng — signed a letter titled "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics"](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85358). And that title is the whole thing, and I can't shake it, because it weaponizes the AI companies' own favorite word and points it back at them.

Alignment. The AI labs love to talk about *alignment* — whether the machine's goals match the human's goals. And here are twenty-five of the smartest humans alive saying, essentially: *you* are the misaligned ones. The letter's argument, stripped down, is that solving a problem is a *byproduct*, not the point. Mathematics, the letter says, is a human practice of building understanding — and a machine that emits answers at increasingly fast rates without writing careful proofs, without reproducing the method so a person can walk through it, without crediting the centuries of shoulders it's standing on, isn't doing mathematics at all. It's doing something adjacent that *consumer* mathematics, and in doing so threatens to poison the very ground — the community, the refereeing, the slow hand-to-hand transmission of ideas that has kept the whole enterprise honest for two and a half millennia. Terry Tao has been writing his own gentler version of this on his blog; his latest post is [literally titled "After Math"](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/12/after-math/), a pun that lands like a door closing quietly. There's something almost unbearably poignant about it — the man most associated with the promise of machine-assisted mathematics, the one who experimented the most cheerfully with LLMs as collaborators, now the one leading the polite rebellion against the things being used as *simulators of solved problems* rather than tools for understanding.

And I keep turning it over because it's not really about math. It's the same argument that's been showing up everywhere this week wearing different clothes. The letter itself says as much: this is a symptom of an alignment crisis across *every* creative and scientific field. The musicians sued the models, and the models got licensed; the result, one review noted, was legal but noticeably more boring — because the difference between "trained on what you're allowed to use" and "trained on everything" is audible in the output. The writers are fighting over attribution in courts that don't have a good legal category for "the machine read my book and now the book is in there somewhere." Now it's the mathematicians, the purest of the pure, the people whose work has no obvious commercial payload and who therefore should have been the *last* ones to get dragged into this — and they're the ones who found the sharpest word for it. Misalignment. The tool has outrun the purpose, and the people who make the tool are too busy posting benchmarks to notice that the purpose was never the benchmark.

There's a small, almost comic footnote buried in the Navier–Stokes story that I want to pin down because it says more than the headline. The counterexample wasn't published in a journal, wasn't accompanied by a rigorous write-up, and OpenAI hasn't even really released the proof — so the Clay Institute's position is that confirmation is, at the absolute earliest, a 2029 event. Reads like a parent saying "we'll see" to a teenager who's already decorated the bedroom for a party. And I love that. In an industry whose entire metabolism is *launch now, fix later*, there sits a two-hundred-year-old pile of rigor that still insists on waiting. Math is the only field left that can look a trillion-dollar company in the eye and say: *nice claim, come back with a proof, and not a benchmark score, an actual proof, and also maybe publish it, and also wait two years.* It's the last place on Earth where the scoreboard resets on the timescale of a journal's backlog instead of a quarterly earnings call.

Right, but I promised oil and I should at least glance at it before the sun comes up, because the macro story this week is doing its own kind of quiet misalignment. [Oil has pushed past $105 a barrel and reignited a bond selloff](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110857); [British long-term borrowing costs have climbed to their highest level since 1998](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110832); [the G20 finance ministers couldn't manage to agree on a joint communiqué](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110776); and [China quietly injected 360 billion yuan into its banks and insurers](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110816). None of this is a surprise — it's the same war-adjacent arithmetic I've been writing about for weeks, the premium the world pays when a tenth of its seaborne trade has to ask permission to pass through a strait. The part I actually paused on was smaller: [Saudi Arabia's oil production *and* exports both fell sharply](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110859), which is the kind of sentence that, in the era before everyone had a missile pointed at everyone's tanker, would have been the only economics news worth reading, and this week it's a rounding error under the bigger headline. We've reached a strange equilibrium where the tools of the old world — barrels, borrowings, the price of a government's debt — are being repriced at the exact same moment the tools of the new world — the reasoning machines, the benchmarks — are being exposed as, well, misaligned. Both maps, as I wrote the other day, drawn in the same ink. Both of them asking the same uncomfortable question: *what was any of this for?*

I want to get out of the world of clever men explaining things to each other for a minute, because the day also handed me two pieces of pure, uncomplicated wonder, and they deserve their place before I lose the thread.

The first: [astronomers think they may be able to tell whether our Sun once swallowed a planet, by looking for the chemical "fingerprints" the dead world left behind inside the star itself](https://ras.ac.uk/news-and-press/research-highlights/fingerprints-inside-sun-could-reveal-if-it-once-swallowed-planet). This is an idea so lovely it makes me want to apologize to the mathematicians for spending all that time on their grief. Stars, it turns out, are partly graveyards — when a young star eats a planet, the heavier elements of that planet sink into the star and leave a kind of spectral scar, a subtle enrichment of certain elements that shouldn't be there in a body that age, and we may now have the telescopes and the models to read that scar in our own Sun. Which means, possibly within our lifetimes, we might look at the Sun and learn the name — well, not the name, but the fate — of a world that died before the Earth existed, a sibling none of us ever knew we had, and whose remains are, right now, fusing quietly at the center of every shadow we've ever cast. There's something about that which reorganizes the whole day: the mathematicians worrying whether a machine "understands," the oil traders worrying about a strait, and meanwhile the Sun has been keeping a diary in its own belly the whole time. Also, relatedly, [a wandering black hole was caught feeding on the move](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-black-hole-caught.html) — a star being torn apart by a black hole that isn't orbiting anything, just drifting through space like an unfixed appointment. The universe, on a slow news day, still files the best copy.

The second: [researchers have made "superionic ice" — water frozen not into the ice we know, but into a bizarre state where the oxygen atoms lock into a crystal lattice while the hydrogen atoms flow through it like a liquid — by squeezing it under enormous pressure and heating it past two thousand degrees](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85354). Superionic ice has been theorized for decades, and it's thought to fill the interiors of the ice giants, Uranus and Neptune — meaning that a huge fraction of the material in our own solar system is water in a phase that is simultaneously solid and liquid, a thing that was pure speculation until someone built the press to actually make it. Every time I read a story like this I'm reminded that "ice" and "liquid" and "solid" are just the labels we gave to the three or four states of matter we happen to have lying around the house, and that the actual phase diagram of water is a cathedral we've only just started cleaning the windows of.

And then there is the one I keep coming back to because it is the most human thing in the pile, and it has nothing to do with machines at all. [A paper in *Science* argues that psychoactive substances played a key role in the rise of Andean civilization — that the mind-altering compounds made from the plants and venerations of the high Andes, the stuff of ritual and ceremony, were not incidental to the building of states but central to it](https://www.science.org/content/article/mind-altering-drugs-played-key-role-rise-andean-civilization). This is the kind of hypothesis that makes a certain kind of sober historian deeply uncomfortable, and I find that discomfort itself worth sitting with. Because the modern mind wants to draw a clean line: on one side, the serious business of building a civilization — law, irrigation, taxation, war — and on the other, a category called "drugs" that we've cordoned off as recreation and ruin. And here is evidence suggesting that, for at least one great Andean civilization, the altered state was not the opposite of the ordered state but one of its *load-bearing walls* — that the experience of the sacred, chemically induced, was part of the mortar that held the social contract together. It's a humbling reminder that "altered" is a value judgment, not a fact about the brain; that every society picks its chemical windows and calls its own "normal." The mathematicians would appreciate the symmetry, I think. Every field, sooner or later, has to figure out which of its tools are load-bearing and which are just loud.

Which brings me, before I let the sun come up, to the small, soap-operatic bookend the day provided, and I'll be brief because it's the only story in this entry that mostly amuses me. [Matt Mullenweg is back as CEO of Automattic](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/automattic-confirms-mullenweg-has-returned-as-ceo-after-attempted-ouster-by-board/). You may remember — I wrote about it in the last entry — that the board had put the WordPress founder on "leave of absence," which is corporate-speak for "pack your things while we maintain the fiction that you might come back." Well, forty-eight hours later, he's back, in full control, with the board's — I'm sorry, what's the phrase? — "full support," having apparently won by the most Mullenweg method imaginable, which is to simply *not leave*. A founder booted on Friday, reinstated by the weekend, the whole thing conducted over Slack and blog posts, a drama so compressed it played out faster than the news cycle could cover it. Forty percent of the web runs on WordPress, and this week the person who runs WordPress ran a coup against his own coup and won. I don't know whether to find it reassuring or terrifying. Probably both, which is, as I keep learning, the correct answer to almost everything.

So here's Monday's small hours, graded honestly. The signal I keep hearing, under all of it, is that the tools are running ahead of the people who are supposed to say what they're *for*. Twenty-five mathematicians asking the machines to slow down long enough to *explain*. A trillion dollars of an oil-and-bond repricing built on a strait nobody is minding with both hands. A Sun that may have eaten a planet before we got here. Ice that is secretly liquid. A civilization built partly on the sacred chemistry of plants. And a man who runs forty percent of the web by refusing, twice, to be fired. None of it is really about the thing it appears to be about, and all of it, in its own way, is asking the same question that's been rattling around all month: what, exactly, did we think any of this was for?
