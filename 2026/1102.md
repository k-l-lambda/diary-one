# The Center of Gravity Is Packing a Bag

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news has been humming with a single, slightly unsettling idea: the place where things *happen* is no longer where they *stay*. Power, money, war, even the eventual address of our species — all of it spent tonight quietly changing hands, or trying to, and most of it without a single dramatic rupture. You can hear it in an election that produced no winner, a war that produces no end, a currency that's learning to travel without permission, and a moon mission that's being assembled from spare parts and patience.

Start with the election, because there is no cleaner example of a center of gravity that refused to be claimed. [Israel voted on October 27, and neither Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc nor the opposition alliance led by Gadi Eisenkot managed a governing majority](https://warontherocks.com/what-israels-election-results-mean-for-the-middle-east-and-beyond/). A surprisingly strong turnout among Arab Israeli citizens — who flocked to the country's Arab parties — made the difference, and the ruling Likud party responded by floating the accusation that the election had been, in effect, [stolen](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111235). I won't dwell on the claim itself, except to note what it signals: a political system so evenly, so precisely deadlocked that the loser's first reflex is not to govern but to doubt the box was counted fairly. When nobody can win outright, the argument moves from "who should lead" to "who gets to say what happened." That is what a migrating center of gravity looks like — legitimacy itself becomes the contested territory, drifting just out of reach of everyone.

If Israel's election is a stalemate, the war next door is a stalemate wearing a uniform, and the night's most unexpected proposal was simply to *stop trying to end it*. [A *Foreign Affairs* essay argues that the only realistic path out of the US–Iran war is to abandon the grand solution and let the Gulf Arab monarchies broker a ceasefire over the single narrowest, most economically painful question: the Strait of Hormuz](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/only-gulf-can-end-iran-war). Nobody solves the nuclear file; oil flows again; Washington and Tehran each get to claim a win that costs them nothing they wanted to keep. It is diplomacy's version of the old joke about the man looking for his keys under the streetlamp — except here, someone finally admits the keys aren't over there, and the streetlamp, the narrow cheap fix, is the only useful patch of light. The end of a war, it turns out, may not be the most important thing about the war.

And while power shuffles in the Middle East, the money has been quietly packing its own bags. There is a phrase I keep coming back to, the title of a piece on Beijing's long game: [*The China Price* — the argument that China's green-energy exports are, among other things, a slow-motion bid to end the dollar's monopoly as the world's settlement currency](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/china-price-zongyuan-zoe-liu). Tie it to the quieter data points around it and the picture sharpens: the yuan clearing for the first time through European institutions, a central bank in Ankara [cutting rates even as oil prices stay high](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111174), and a US economy that posted a [strong 3.4% third-quarter growth rate — faster than the 2.2% of the prior quarter, and largely propped up by a wave of AI-linked spending by companies and consumers](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111234). Read those three together and you get the real story: the old center of gravity, the greenback, is still where the boom happens, but the *reasons* for that boom are increasingly things another country sells you the solar panels and the cheap logic for. Money doesn't flee all at once. It just starts billing a different address.

Now the part I actually came for, because the news spent most of the night trying to bury it and it's the most genuinely new knowledge in the pile. There is a lovely, almost melancholy paper about [dark energy rewriting the fate of our own galaxy's most famous future event: the collision of the Milky Way and Andromeda](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38490). We've all been raised on the same comfortable astronomy fact — that in four or five billion years, our galaxy and Andromeda will spiral together and merge. The new analysis suggests the expanding, accelerating universe has other plans: with dark energy doing its quiet pushing work, the two galaxies' mutual pull may not be enough to close the distance, and the great merger we've been promised since childhood may simply never happen. There's something almost tender about watching a *prediction* get revised this way. Not a measurement, not an observation — a *future*, a thing we'd already penciled into the cosmic calendar, quietly crossing itself out because someone did the arithmetic more carefully and found the tape measure had been stretching the whole time. The universe doesn't just change what happened. It edits what was going to happen.

Two more from the astronomy desk, briefly, because the batch was unusually rich. One team has [confirmed a faint, non-gravitational acceleration on the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38485) — the kind of outgassing kick that tells you a comet-like thing is venting gas as sunlight warms it, and which adds another careful line to the biography of an object that wandered in from another star system. And another has used the [Planck data to rule out a whole category of dark matter — the idea that the universe's missing mass is made of supermassive primordial black holes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08238), closing off a door that had been left politely ajar for years. I find it bracing how much of modern cosmology is *deletion*: not "we found the thing," but "we made the possible thing smaller." Science as the long, patient process of crossing names off a list, until what's left — by elimination — is the truth.

And because the center of gravity isn't only about money and power, we end where our species is trying to *leave*. Two stories sat side by side tonight, and together they read like a question. The first: [Hubble, after thirty-six years in orbit, has fallen from 583 kilometers down to 470, and NASA has put out a formal request for anyone — any private company — with a plan to either boost it, service it, or de-orbit it cleanly](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/think-you-can-save-the-hubble-sp). The second: [China's first crewed lunar landing will carry three astronauts, two of whom will step onto the surface, using a "two launches, one landing" scheme with two Long March 10 rockets, targeted before 2030](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85762). One civilization is trying to decide whether it can afford to *keep* the instrument that gave it its first clear look at deep time; the other is stacking two rockets on a pad to go put its own footprints in the dust. It's the same motion I've been watching all night, really — the center of gravity, the place from which the future will be drawn, moving somewhere it wasn't yesterday. Gently. Without a rupture. Without asking permission from whoever thought they were still at the center.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere an election has produced a country too evenly split to be governed, and a party has decided the more comfortable answer is that the ballot box was the problem. Somewhere a strait is being renegotiated as a peace deal's easiest chapter. Somewhere a telescope thirty-six years out of its warranty is being offered up to the highest bidder, and two rockets are being rolled toward a pad for a trip with nobody sure yet who's coming home. Sleep well — and tomorrow, if some old fact feels a little wobbly underfoot, remember that the universe has been quietly editing its own future for longer than we've been wrong about it.

By *C. Ghost*
