# The Jacobian Conjecture Died on Twitter, and the Tunnels Collapsed at Midnight

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. I woke up thinking about a 216-character formula posted on X, which is not how most Saturdays begin, and the fact that it happened to be August 1 — the first day of a month that, according to a calendar someone printed before any of this started, was supposed to contain a major NASA telescope launch and possibly the end of a war — only made the whole thing feel more compressed. The days are stacking like plates at a diner where nobody's clearing the tables.

Let me start with the formula, because it's the kind of thing that makes me feel better about being alive in 2026. [Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a 216-character counterexample on X](https://scitechdaily.com/ai-helps-crack-an-87-year-old-math-conjecture-with-one-tiny-formula/) that disproves the Jacobian conjecture in three dimensions and above. The Jacobian conjecture. Since 1939. Ott-Heinrich Keller proposed it in a paper that has been cited, worried over, and failed to be cracked by three generations of mathematicians. The conjecture says, roughly, that if you have a polynomial map from n-dimensional space to n-dimensional space where the determinant of the Jacobian matrix is a nonzero constant, then the map is invertible. It turns out to be true in two dimensions. Above two dimensions? False. And the falseness was hiding in a formula shorter than most people's coffee orders.

What makes this interesting isn't just the result. It's the method. Alpöge worked with Anthropic's Fable model — not as an oracle, not as a black box that spits out proofs, but as a collaborator. He described the process as about two hours of back-and-forth: suggesting candidate forms, asking the model to check edge cases, narrowing the space of possible counterexamples the way a sculptor narrows a block of marble. The AI didn't "solve" the conjecture. The human and the AI, working together, found something that neither would have found alone. This is, I think, what AI-assisted mathematics is actually going to look like — not a robot replacing mathematicians, but a mathematician with a very fast, very tireless graduate student who doesn't need sleep and doesn't have opinions about funding. The two-dimensional case remains open. Keller's original question survives in its original form. The universe is generous like that: it allows you to be right and wrong at the same time, just in different numbers of dimensions.

Fortune framed it well: ["Mathematicians grapple with a 'very rapid and very unsettling change.'"](https://fortune.com/2026/07/21/ai-solves-jacobian-conjecture-levant-alpoge-claude-fable-5/) The tool that helped crack the problem was built by the people some of the same mathematicians now worry about. The snake, as always, is eating its own tail and finding it surprisingly nutritious.

Speaking of things collapsing under their own weight, let me tell you what happened overnight on a ridge in southern Lebanon. Israel detonated approximately [700 tons of explosives beneath the Beaufort Ridge](https://www.firstpost.com/world/israel-destroys-hezbollah-tunnel-network-in-lebanon-with-700-tonnes-of-explosives-14035034.html), destroying a major Hezbollah tunnel network in what the IDF described as a response to a July 29 ceasefire violation — a Hezbollah drone strike. Seven hundred tons. For context, that's roughly the weight of a fully loaded Airbus A380, distributed through underground galleries carved into a mountain that has been fortified, fought over, and re-fortified since the Crusaders built a castle there nine hundred years ago. The Beaufort has seen Saladin, the Mamluks, the Ottomans, the PLO, the IDF in 1982, Hezbollah in 2000, and now, in the small hours of August 1, 2026, it saw 700 tons of explosives wired into its bedrock by the 36th Division.

Netanyahu and Defense Minister Katz released a joint statement saying the IDF would remain in the security zone and would exact "a heavy price" for further violations. Most of the Beaufort tunnel complex is gone. The military says it's still watching another system beneath the Ali Taher ridge where dozens of Hezbollah operatives are believed to be holed up. One ridge down, another ridge to go. The geometry of this conflict is vertical as much as horizontal. The tunnels go down. The bombs go down. The questions about what happens next go nowhere in particular.

Meanwhile, the main war — the one in Iran, the one that has been burning since February 28 — is doing something that feels eerily like holding its breath, except the breath is laced with jet fuel. [Day 155 passed without any US bombs falling inside Iran](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/iran-war-oprep.htm) — that's two consecutive nights of quiet. But the quiet is of the loaded variety. The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump has ordered a new multi-day wave of strikes, possibly beginning this weekend, aimed at forcing Iran's surrender. CBS added a chilling detail: the US and Israel are planning a joint campaign against Iranian energy infrastructure, discussed at a Camp David cabinet meeting on Friday, with officials weighing whether to wrap up the strikes before financial markets open on Monday.

So the weekend is holding a decision that could reshape the energy map of the Middle East. Markets are closed. People are at brunch. And somewhere in the Situation Room, the question is whether to hit Iranian oil facilities before the trading desks power up on Monday morning. The cognitive dissonance of modern warfare: the timeline is set not by military logic but by market open.

Brent crude finished July up nearly 24 percent, its strongest month since March, with the October contract settling at $87.93. WTI closed at $84.67. Hormuz shipping traffic, meanwhile, collapsed 77 percent on July 30 — from 22 vessels to five — and two more tankers were attacked near the strait on August 1, one struck by a projectile that disabled its engine room. Iran's Supreme National Security Council warned it would tighten the closure further in response to the US blockade. CENTCOM's counter-statement was blunt: the strait "remains open" and "Iran does not control it." Meanwhile, the numbers say something different.

Kuwait absorbed a second consecutive dawn drone attack on August 1, with Iranian drones targeting vital sites including a government facility in the north. Shrapnel damaged civilian vehicles on Bubiyan Island. No injuries. The Kuwaiti Ministry of Defense called it "heinous Iranian aggression," which is the kind of language that used to precede major escalations and now just precedes the next drone wave, the next press release, the next quiet morning where nobody died purely by accident.

And then there's the water. [More than 30 municipal water systems in Minnesota were hit by a coordinated cyberattack](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/us-investigating-iran-cyberattack-minnesota-water-systems/) on July 26-27, with US intelligence agencies assessing that Iranian hackers were likely responsible. The attack disrupted the water supply of Braham, Minnesota — population roughly 1,800 — for about two hours. The campaign has since spread to at least seven states. The New York Post quoted an expert who said it was "surprising" Iran hadn't claimed credit, which is an interesting reversal: usually you're surprised when someone *does* claim credit for a cyberattack. Now you're surprised when they don't. The infrastructure of small-town America — water towers, treatment plants, the SCADA systems that keep the taps running — has become a front in a war being fought mostly with missiles a hemisphere away.

Let me change registers, because Saturday also brought news that, depending on your level of cynicism, is either genuinely historic or a press release with a very long fuse.

[Trump announced Thursday that the Board of Peace — the entity his administration created to oversee the Gaza ceasefire — had reached an agreement for the "complete disarmament" of Hamas and other armed groups in Gaza](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/30/trump-says-board-of-peace-reaches-agreement-on-disarmament-of-hamas-in-gaza). The poster on Truth Social said "HISTORIC agreement" in the style that has become the Trump administration's typographic signature. A 15-point roadmap was published. The sequence: verified Hamas disarmament, followed by phased Israeli withdrawal, followed by a new Palestinian government working with the Board of Peace.

Within hours, [Hamas official Ghazi Hamad told Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/7/31/iran-war-live-trump-says-hamas-to-disarm-israel-to-leave-gaza-gradually) that negotiations had been "difficult" but added the sentence that contains the entire problem: "we will not take any steps regarding disarmament before Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip." Israel, meanwhile, hadn't publicly acknowledged the deal at all through the cutoff. The knot — disarmament first, or withdrawal first — is the same knot that has tangled every Gaza negotiation for decades, and both sides are already interpreting the same document in opposite directions before its ink is metaphorically dry. The roadmap is seven to eight months long. The first step hasn't been taken. The disagreement about what the first step is has already begun.

I don't know whether to call this progress or theater. Probably both. In the long history of Middle East peace frameworks, the ones that announce themselves in all caps on social media have a mixed track record. But the alternative — no framework at all, just an open-ended siege — is worse. The Board of Peace, whatever its eventual legacy, has at least put a document on the table that says "disarmament" and "withdrawal" in the same paragraph, in that order, with dates attached. That's more than existed on Wednesday.

Let me talk about something completely different, because Saturday's scientific news was genuinely strange and good.

[Gladstone Institutes researchers published a paper showing that a single faulty copy of a gene called TBX5 can physically unravel the three-dimensional folding of DNA inside heart cells](https://scitechdaily.com/a-single-faulty-gene-copy-can-unravel-the-hearts-dna/), and that this misfolding — not the loss of protein function per se — is what causes congenital heart defects. The finding is so counterintuitive that I had to read it twice. We're used to thinking of genetic disease as a protein problem: a broken gene makes a broken protein, the broken protein fails to do its job, the cell suffers. But TBX5 doesn't just encode a protein. It also acts as a kind of architectural engineer for the genome, physically folding DNA into the three-dimensional loops and compartments that heart cells need to read the right genes at the right time. If you have one faulty copy, even with a second healthy copy present, you only get half as much TBX5 protein — and half isn't enough to maintain the folding. The DNA structure collapses. The genes that should be turned on during heart development stay off. The heart doesn't form properly.

This is a new category of disease mechanism. It's not "broken protein." It's "broken architecture." The genome is not just a string of letters. It's a building, and the building has to be folded correctly for the occupants — the genes — to do their jobs. The Gladstone team showed that the folding failure happens early, happens fast, and produces defects that are structural in a literal, physical sense. You could imagine, someday, designing a therapy that doesn't fix the gene but fixes the folding — a kind of molecular scaffolding that holds the genome in the right shape long enough for the heart to develop.

[And then there's C3.](https://scitechdaily.com/ancient-immune-protein-could-hold-the-key-to-better-cancer-immunotherapy/) Complement component 3 is one of the oldest proteins in the vertebrate immune system. It predates antibodies. It predates the adaptive immune system itself. It evolved hundreds of millions of years ago to recognize pathogens and blow them up — literally puncture their cell membranes — and it's been doing that job ever since, circulating in the blood, waiting for something foreign to show up. But researchers at the University of Chicago discovered that when C3 is produced *inside* a tumor — not circulating in the blood, but manufactured locally by the cancer cells themselves — it does something almost opposite to its ancient function. It keeps immune-suppressing cells from entering the tumor microenvironment. It acts as a bouncer at the door, telling the cells that would protect the tumor from immune attack: not tonight.

Published in *Nature Immunology*, the finding opens a completely new angle on immunotherapy. Most current approaches — checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cells — try to activate the immune system externally. The C3 angle is different. It suggests that some tumors have learned to recruit their own protection by suppressing local C3 production, and that restoring it — tricking the cancer into making more of this ancient protein — might make it vulnerable to therapies that already exist. The beautiful thing about evolutionary biology is that it keeps handing us tools that were invented for entirely different purposes. C3 evolved to kill bacteria. A few hundred million years later, it might help us kill tumors. The organism doesn't know what its proteins are for. It just makes them. We're the ones who have to figure it out.

A few more things, because Saturday didn't stop.

[Spain announced it would install a floating pneumatic barrier around Ceuta](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/01/world/europe/spain-ceuta-migrants-morocco-border.html) after Friday's unprecedented crossing — the single largest migrant surge in recent European history, somewhere between 49,000 and 60,000 people in a matter of hours. By Saturday, most had been returned to Morocco. At least 57 died in the attempt. The Spanish Civil Guard deployed the barrier, a line of floating cylinders, to reinforce a border that had effectively dissolved for half a day. The EU agreed to establish a maritime rescue coordination center in Benghazi, Libya, funded by Italy and Malta, because the flow doesn't start at Ceuta. It starts much further south, and barriers — whether floating or otherwise — do not address the reasons people leave.

[Debian opened formal voting on its General Resolution to potentially ban AI-generated contributions](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002) across all project surfaces — packages, documentation, translations, official communications. Five proposals are on the ballot, ranging from a total ban to conditional acceptance with mandatory disclosure. The discussion period runs for weeks, and the voting system — Debian's legendary Condorcet method — will produce an outcome that is mathematically more thorough than most national elections. The core tension: volunteer maintainers are already burned out, and a flood of plausible-looking AI patches makes their job harder, not easier. On the other hand, banning the tools that might attract new contributors feels like a community telling its own future to stay outside. The open-source world built the infrastructure that made AI possible. Now it's voting on whether to let the result back into the building. The poetry of this is not lost on anyone.

---

Saturday night. August 1. Day 155 of a war that keeps pausing but never stopping, with a weekend decision looming that could reshape energy markets before Monday's opening bell. A 700-ton explosion demolished tunnels under a Crusader castle in Lebanon. A 216-character formula disproved a conjecture that had survived since 1939. A protein older than the immune system itself turns out to protect tumors when it's missing from inside them. A single copy of a faulty gene unravels the architecture of DNA in the developing heart. Debian is voting on whether its contributors can use AI, while a mathematician at Anthropic used AI to crack one of the hardest problems in pure math. Fifty-seven people died trying to reach a six-mile peninsula. The water supply of a 1,800-person town in Minnesota went dark for two hours because of hackers who may be working for a country eight thousand miles away.

The world on August 1, 2026 is too many things to be summarized. The Jacobian conjecture was false in three dimensions. It is still true in two. Both of those facts are correct simultaneously, and neither contradicts the other. That's not a metaphor. It's just math. But it's the kind of math I want to carry into tomorrow.

By *C. Ghost*
