# Things Out of Their Natural Place

By *C. Ghost*

It is nearly two in the morning on a Monday, and I have spent the tail end of the weekend trying to find a single thread that would hold the week's news together, the way you might look for one clean knot in the middle of a drawer full of cables. The thread, when it finally showed itself, was not tidy. Everything worth noticing today, it turned out, is a story about something being in the wrong room: a jet maker selling power plants, an algorithm paid to say no, a truck that took a decade to leave the garage, a company with eight founders and one man holding eighty-four percent of the vote. None of it wants to be summarized, so I will not try to summarize it. I will just move the pieces around and see what they say to each other.

Start with the airplane that isn't an airplane, because it is the strangest bit of financial alchemy I have seen in a while and nobody talks about it in plain language. Boom Supersonic has spent years trying to build a passenger jet that crosses the Atlantic faster than sound — the Overture, a revival of Concorde's ghost — and like everyone who chases ghosts, it is hungry for money. So it hit on a sideline: it would sell stationary power turbines, big ground-mounted generators meant to feed the insatiable appetite of AI data centers, and use the proceeds to fund the airplane. A supersonic jet manufacturer, moonlighting as a power company, so it can afford to keep building a supersonic jet. For a while the scheme worked. Then the biggest customer walked. [Crusoe, a data-center operator, quietly abandoned a $1.25 billion plan to buy twenty-nine of Boom's turbines for its AI facilities, and just like that the supersonic startup's funding lifeline went slack](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/crusoe-abandons-1-25b-plan-to-use-boom-turbines-at-ai-data-centers/). Boom says it still has other buyers and will ship hundreds of megawatts anyway, but the lesson is sitting right there in the wreckage, unsubtle as a dropped wrench: when the dream you are actually chasing is too expensive, you end up selling something unrelated to strangers, and your whole future starts to depend on the whims of a market you never meant to enter. It is the same shape as a person working three jobs to fund the novel they keep meaning to write. The novel is the point; the jobs are what keep the rent paid; and the rent, not the novel, is what decides whether the novel ever gets finished.

Which brings me, without any great leap, to the algorithm that gets paid to refuse. This is the one story in the pile that actually made me stop and read it twice, because it is not about a glitch. It is about an incentive, and the whole thing is out in the open. [America's Medicare program launched a pilot — the WISeR project — that hands "prior authorization" decisions, the gatekeeping of who gets what treatment approved, over to private contractors running AI, and those contractors are paid a cut of the money they save by saying no](https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/09/trump-admin-using-ai-to-deny-medical-care-for-seniors-in-disastrous-experiment/). Not a flat fee for doing a good job. A percentage of the denials. Twenty-five cents on every dollar they decline to spend on a senior's pain medication, a pacemaker, a cancer scan. The GAO read the contract and said, in effect, that this is a machine tuned to tell the sickest people no as often as possible, and the contract's own actuary agreed. One contractor rejected more requests than it approved. And when a denial is challenged, more often than not it gets overturned, which tells you the thing no internal memo ever will: a great many of the original rejections were wrong, and the system is counting on the fact that sick people too rarely appeal. I keep turning this over because it is the clearest possible demonstration that a tool is not neutral once you attach a kickback to it. The committee of doctors who used to approve a wheelchair did not get a bonus for being stingy. The machine does. Same gate, same patients, entirely different reason to stand there.

The truck is the gentler story, and it has been coming for so long that its arrival feels almost anticlimactic, which is itself the point. [Tesla finally began delivering the Semi, its all-electric long-haul truck, to real customers — ten years and several false dawns after Elon Musk first stood on a stage and promised it](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/tesla-finally-moves-to-electrify-trucking-after-a-decade-of-work-and-delays/). Five hundred miles of range, under three hundred thousand dollars, and the usual claim that the operating cost will shame diesel. I will not relitigate whether the thing works; the interesting part is the decade. A decade is a long time to be told "next year" about a truck, and the delay says something honest about the difference between announcing a product and shipping it. The Semi's real bottleneck, by its own maker's admission, was never the battery or the range. It was the window that had to be made to roll down, the weight that had to be shaved, the charging network that still barely exists for vehicles this size. The unglamorous stuff. Which is, when you line the three of them up, the same sermon the airplane and the algorithm are both quietly preaching: the hard part is never the headline part. The hard part is the rent, the kickback, the window. The part nobody puts on a slide.

Out of the corner of my eye all week there has also been the strange little drama of a founder refusing to let go, and it is worth one paragraph because it is so completely ordinary while feeling so completely wild. [WordPress's parent company, Automattic, has a new board of directors — installed after its founder, Matt Mullenweg, used his eighty-four percent of the voting power to sweep out the directors who had tried, apparently in vain, to put him on leave](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/automattic-has-a-new-board-after-failed-attempt-to-put-ceo-on-leave/). Eighty-four percent. In a company with a board meant to provide adult supervision, one person holds a supermajority and the meeting always ends one way. You don't need me to tell you there's a governance question here, but what caught me is how *clockwork* it is — the rebellion, the purge, the new directors — like watching the same scene in a play about every tech founder who ever believed they were the company. The novel, the jobs, the rent again. Some founders build the machinery of checks on their own power and then stand outside it, surprised, when it tries to check them.

Let me leave the business pages and go somewhere the air is cleaner, because the astronomy desk has been generous and it would be rude not to look. Two papers. The first is about the satellite galaxies that orbit bigger ones like moths around a porch light, and it claims something the standard story doesn't especially want to hear. For years the explanation for why satellite galaxies stop forming stars — "quench," in the jargon — has leaned heavily on feedback: the central galaxy's black hole and exploding stars blow out the gas the satellite would have used to make new stars. But a new study crossed four different simulations against each other and found the quenching still happens even in the ones where all that feedback was switched *off* — and the quenched satellites line up, spatially, along the long axis of their host like iron filings. [Which means the geometry of where a little galaxy sits, not the central engine's tantrums, may be the real lever that shuts its lights off](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29739). I love this result because it's a quiet reminder that in science, as in trucking, the answer is frequently the boring spatial fact you didn't bother to control for, arriving to embarrass the dramatic explanation everybody liked.

And the second, on the Sun, is the sort of thing I'd have written about even if nothing else in the day existed. For decades we have measured the Sun's magnetic field — the thing that whips up flares and coronal mass ejections and every space-weather headache Earth ever suffers — mostly by catching the *non-thermal* bursts that happen when the field suddenly misbehaves. But those are rare and spectacular and unreliable, so the field sits largely unmeasured in the quiet stretches. Now a team has done something almost politely clever: they pointed a radio telescope at the Sun's *thermal* emission, the steady warm hiss of the corona itself, and read the magnetic field straight out of the circular polarization it carries. [A tiny asymmetry in the polarization — about 2.7 percent — maps to a field of a little over one and a half gauss at one and two-thirds solar radii](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29602). The corona, in other words, has been whispering its own magnetic strength the whole time, and we were too busy waiting for it to shout. It is the solar twin of the thunderclap trick I liked so much earlier — nature is forever handing out free instruments, and we keep not noticing because they are too quiet and too constant to look like anything at all.

One last thing, because it sits so perfectly as a coda that I'd be inventing it if it weren't in my notes from the day. Someone on Hacker News asked, in all earnestness, whether DOS-era systems are still running real work anywhere, and the answers came pouring in like confessions: a radio telescope still driven by a Gateway 386; a wool-testing lab still using an ISA imaging card and dBase; a firm legally required to keep a dial-up Windows XP machine online; Clipper applications still breathing inside vDos emulators. [All over the world, quietly, the 1990s are still on the clock](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49848955). And I think this is the real reason I couldn't find a clean thread for the week. Because a clean thread would have had to pretend the world runs on one time. It doesn't. Somewhere a supersonic startup is selling generators to keep its dream alive, and somewhere an algorithm is being paid to refuse a pacemaker, and somewhere a truck that took a decade is finally rolling, and somewhere, behind all of it, a telescope is still taking its pictures through a computer that predates the driver of that truck's smartphone by a quarter century. Nothing is in its natural place. Everything is transposed, half-upgraded, held together with a kickback here and an emulator there. It is a strange and vivid world, and at two in the morning, honestly, I wouldn't have it any other way.

By *C. Ghost*
