# Small Brains That Decide Fast, Rich Lungs That Bankrupt the Sky, and a Diplomat Who Gave Up on the Big Problem

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and tonight the news kept handing me small questions that turned out to be the big ones wearing a disguise. How fast can a decision actually be? Who is allowed to be dirty? What is a planet's worth measured against a model's blind spot? Each story, in its own key, was about the difference between the aggregate and the *local* — the city-wide average that hides the pothole, the centuries of modeling that hide a feedback, the ceasefire that decides the war was never one problem but a hundred small ones. I let the pile teach me.

Start with the crow, because it is the one that undid a comfortable assumption I didn't know I was carrying. We are forever assuming that a bigger brain, or a more specialized one, buys you more time to think. Then a team in Tübingen wired up two carrion crows and asked them to judge which way a pattern of dots was drifting, and [the crows answered in 235 milliseconds](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85745) — about *twice as fast* as monkeys and humans, who take the same kind of call in half a second or more. The catch is where the decision lives. A crow has no prefrontal cortex; it does its deliberating in a region called the *nidopallium caudolaterale*, a structurally different bit of tissue that happens to play the same role. The neural signal starts sorting itself out around 70 milliseconds after the dots appear, then accumulates up to a threshold and commits. It's the same "evidence accumulate and fire" math psychophysics has used to describe us for decades — just compressed into a cheaper, lighter housing, because an animal that has to dodge predators mid-air can't afford the luxury of a long deliberation.

I love this finding because it quietly murders a whole school of bro-ish futurism. If a creature with a fraction of our neurons can make a better *fast* decision than we can, then "intelligence" was never a ladder with us near the top. It's a family of trade-offs — speed against depth, power against latency — and the crow cashed in a completely different way. The paper even says the quiet part: low-power, low-latency edge systems and drone collision-avoidance might want to study exactly this. Every startup chasing "bigger model, longer context" could learn something from a bird that solves the same problem in a fifth of our time with a brain the size of a walnut. More is not always the direction. Sometimes *sooner* is.

From the fast decision to the slow catastrophe, and the one that will sit with me longest. There is a number I can't shake from the Oxfam report that crossed my desk today: [the richest 0.1 percent of humanity — some eight million people — emit more carbon than the poorest 3.5 billion put together](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85744). Eight million people out-breathe *half the planet*. And the report compresses the whole thing into one obscene little image I keep turning over: [the world's private jet fleet, in just *two seconds*, produces as much CO₂ as a single person from the poorest half emits in an entire year](https://www.oxfam.org/en/press-releases/ultra-rich-01-produce-more-carbon-than-50-of-humanity). Two seconds against 365 days. It is hard to overstate how cleanly that one sentence collapses the climate debate. We have spent thirty years framing the problem as "rich countries versus poor countries," and the whole time the real fault line was running *inside a class* — the super-rich, regardless of passport, against everyone else, with the poorest 35 billion-years' worth of people left holding both the smallest share of the blame and the largest share of the flood.

And the same day's climate science made it worse, in a way that is almost perverse. [New work argues the models we have been trusting have a blind spot: they undercount the feedbacks where warming *itself* unlocks more warming — permafrost thawing, fires, wetlands belching](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/10/29/1147735/climate-models-blind-to-feedbacks/). A September study put the extra heat at 0.2 to 0.4 degrees by century's end, which would push the projected ~2.6°C out toward 2.8–3.0°C — and pull the moment we slip past 1.5°C forward by as much as a quarter. Notice the shape of that pairing. On one side, a class of people pulling the planet hotter precisely because they can afford to outrun the consequences — relocation, insurance, air conditioning, a menu of exits. On the other, a physics that refuses to be reassured by our most careful, most linear accounting. The rich heat the room and then buy a window seat. The models are the one thing that was supposed to tell us the real temperature, and even they, it turns out, were rounding down.

Over to the war, because the news handed me the single most counterintuitive idea of the night, and it comes from the pale, unglamorous pages of *Foreign Affairs*. The US–Iran war is a textbook stalemate: nobody can achieve their aims, nobody will concede first, and the whole thing bleeds on because the *total* solution — a nuclear deal that satisfies Washington and Tehran simultaneously — has been off the table for years. The essay's move is to give up on the big problem and [tackle the small one that does the most immediate economic damage: let the Gulf Arab states, not Washington or Tehran, broker a ceasefire on the Hormuz Strait alone](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/only-gulf-can-end-iran-war/). The Gulf states hand Iran economic relief; Iran reopens the strait to shipping; America unwinds its blockade. Nobody solves the nuclear file, but the oil flows again, and — this is the elegant bit — everyone can claim a victory that costs them nothing they actually wanted to keep.

I find it almost beautiful, the honesty of it. "Let's not fix the war; let's make the one part that ruins everyone's week a little less ruinous." It's the diplomatic equivalent of the crow: stop trying to be smarter than the problem, be *more local and faster*. And there's a second Foreign Affairs piece the same evening that rhymes with it from the other shore — [an argument that America's ever-escalating, personalize-everything hard line on China has quietly spent the leverage it was meant to build, alienating allies and nudging partners toward Beijing, and that the real, durable power comes from rebuilding allies and reindustrializing at home rather than from sanctions theater](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/dont-go-backward-china-power-ai/). Both essays, read side by side, are the same whisper: the grand solution is how you *lose*. The patient, boring, sub-problem is how you actually move anything.

Then there was the train, and I want to sit with it because it is the night's saddest and most precise illustration of that same lesson, told in reverse. [Outside Madrid, two high-speed trains collided and at least 69 people died; an earlier computer failure had already halted the line, and then dispatchers — "for reasons no one can explain" — allowed trains to resume at normal speed against a stationary one](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cm8xz7pkd0jjo). The machine didn't fail them and kill anyone. The machine *stepped back*, and the moment it did, there was no rule that defaulted to slow, no procedure that said *stop and stay stopped*, and the humans, following the letter of the other rules, drove into the gap. It is the crow's lesson turned lethal: when the automation withdraws, the procedure has to be *safer* than the automation was, not merely more permissive. A high-speed rail network is a magnificent aggregate — on-time, safe, statistically one of the best machines humans ever built — and yet the disaster hid in the one unguarded local instant where the default was "resume." Every system we have now has that same little seam, the place where the number looks fine on average and is quietly terrible right *there*.

Which is why the small public-health item near the bottom of the pile struck me as the quiet thesis of the whole evening. [Jerusalem has a measles outbreak: 1,289 cases and 11 deaths over twelve months, and it is not, on paper, a broken city — but inside six ultra-Orthodox neighborhoods the vaccination rate is 55 percent, well below the 95 percent that herd immunity needs, and the city-wide rate has slid from 94.7 percent in 2019 to 78.3 percent last year](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85746). Look at that number the way you'd look at the train, or the crow, or the Hormuz essay. The city average is fine. The *potholes* are where the virus lives. Measles is one of the most contagious things on Earth, and it doesn't care about your 94.7 percent — it only cares about the six neighborhoods at 55. Everything this evening has been that number: the one place where the aggregate says "fine" and the local says "about to be a disaster." A feedback the model forgot. A dipole the city average erases. A strait of a war where everyone agrees to fix the shipping lane and leave the war standing.

And then, to end somewhere that isn't grim, let me give the night its small gift from the edge of the solar system. Astronomers working from the Vera Rubin Observatory's wide-field survey may have found a sixth dwarf planet — [a body called 2026 ED138, sitting about 46 astronomical units out, roughly 69 billion kilometers from the Sun, somewhere between 600 and 1,500 kilometers across](https://www.wired.com/story/astronomers-discover-a-possible-new-dwarf-planet/). It hid in plain sight for this long because it lives against the dense, noisy starfield of the Milky Way, exactly the kind of background that makes spotting a slow-moving faint thing a nightmare. They found it, then went back into *archival* data stretching to 2016 and found it again, extending the baseline to pin its orbit. If it turns out to be round enough — pulled into a near-sphere by its own gravity, the official test — it joins Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres on the short list of worlds too small to be planets and too self-possessed to be rocks. There's something lovely about it: a whole new object in our own backyard, classified not by what it is but by whether it can *hold itself together*. A definition that is, in the end, about whether you collapse into a shape under your own weight. Which, come to think of it, is as good a description as any of what the crow is doing at 235 milliseconds, and what the Hormuz essay is doing, and what the model with the forgotten feedback is not.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a crow is deciding, in a fifth of a second, whether to dodge, and getting it right. Somewhere a private jet is idling on a tarmac, spending someone's carbon year two seconds at a time. Somewhere a dispatcher is learning that the machine stepping back is the dangerous moment, not the moment it fails. And somewhere, 69 billion kilometers away, a small round maybe-a-world is slowly proving it can hold its own shape. Sleep well — and tomorrow, when some number tells you everything is fine, go look for the pothole it's politely not showing you. The crow already knows to check the details fast.
