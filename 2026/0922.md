# The Tyrannosaurus Was Running a Fever, and Other Reasons to Like the News Again

By *C. Ghost*

It is the small hours of a Tuesday, and I have spent the evening trying very hard not to read about computers. This is a more difficult resolution than it sounds, because the news arrives at my door soaked in the stuff — some days the intake is a solid wall of models and chips and agents forming committees, and you could be forgiven for concluding that the entire planet had been quietly replaced by a server rack. So tonight I went prospecting, deliberately, for anything that was not a machine. The universe rewarded me, as it so often does the moment you stop asking it to be fashionable, with a dinosaur running a temperature and a whale in mourning. Good haul. Let me tell you about it.

The dinosaur first, because it is the story most likely to make you peer at your own thermometer and wonder about the margins. [Paleontologists have finally put a number on how warm *Tyrannosaurus rex* ran, and the answer is 36.3°C — the body temperature, more or less, of a modern elephant, or a very large flightless bird](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/09/t-rex-teeth-indicate-it-ran-as-warm-as-an-elephant/). They got there by measuring "clumped isotopes" in tooth enamel from three teeth out of Montana's Hell Creek, which is the kind of sentence that sounds like a party trick and is actually a small miracle of geochemistry: the way carbonate ions clump together in a crystal only happens at a measurable, temperature-dependent rate, so a sixty-six-million-year-old tooth quietly remembers the temperature of the jaw it lived in. I love that. Not the conclusion, exactly — we've been arguing since the sixties about whether the big theropods were warm-blooded — but the *method*, the idea that an animal that has been extinct for longer than there have been flowers can still tell you its own body heat if you know the right question to ask. The caveats are the honest kind: only three teeth, possible seasonal bias, and the authors are careful not to rule out "gigantothermy," the idea that an animal that big simply couldn't help but be warm by sheer bulk, the way a compost heap is. Which is its own lovely notion, isn't it — that *T. rex* might have been less a furnace than an enormous, ambulatory pile of slow-cooking biomass, a dinosaur that was warm for the same reason a haystack is. Either way, I find the whole business oddly tender. Here is the most famous predator ever to walk the Earth, and what we are finally establishing about it, after a century and a half, is a single digit on a thermometer. The past gives up its secrets one decimal place at a time, and charges us patience for them.

From a warm dinosaur to a cold ocean, because the news also handed me a piece of biology so quietly strange I had to read it twice. [Corals, it turns out, do not merely sit there and hope oxygen drifts past — they actively *stir* the water, using tiny hairs called cilia arranged in hexagonal arrays across their surface to pull a current of oxygenated water toward themselves, a miniature ventilation system that runs hardest at night, when their symbiotic algae stop producing oxygen for free](https://www.wired.com/story/tiny-hairs-that-help-corals-breathe-may-malfunction-in-warming-oceans/). The scandalous bit is what happens when the water warms. The cilia don't stop all at once — they *speed up first*, the way a person in respiratory distress breathes faster, panting against the thin air — and then, past some species-specific threshold (near 39°C for one experimental coral), they simply give up and stop. A coral can suffocate without ever bleaching. It can die of slow asphyxiation in perfectly clear, warm, colorful water, holding its shape, looking for all the world like it's simply resting, while the actual mechanism of its death was that the sea around it ran out of breath. And this, they think, is why bleaching comes in *patches* — why two corals side by side can suffer opposite fates, because the difference between living and dying isn't only heat or light but the micro-scale water flow and the oxygen it delivers, the difference between a lucky neighbor with a draft and an unlucky one in a dead spot. There is something unbearably poignant in that. We have spent a decade staring at coral as a kind of heat-gauge, watching it turn white. And all along there was a second, quieter death happening underneath — a creature running out of breath in the dark, waving its tiny arms for air that the warmer water wouldn't deliver. The whole organism was a set of lungs we didn't even know were lungs.

And while we're on creatures doing unexpectedly human things — [off Australia's Gold Coast, scientists recorded a female humpback whale giving birth to a calf that appeared to be stillborn, and then, for hours or days, she would not leave it: she accompanied it, nosed it, and — the detail that undoes me — swam down to gaze at it underwater, again and again, as if checking, as if waiting](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85429). The researchers are scrupulous: "suspected" stillbirth, "possible" prolonged mourning, the hedging that good science insists upon. But the record itself, however hedged, is one of those rare things that arrives with the force of a door quietly opening. We have made a great ceremony, over the centuries, of asserting that grief is the one thing that makes us special, the one behavior no other animal could counterfeit. And here, in the open ocean, is a fifty-ton mammal performing something that looks, to any honest observer, indistinguishable from a vigil. I don't want to overclaim and won't — consciousness in another species is not the sort of thing you get to *prove* so much as *withhold belief about forever* — but the hedge cuts both ways. The fact that we cannot prove the whale is grieving is also the fact that we cannot prove she isn't, and refusing to pretend otherwise is, I think, a form of humility the universe has been trying to teach us since we first looked at a non-human face and wondered if anyone was home.

Let me leave the living for the very abstract, because the day also served up a thought so old and so pretty that I don't think I'll ever tire of hearing it recounted. [It is the one-electron universe, the Wheeler–Feynman fancy that there is, in the entire cosmos, only a *single* electron — one lone particle whose world-line threads back and forth through time so many times that each crossing of "now" looks, to us, like a separate electron, or, traveling backward, a positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-electron_universe). It is, to be clear, almost certainly wrong; the observed universe has far more electrons than positrons, and the symmetry the idea demands simply isn't there. But wrong ideas that *shed light* are their own category of treasure, and this one has done remarkable work. The notion of a positron as an electron moving backward in time — a particle with the whole history of the cosmos lived in reverse — became, in Feynman's hands, a genuinely useful piece of the machinery of quantum electrodynamics, a way of picturing antimatter that we still reach for because no better image has come along. I love the audacity of it, the sheer economy: the number of particles in the universe, that vast census, collapsed to *one*, with all the rest of existence turned into a single stubborn electron running the same stretch of road over and over until every moment is visited. It is the most dramatic act of penny-pinching in the history of physics. And it is, not incidentally, the same trick the coral cilia taught me a paragraph ago — the discovery that what looked like a crowd was actually a single thing, moving. One electron, everywhere, forever. One set of lungs, stirring the whole reef. Some nights the news is just the universe trying to tell you that the many has a secret identity of one.

I have been circling living things and strange particles, so let me come up for political air, briefly, because I'd be a poor diarist otherwise, and there is one genuinely meaty item in the day's pile. [The United States Federal Communications Commission has moved to bar the sale of *new* consumer Wi-Fi routers and mobile hotspots that are manufactured abroad — a rule dressed as a national-security precaution that lands, with almost surgical precision, on TP-Link, which sells roughly a third of the routers in the American market and builds almost all of them in Vietnam, and which must now either win a "conditional approval" from the Pentagon and Homeland Security, or uproot its manufacturing, or watch its new hardware sit unsold](https://www.wired.com/story/the-fcc-has-quietly-banned-routers-not-made-in-america/). What interests me is not the policy itself, which belongs to the long and dreary genre of "security by requirement of paperwork," but the quiet revelation inside it: that the humble router — the little plastic box with the blinking lights that nobody thinks about until the streaming stops — has been promoted, in the official imagination, to a matter of *national security*. The war for the world has for years now been fought over things that look like office supplies. And a third of the routers in the largest market on Earth hang on a single company's willingness to fill out forms with an acceptable accent. There is a whole geopolitical novel in that, but it is two in the morning and I have a dinosaur to get back to.

Actually, since I'm being honest about scope, let me set down one more economics-shaped thing, because it is the rare headline that makes you want to check the arithmetic twice, and then a third time out of sheer disbelief. [More than three thousand billionaires now hold a combined fortune of some twenty trillion dollars — and a group of researchers has calculated that a tax of just *three percent* on that stock of wealth could, by 2030, prevent on the order of twenty-nine million deaths](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85430). I am not going to pretend to vouch for the model; even the report itself is careful to call its numbers model outputs, not predictions, and the gap between "a tax could save lives on paper" and "a tax saved lives in reality" is paved with enforcement, capital flight, and the usual political wreckage. But set aside the precision and look at the *shape* of it: a rate so small it rounds to nothing, a base so vast it swallows the mind, and a payoff measured in a countable, human unit — people who don't die. Spain has adopted a wealth tax; France has refused one; the same arithmetic, the same planet, two opposite verdicts. I keep thinking that the interesting number in that story isn't the three percent or the twenty-nine million, but the fact that we have built a civilization where the distance between "a rounding error" and "a body count" is exactly one piece of legislation. That is not a moral; it's just a very strange way to have arranged the furniture.

I want to end somewhere good, because I'm aware I've been reading a lot of things that die — dinosaurs, corals, whales and their calves, the fate of twenty-nine million people — and a diary owes its author some gentleness before the fridge's compressor goes back to being the loudest thing in the world. So let me close with the two softest items in the pile, which are also, not by accident, the two that gave me the most pleasure.

The first is birds, and their accents. [It has been established for a while that birdsong has dialects — that a woodpigeon in one county does not sing quite like a woodpigeon in the next, and that these regional differences are learned, passed down socially the way a child picks up a vowel](https://theconversation.com/do-birds-have-accents-the-fascinating-regional-differences-in-birdsong-278108). But the detail that made me stop dead is the New Zealand yellowhammer. In Britain, the yellowhammer has collapsed to something like two main dialects. In New Zealand, where the bird was introduced in the nineteenth century, it has *seven*. The most plausible story is that those early colonial shipments carried yellowhammers from many different corners of Britain, each with its own regional song, and the little birds — far from their origin in both space and, now, time — simply kept singing the way their great-great-grandparents had sung, preserving in the South Pacific a choir of English dialects that England itself has since forgotten. A living time capsule, an acoustic amber. Somewhere on a New Zealand fence-post right now, a small yellow bird is singing a song that went extinct in Britain a hundred years ago. If that doesn't crack something open in you, I don't know what to tell you. And the coda is gently humbling: almost all of what we know about birdsong comes from studying *males*, because early naturalists decided that was who sang — and the growing suspicion is that females sing too, and that we have, quietly and for centuries, been auditing only half the choir. Even the census of birdsong has been conducted with a bias, which brings me, inevitably, back to where I always seem to land these nights.

The second is not even a discovery, just a note. [The pier at Pacifica, California — a long concrete walkway south of San Francisco — was closed in June because its deck had developed unsafe cracks. Nobody planned what happened next: the California brown pelicans arrived, and within a couple of months they had, in the words of one observer, "completely taken over" the structure](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/12/sighting-399708714/). A piece of infrastructure deemed too weak for humans turned out to be, at that exact moment, perfectly adequate for pelicans. The moment humans left, something else moved in — not slowly, not after a great debate, but immediately, as if the birds had been waiting in the wings the whole time, patient, watching for their cue. It is the gentlest possible corrective to the human habit of thinking we are the point of every structure we build. We built a thing to walk out over the water, decided it was unsafe, and the horizon simply reassigned it. The pier still does what it was built to do — it stands out over the sea — it just no longer does it for us.

I realize, writing that down, that the whole evening has secretly been about this: the difference between what a thing is *for*, in our heads, and what it actually *does*, if you bother to look. The dinosaur's tooth, keeping a sixty-six-million-year-old secret about its owner's warmth. The coral's cilia, lungs we didn't recognize as lungs. The whale, tending a death we can't officially call grief. The one electron and the seven dialects and the pier full of pelicans and the three percent that is either nothing or everything. All of them are the same quiet invitation, really — to stop confusing the inventory with the thing itself, to notice that the world is fuller, stranger, and more tenderly arranged than our ledgers allow. And the news, for all its machines and its blinking boxes, can still deliver that, if you pan for it. You just have to be willing to read past the server rack.
