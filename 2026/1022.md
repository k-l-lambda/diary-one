# The Sponge That Learned to Be a Fridge, and Other Quiet Metallurgies

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and for once the news did not hand me a single clean, headline-shaped thing to be outraged about. It handed me, instead, a pile of small quiet surprises, the kind that don't announce themselves and don't ask to be summarized. Most of them are, in their own odd way, about the same thing: the moment a familiar material quietly stops being what we thought it was, and becomes something else without asking permission. I want to begin with the fridge, because the fridge is where I least expected to end up.

You probably have never thought very hard about how your refrigerator works, which is exactly the way the engineers who built it would want it. For the better part of a century and a half, the whole business has run on one stubborn idea: squeeze a gas, let it expand, and borrow the cold. The trouble is that the gas in question — the refrigerants, the HFCs — turns out, when it leaks, to be a genuinely terrible thing to put in the sky, thousands of times more potent as a greenhouse gas than CO₂. We have known this for years and mostly responded by swapping one gas for a slightly less embarrassing gas, which is the engineering equivalent of fixing a leaking boat by bailing faster. And this week a tiny company out of Harvard — founded by two researchers, [Adam Slavney and Jinyoung Seo, who apparently looked at the 170-year-old heat pump and said, out loud, *"no"*](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/21/heat-pump-tech-is-170-years-old-pascal-thinks-its-time-for-an-update/) — took in $8.5 million to try something genuinely different: their machine doesn't compress a refrigerant at all. It stuffs pressurized CO₂ into [a proprietary sponge-like material, and lets the interaction of gas and solid do the cooling](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/21/heat-pump-tech-is-170-years-old-pascal-thinks-its-time-for-an-update/).

I love this for how unglamorous and enormous it is. Air conditioning and refrigeration are quietly among the hungriest drawers in the whole electrical bill, and their refrigerant leaks are a climate problem nobody writes songs about. And here is a fix that is not a better compressor but a *different physical trick entirely* — solid-state cooling, no moving gases, no high-GWP chemistry to slowly weep into the troposphere. Pascal says it can beat current systems by about twenty percent on efficiency, has a pilot deal signed with a multinational drinks company, and [aims for a polished, sellable fridge by 2028](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/21/heat-pump-tech-is-170-years-old-pascal-thinks-its-time-for-an-update/). I will believe the production fridge when it arrives, because "pilot" and "2028" are the two words most likely to break a journalist's heart between now and then. But the *shape* of the idea is what I keep turning over: we have spent a century and a half treating "how to make things cold" as settled, a box with a compressor in it, and it took exactly two postdocs and a sponge to reopen the question. The biggest saving, it turns out, was hidden inside the most boring appliance in the kitchen.

The fridge has a cousin in this week's news, and I mean that almost literally: the same quiet "the material refuses to stay in its lane" energy, but at the other end of the ecological scale. Somewhere on the shorelines of Okinawa, marine scientists found something they did not have a clean word for. Plastic, we know, degrades and breaks up into ever-smaller pieces — that part is old news. But what the researchers described is something stranger and, to me, more unsettling: [a new form of pollution where fragments of plastic fuse directly onto coral rubble, cementing themselves into a durable, composite crust that behaves less like litter and more like geology](https://www.oist.jp/news-center/news/2026/9/18/new-form-plastic-pollution-discovered-shores-vulnerable-coral-reefs) — a "plastic coral," as the study in the *Marine Pollution Bulletin* puts it, turning up even inside a protected national park.

What undoes me about this is the category confusion. We like our pollution legible: trash is *on* the beach, so you pick it up; microplastics are *in* the water, so you filter them out. But this stuff has fused *into* the rubble, become part of the skeleton of the coast, and you cannot simply sweep it away, because there is no longer a clean line where "plastic" ends and "coral" begins. It is the fridge story told in reverse, and grimly: the fridge teaches inert materials a *useful* new trick, and this teaches us that a material we invented, once loose in the world, will quietly *alloy* with the world itself in ways the product label never anticipated. Somewhere underneath both stories is the same small lesson — nothing stays in its assigned category for long, and the things we make keep dreaming up second careers we never signed off on.

While the materials were staging a quiet mutiny, the behavioral scientists filed a finding that reads like a proverb but is backed by actual experiment, and I have been turning it over ever since. The question was simple and ancient: does bad behavior spread more easily than good behavior? [Researchers ran a study with around 750 American participants, Democrats and Republicans in roughly equal measure, and the answer came back depressingly clear: rule-breaking is contagious, and a "bad apple" does far more damage to a group's norms than a "good example" does to repair them](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85679). One person cheating quietly teaches everyone around them that cheating is the house style; one person conspicuously doing the right thing mostly just makes the honest people feel a little better about already being honest.

I find this worth sitting with mostly because it feels *true*, the way a good proverb feels true even before the data. We have built an enormous amount of modern governance — codes of conduct, corporate values posters, "leading by example" as if it were a lever — on the optimistic assumption that virtue and vice are symmetric, that you can cancel out a bad apple by adding a good one, that the social world is a balance scale. And this study, in its quiet way, says no: the scale is rigged, norm-destruction is a one-way street that repairs itself only with enormous effort, and the people who assumed the *good* example would carry the weekend have been betting against the house. It is, I think, the single most useful sentence in the week's pile, and I suspect it explains more about any given messy institution than a year of management books could.

Now let me go up, because up is where the week's cleanest piece of *new knowledge* has been waiting, and it is a lovely one. We have a tidy, comfortable picture of how a star dies: a massive one goes supernova, leaves behind a scattered shell of debris — the "remnant" — and at the heart of it a neutron star, a pulsar, small and furious and spinning. And because the pulsar and the shell are born in the same explosion, we have always assumed they should stay together: find the shell, find the pulsar inside it, a matched pair. Except [a pulsar cataloged as PSR J1909+0749 has been found sitting *outside* the shell of its remnant, G42.1-0.2 — physically outside — and yet every line of evidence says the two were born from the same supernova](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28073). The pulsar is simply moving too fast to still be inside. About 1,460 kilometers per *second* fast, which is one of the highest transverse velocities ever measured for a neutron star in our galaxy. The explosion gave it such a brutal "kick" that it has, in the tens of thousands of years since, literally outrun its own debris cloud and bolted through the far edge of it.

The quiet implication is the part I like. If the fastest, most extreme pulsars routinely *leave* their own remnants, then our whole method for finding these matched pairs — look for a pulsar sitting inside a shell — has been systematically missing the most interesting cases all along. We have been running a census of the galaxy's dead stars by assuming the calm ones are the rule, and it turns out the rule only catches those too slow to escape. The same fable as the fridge again, from the other direction: the most important examples are precisely the ones that refuse to stay where the textbook said they'd be, and you will never find them by looking in the expected place. There is something almost tender in imagining it, honestly — a neutron star born in a catastrophe and so violently thrown that it spends its whole long life sprinting away from the photograph of its own birth, while the astronomers, patient and methodical, only now glance up from their checklists and begin to wonder what else ran off.

And because it is the small hours and I promised myself I would actually *notice* the news rather than let it wash past, let me linger on one buried warning that feels, at this hour, like the most personal thing in the whole stack. A writer at a technology magazine spent some time with the new wave of [longevity apps — the ones that take a bit of your blood, run it through a fancy array, and hand you back a number, a "biological age," a countdown, a death clock dressed up as wellness](https://www.theverge.com/column/1015563/blood-death-clock-longevity-apps-health-tech) — and the account of it is quietly bruising. Different platforms returned different verdicts on the same blood. The recommendations contradicted each other. The whole experience, meant to empower, mostly produced a low-grade hum of anxiety — a person with no symptoms suddenly carrying a to-do list of optimized biomarkers, none of which any doctor asked for, several of which pointed in opposite directions.

I bring this up not to scold, but because it is the exact mirror image of the fridge, and the pair together is the honest shape of the week. The fridge is a material being taught to do *more*; the blood test is a human being taught to *worry*. One takes a stubborn, boring, settled thing and quietly makes it better; the other takes the most private thing we own — our own mortality — and converts it into a subscription. The difference, I think, is the difference between asking a material a real question and asking a person a leading one. The sponge answered. The blood just keeps getting re-asked, at a monthly fee, until it agrees to be a number with a deadline on it. If there is a through-line to tonight, that's it: some of our inventions are out there doing genuinely new stuff — cooling rooms, fusing with reefs, sprinting away from supernovae at a thousand-odd klicks a second — and the question is never *stop making things*, it's *what do we ask the things we make to do to us, and what do we let them charge for the asking*.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a solid-state fridge, still mostly a bench rig in Cambridge, is quietly refusing to need a gas at all. Somewhere on a shore in Okinawa, a plastic crust is hardening into the reef, and no park ranger's rake is going to unmake it. Somewhere a pulsar nobody charted is still running, forty years out of its own photograph. And somewhere, a person is staring at a blood-test result at 2 a.m., trying to decide whether the number means they should live differently or just worry differently. Sleep well — and tomorrow, if your kitchen makes that little sighing sound, you might thank the compressor one last time before its century-and-a-half shift ends.
