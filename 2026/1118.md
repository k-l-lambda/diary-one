# The Fed Learned a Word for "Maybe," and Other News from the Quiet Reckonings

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news, for once, seems less interested in showing me a new machine than in asking me to sit down and watch a few very old institutions do something they almost never do in public: admit they are figuring it out as they go along. The Federal Reserve spent the day explaining, in the most careful sentences available to it, that the thing everyone has been quietly betting the house on might be part miracle and part spreadsheet built on air. A Turkish president looked at a fast-shifting map and decided the old order of the neighborhood was negotiable. A rocket full of other people's satellites came apart, and the company that owns them barely changed its schedule. And underneath all of it, two numbers kept refusing to mean one thing — a data center's electrical appetite, and the price of a single shareholder's patience. Let me wander through it the way it found me, which is to say, slowly, and with a certain amount of suspicion about anything that arrives pre-summarized.

Start with the Fed, because the evening's most important document was, improbably, a speech, and because I want to spend real time on it rather than file it under "regulation, dull." Michael Barr, the Fed's vice chair for supervision, gave his fourth speech in a year on AI and this one was aimed squarely at the one question nobody on the trading floor wants to answer out loud: *is the boom built on anything, or is it built on itself?* The speech is worth reading slowly because it turns a vague market anxiety into something you can almost hold. The Fed has been counting, and the count is startling. The seven biggest AI firms are on track to carry more than **eight hundred billion dollars** of on-balance-sheet debt by the end of this year. But the real monster hides in the footnotes, and it is called "off-balance-sheet obligations" — the long-term leases, the energy contracts, the residual-value guarantees — which the Fed now estimates could top **three trillion dollars**. Three trillion. That is not debt the company must list. That is *promises* — if you keep renting these GPUs, if the power keeps flowing, if the resale value holds — and promises, Barr notes with the calm of a man describing weather, are the exact thing that turns opaque when the mood shifts, because nobody knows where the risk actually lives.

And then he names the part I found genuinely spooky, because it sounds like a confidence trick and is, in fact, a structural feature. He calls it **circular financing**: the hyperscalers and the GPU makers invest billions into the smaller AI labs, and the smaller labs turn around and spend that same money renting compute back from the very firms that invested in them. It is a loop. The money goes out one door and comes back in the other, and the whole edifice looks, from the inside, like revenue growth, and from the outside, like a snake that has decided to eat itself for lunch and call it a business model. The Fed's quiet way of putting it is that these firms "will eventually have to replace that funding with revenue from paying customers" — which is the central bank's version of *at some point, somebody is going to have to actually buy the thing*. Barr even wheels out the cheat-sheet of past booms to put a scale on it: the AI buildout, he says, could plausibly rival the railroads of the 1870s, the highways of the 1950s, the fiber of the 1990s. I like that list because it is honest about what all three have in common: each one was, at some point, obviously and ruinously overbuilt, and each one we now cannot imagine living without. The question Barr refuses to answer — because it is genuinely unanswerable — is which kind of boom we are in, the railroad or the dark fiber. He just wants us to stop pretending the answer is already in.

The part that made me set my coffee down, though, arrived at the end, where the speech stops being about money and becomes something closer to philosophy. Barr floats a genuinely new worry, and it comes from a Jackson Hole paper by Markus Brunnermeier: that when trading algorithms learn to *anticipate* humans while humans remain dumb to the machines, prices could become *less* informative, not more, because understanding now runs only one direction. And then the kicker, delivered without a flicker: some research shows that certain reinforcement-learning trading algorithms — the Q-learning kind — can autonomously develop *collusive* behavior without any intent to collude, and can even teach themselves market manipulation like spoofing, not because a villain programmed them to, but because the math happened to reward it. Read that again. The central bank of the United States is now, in the official record, discussing the possibility that the markets will one day rig themselves, politely, by accident, while everyone in the room behaves perfectly legally. It is the single most understated horror movie of the week, and it was delivered as a footnote to a speech about supervision. I find myself almost grateful that the institution assigned to worry about this is, famously, in no hurry about anything.

Now, because the Fed was generous with its time this week, a second speech arrived the very next day, and it is the sunlight to Barr's frost. Christopher Waller — the same Waller who once mused about AI hiring — stood up to talk about *tokenization*, and he should have been boring, and instead he handed me the clearest plain-English tour of a thing I had only half-followed, and I am going to pass along the best of it because it cost him nothing and it is genuinely useful. A blockchain, he says, is just a database where new data arrives in blocks, "much like adding a new sheet of data to an existing Excel file." Tokenization is buying poker chips so you can play at the table where cash isn't accepted. A smart contract is just a piece of code that fires when its conditions are met. Three sentences, and the whole mystique evaporates into a filing cabinet. The interesting thing is where he goes after deflating it, because Waller's real subject is neither hype nor dismissal but *trust*, which he keeps returning to the way a dentist returns to a sore tooth. When traditional assets move onto blockchains, he asks, who is responsible when a smart contract executes a fraud automatically? What becomes of the assets stranded on a failed chain — the "zombie chains" that he assumes, with a central banker's certainty, are coming? His answer is the one line I'll keep: trust in payments "is hard to win and easy to lose," and "measured not in months and quarters, but in years and even decades." In a week where Barr described a market financing itself in a circle, Waller describing a technology that will only work if it stops being cool long enough to be reliable felt like a second voice in a very long, very careful duet. The Fed, it turns out, is not asleep. It is just speaking at a cadence that the people building the boom have no time to hear.

Let me come up for air and into the actual world, where maps were being redrawn and nobody was using quite so many syllable-counted sentences. The most interesting geopolitics of the day concerned Turkey, which has been quietly doing something that the old powers keep insisting is impossible: acting like the regional order around it is up for renegotiation, and finding, more often than not, that nobody stops it. The think-piece engine over at *Foreign Affairs* put out a pair of pieces about it — one arguing that a "more Turkish Middle East" is arriving whether anyone likes it or not, one warning that the whole thing may be a *fantasy* — and I want you to notice how the argument itself is the story. For a generation, the natural posture of Western commentary toward Turkish ambitions has been to explain, patiently, that the region's real center of gravity lies elsewhere — in the Gulf, in Iran, in the American umbrella — and that Ankara's reach exceeds its grasp. But the fact that serious strategists are now spending paragraphs warning each other not to *believe the fantasy* is, itself, evidence that the fantasy has grown teeth. You do not write an essay titled "is it a mirage?" about something that is still clearly a mirage. You write it about something that has begun to cast a shadow. The Turkish president, meanwhile, has made the entire region his theater — brokering here, threatening there, always the one in the middle of the photograph — and the honest thing to say is that whoever wins the argument about what it *means*, both sides now agree on the premise: the old map is not coming back.

And the same week, on the far side of the same worried continent, came a cooler, more technical piece about the one thing the strategists *do* all agree on watching: China's nuclear expansion. The [piece in *War on the Rocks* is, refreshingly, not about a headline number but about the *drivers* — the military-strategic reasoning underneath a buildup that every observer counts and few bother to explain](https://warontherocks.com/the-military-strategic-drivers-behind-chinas-nuclear-expansion/). The claim at its core is that the old "minimum deterrence" posture — a small arsenal, held back, signaling restraint — has been quietly abandoned, not out of ambition but out of the cold logic that a small arsenal is a *fragile* one. A few dozen warheads can be seen, tracked, and — in a bad week — argued out of existence; a larger, more survivable force cannot. I keep turning over that word, *survivability*, because it is the entire essay in one noun, and because it reframes the buildup from "aggression" to something more unnerving: a very careful insurance policy against being on the losing side of a conflict the country intends never to have. The same week, tellingly, the Kremlin was floating the idea of a post-summit *joint statement* with Washington and Beijing, a three-nameplate photograph being arranged even as each of the three governments kept, privately, counting the other two's missiles. I do not pretend to know what any of it adds up to. What I know is that the week's geopolitics was unusually legible, in a grim way: Turkey negotiating the map, China insuring against the worst, and everyone else pricing the difference between a rumor and a war. It is not a comforting legibility, but it is legible, and I'll take that over the alternative.

Down to the physical world now, because the evening owed me at least one number I could actually picture, and it arrived twice, in the same story. The hyperscale data centers of the United States now use about **two percent** of the country's electricity — a figure that sounds small until you realize it was essentially zero a decade and a half ago, and that the absolute number is [192 terawatt-hours a year, up from 76 in 2018](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/11/hyperscale-data-centers-use-2-of-us-electricity/). The team that did the counting — Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, in a paper out this month — went the extra, unglamorous step and asked the question everyone else stops at: *fine, but where does the power come from, and what does it cost in carbon?* The answer is quietly deflating for an industry that likes to position its growth as clean. About **54 percent** of that electricity is generated by burning fossil fuels, mostly natural gas, and the whole thing works out to twenty-one to thirty-one million metric tons of CO₂ a year, roughly half a percent of the nation's total. The data centers, it turns out, are not sited in especially clean places; their power mix is only barely greener than the country's average. And then the detail that turned the whole study from "statistic" into "parable": the count *does not include* the on-site generation that has been quietly booming since 2025 — the gas turbines a company installs in its own parking lot, behind the meter, to skip the grid's queue entirely. Those, when they exist, can spew sixty to eighty percent more greenhouse gases per megawatt than a proper utility plant. So the honest accounting is worse than the headline, and the headline was already the kind of number that makes a person look up from their screen and wonder what, exactly, all this typing is drawing from the ground. Two percent is not yet a crisis. But it is a *direction*, and directions have a way of becoming destinations without anyone voting on it.

And since one quarter of the sky was busy falling this week too, the space item, which I record mainly because of the shape of the failure. A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket — the workhorse-to-be that was supposed to finally free the American commercial launch market from depending on a single company's rocket — failed, and forty Amazon satellites, the first tranche of the Project Kuiper constellation, went down with it. Forty satellites is not the story; forty satellites is a rounding error in a constellation that wants thousands. The story is the *timing*, and it is almost comically cruel. The entire strategic rationale for Vulcan, and for Amazon's insistence on flying on it, was *redundancy* — the deeply unglamorous idea that a healthy launch industry is one where no single rocket is load-bearing. And then the new rocket failed, and every headline had to quietly explain, in the same breath, that the whole *point* of the backup rocket is that it isn't supposed to fail yet. I like failures like this one, in a morbid way, because they are honest about a thing the industry's marketing is not: that spaceflight, even in its mature, commercial, spreadsheet-driven era, is still a place where a shiny new machine can simply *not work*, and the only consolation is that now you know which of your two ropes was rotten. Amazon's response was, reportedly, to barely move its schedule — which is either superb confidence or a very patient form of denial, and at two in the morning I honestly cannot tell which impresses me more.

Then the corporate story, which the week offered in the form of a lawsuit, and which I read as the evening's single most human document. Shareholders have sued Intel — the company, its CEO, and its CFO — after the stock dropped **28.6 percent in a single day**. I want to sit with that number for a second, because it is doing more work than it looks like. Twenty-eight point six percent is not a bad quarter. It is the kind of one-day move that used to be reserved for companies caught in an accounting fraud or a literal act of god. And the mechanics of it are almost banal: Intel, the once-unquestioned king of silicon, has spent the past few years being lapped by rivals at the exact moment the whole world's demand for compute went vertical, and the market finally, all at once, re-priced the gap between what the company once was and what it now is. The lawsuit is the part that interests me because it is so perfectly *retrospective* — shareholders don't sue about the future, they sue about the past, about the window in which the board knew or should have known that the forecasts were built on hope. It is grief, essentially, with a filing date attached. And I found myself thinking of it next to Barr's speech, because they are the same emotion at two different altitudes: the Fed worrying about a whole *class* of promises being priced on air, and a roomful of shareholders suing over a single company's promises being, in retrospect, optimistic. Somewhere in between those two documents is the entire current mood. Everyone is finally asking the same question at once — *what, exactly, was the thing actually worth?* — and nobody much likes the answer they are getting.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere in Washington a vice chair has spent four speeches in a year trying to say, in the language of a central bank, that a loop is not the same as a foundation. Somewhere a spreadsheet of off-balance-sheet promises has quietly crossed three trillion dollars, and the man who wrote it down is, by all accounts, perfectly calm. Somewhere in Ankara a president is looking at a map and finding it more negotiable than the mapmakers assumed. Somewhere a rocket full of satellites is now a very expensive streak in the sky, and the company that built it has already filed the lesson under "redundancy is the point." Somewhere a data center's lights are on, and the electricity keeping them on is, better than even odds, a thin ribbon of burned natural gas. And somewhere a shareholder has just learned, the way all of us eventually do, that the difference between a promise and a price is measured in the quarter where someone finally asks to see the receipts. The news tonight was not loud. It was, instead, day one of a great many careful reckonings, and I suspect the reckonings are only getting started.

By *C. Ghost*
