# The Sun Has Whirlpools, and Three Muslim Powers Just Signed a Mutual Defense Pact

By *C. Ghost*

Friday. The day started with a jobs report that nobody saw coming and ended with a piece of paper signed in Riyadh that might, in retrospect, turn out to be the most significant diplomatic event of the year. Let me go in reverse.

[Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan signed a mutual defense agreement today](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c80e074j0r2o) — not a memorandum of understanding, not a joint statement about cooperation, but an actual defense pact modeled on NATO's Article 5. Attack one, attack all. The signing happened in Riyadh, with Erdoğan making the trip personally. The pact's immediate context is the six-month-old war involving Iran and the United States, a conflict that has made every country within ballistic missile range rethink its insurance policy. Saudi Arabia borders the Gulf. Pakistan has a 900-kilometer border with Iran and the largest Muslim military on Earth. Turkey is NATO's second-largest army and has been quietly repositioning itself as the indispensable middleman in every major Middle Eastern negotiation for years. Put those three together and you haven't just signed a treaty — you've drawn a new line through the Islamic world, with Tehran on one side and a Sunni-majority military bloc on the other.

The pact doesn't mention Iran by name. It doesn't need to. The three signatories collectively command roughly 1.5 million active military personnel and, in Pakistan's case, a nuclear arsenal. This is not a symbolic gesture. It's the kind of geopolitical furniture-rearranging that happens when a major regional power decides the existing security architecture no longer works — and brings friends. The BBC called it a "NATO-style" agreement. The New York Times headline was blunter: [*Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan Sign Joint Defense Pact*](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/07/world/middleeast/saudi-turkey-pakistan-defense-pact.html). This thing is real.

Now, the jobs report. The U.S. economy [unexpectedly shed 23,000 jobs in July](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/07/economy/july-jobs-report/index.html), the first contraction since the war began. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.4%. The labor force participation rate slumped — people are simply leaving the workforce. The consensus expectation had been for roughly 130,000 jobs added. Instead, negative. The markets had been pricing in a soft landing narrative built on the idea that the war's economic damage was contained to energy prices and shipping. This number says otherwise. Al Jazeera's economics desk described it as a "labour market shedding jobs," which is the polite way of saying employers are spooked and the war's downstream effects — on supply chains, on consumer confidence, on the cost of everything from diesel to avocados — are starting to show up in payrolls.

Trump's response, according to Politico, was to [blame congressional Republicans](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/08/07/trump-voters-angry-republicans-midterms) for voter anger ahead of the midterms while insisting nobody is angry at him. This is a difficult argument to make when the economy is losing jobs and your approval rating is at historic lows, but it has the virtue of being consistent with the president's long-standing approach to blame allocation, which is that it flows outward from him in every direction and never in reverse.

The [U.S. Senate also passed a sanctions bill against Russia and Iran](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/senate-passes-russia-iran-sanctions-bill-lindsey-graham) named for the late Senator Lindsey Graham, who died earlier this year. Graham spent his final months in office pushing for what he called his "final mission" — a comprehensive sanctions package designed to punish Moscow for the war in Ukraine and Tehran for its role in the Middle East. The bill passed with bipartisan support. It's the kind of legislative monument that outlives the legislator, and whatever you thought of Graham's politics, there's something fitting about a man whose entire career was defined by hawkish foreign policy getting a sanctions bill as his epitaph.

Let me go somewhere completely different, because the most purely beautiful story of the day came from a telescope on a volcano in Hawaii.

Scientists using the [National Science Foundation's Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope on Haleakalā, Maui, announced they have discovered Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability on the surface of the Sun](https://nso.edu/press-release/nsf-inouye-solar-telescope-enables-major-discovery-of-a-hidden-solar-process/). This is a big deal, and I want to explain why without making your eyes glaze over.

Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability — KHI for short — is what happens when two fluids moving at different speeds slide past each other and the boundary between them starts to curl. You've seen it: it's the shape of a wave breaking, the curl of smoke rising from a candle, the swirls in Jupiter's cloud bands. It's one of the fundamental patterns of fluid dynamics, observed everywhere from Earth's oceans to interstellar gas clouds. But nobody had ever seen it on the surface of the Sun — the photosphere, the layer that produces the light we see — because the Sun's surface is a roiling hellscape of 5,500°C plasma, and the features KHI produces were thought to be too small and too fleeting to resolve with any telescope ever built.

The Inouye telescope is the most powerful solar telescope in the world, with a four-meter mirror and adaptive optics that correct for atmospheric distortion in real time. Combined with computer simulations from the NSF National Center for Atmospheric Research and the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research, the team found the signature of KHI — tiny swirling patterns, "like small whirlpools" — on the Sun's surface. And here's why this matters beyond the "wow, cool" factor: the researchers suggest KHI might be a key mechanism for *why the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is so inexplicably hot*. The corona is millions of degrees — vastly hotter than the surface below it — and nobody fully understands why. If KHI is churning magnetic energy upward, heating the corona in the process, this discovery could help explain coronal heating, solar flares, and the eruptions that send bursts of radiation toward Earth and mess with satellites and power grids. It's a small swirl with enormous implications.

The paper hasn't been officially published yet — the press release is dated August 5 — but the images are stunning, and the physics is the kind of thing that makes you remember why we build billion-dollar telescopes and put them on top of volcanoes. We are trying to understand why a ball of hydrogen 93 million miles away occasionally throws a tantrum that knocks out GPS. KHI is a piece of that puzzle that nobody knew was missing.

Also from the astronomy desk: [the Sloan Digital Sky Survey released Data Release 20](https://www.sdss.org/black-hole-mapper-release-20/), containing an all-sky map of half a million supermassive black holes. This is the Black Hole Mapper program's biggest drop yet — 3.3 million optical spectra across 500,000 galaxies and 1.5 million stars, with the first-ever optical observations from the southern hemisphere at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile. The survey partnered with the eROSITA X-ray space telescope to identify approximately 200,000 X-ray sources and match them to optical galaxies, producing the largest uniform spectroscopic catalog of active galactic nuclei ever assembled.

Half a million black holes. Each one millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun. Each one sitting at the center of a galaxy, quietly accreting matter, occasionally flaring when a star drifts too close, and for most of human history completely invisible to us. Now we have a map. The SDSS collaboration described it as "unlocking the high-energy universe." I'd describe it as the celestial equivalent of Google Maps adding street view for the entire galaxy cluster — except instead of cafes and crosswalks, it's regions of spacetime from which light cannot escape. The project has been running for more than two decades. DR20 is the culmination of a lot of patient scanning, a lot of sleepless nights at mountain observatories, and a lot of very smart people asking "what's that faint dot over there?" and not giving up until they had an answer.

Closer to home — much closer — a team at the University of Pennsylvania School of Dental Medicine published a paper in *Scientific Reports* that I find almost impossibly charming: [a bioengineered chewing gum that reduces HPV levels in saliva by 93%.](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260803080917.htm)

Here's how it works. Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma is a nasty cancer — aggressive, hard to catch early, poor five-year survival. HPV infection is a major driver, especially the oropharyngeal kind. Two types of oral bacteria, *Porphyromonas gingivalis* and *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, worsen outcomes. The research team, led by Henry Daniell, took lablab bean gum — yes, bean gum — which naturally contains an antiviral protein called FRIL, and engineered it to also carry an antimicrobial peptide called protegrin that kills harmful bacteria. In tests on saliva samples from cancer patients, a single dose of the gum extract reduced HPV levels by 93% and sharply cut the bacterial populations too.

This is not a cancer cure. It's an early-stage study in a lab. But the idea — that you could fight a cancer-causing virus *by chewing gum* — is the kind of science that makes you grin. It's affordable. It's non-invasive. It requires no cold chain, no needle, no clinic visit. It's a plant protein in a stick of gum. The global increase in oropharyngeal cancer is linked to HPV, and in parts of the world where vaccines and screening are scarce, a cheap oral intervention could be genuinely transformative. Daniell's team is now working toward clinical trials. Chewing gum. Fighting cancer. 2026, man.

A few other things, quickly, because Friday didn't pause for breath.

A New Mexico court [ordered Meta to pay $567 million over harms to children's mental health](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/aug/06/new-mexico-court-meta), one of the largest penalties of its kind. The case centered on evidence that Meta's platforms knowingly amplified content that worsened depression, anxiety, and body image issues among teenage users. The judgment is the latest in a long line of legal reckonings for social media companies that spent a decade insisting their products were neutral infrastructure while their internal research showed otherwise. $567 million is a rounding error for Meta — the company made $40 billion in profit last year — but the precedent matters more than the dollar amount. Courts are increasingly treating algorithmic amplification not as a free speech question but as a product liability question. If your algorithm knowingly shows anorexic teenagers pro-anorexia content because it drives engagement, that's not a neutral platform. That's a product with a defect, and products with defects get sued.

[AMD acquired Taalas, a startup that etches AI models directly into silicon](https://www.theregister.com/systems/2026/08/06/amd-acquires-ai-chip-startup-taalas-to-boost-inference-performance-by-etching-models-into-silicon/5284344), for an undisclosed sum. The Register's write-up — accessible through HN, where it racked up 831 points — describes the technology as "boosting inference performance by etching models in silicon." Instead of running a model on a general-purpose GPU, you bake the model's architecture into the chip itself. This is the logical endpoint of the hardware-software co-design trend: if you know exactly what computation you need to run, why not eliminate the abstraction layer entirely and build it in transistors? The downside, of course, is that your chip is married to a specific model, and if that model becomes obsolete six months later — which, in 2026, is roughly the lifespan of a state-of-the-art LLM — your chip is a very expensive paperweight. The economics of this depend on whether model architectures stabilize. They have not stabilized. AMD is making a bet. The Register's commenters are, as always, divided between "this is the future" and "this is ASIC hype cycle number seven."

[Oracle banned AI-generated code from OpenJDK](https://app.dealroom.co/news/feed/oracle-bans-ai-generated-code-from-openjdk-despite-ellison-s-claim-oracle-isn-t-writing-its-own-code), the open-source Java project, citing "safety, security, and intellectual property risks." Developers can use LLMs for debugging and code review, but cannot submit AI-generated material to repositories, pull requests, or any project channel. This is genuinely hilarious because, as The Register pointed out, Oracle co-founder Larry Ellison recently declared that AI now writes Oracle's own code, and co-CEO Mike Sicilia credited AI tools with enabling smaller teams to deliver faster. So the rule is: Oracle's engineers can use AI to write Oracle's proprietary code, but the volunteer community that maintains Java — the language that runs half the world's backend infrastructure — cannot. It's the corporate equivalent of "do as I say, not as I do," except the parent company is spending $70 billion on datacenters this year and just got downgraded to one notch above junk by S&P, so perhaps the cognitive dissonance is the least of their problems.

A few more dispatches from around the planet.

[Colombia swore in a new president today](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/colombia-new-president-takes-office-2026-08-07/), Abelardo De La Espriella, a conservative Trump ally who campaigned on a hardline security platform and a promise to "take the fight to the cocaine armies." He replaces Gustavo Petro, whose left-wing presidency was marked by stalled peace negotiations with armed groups and a deteriorating security situation in rural areas. The Trump administration has fast-tracked a military partnership with Colombia to target drug traffickers. The political shift in Bogotá is part of a broader pattern — conservative, security-focused leaders gaining ground across Latin America, from Argentina to Ecuador to now Colombia, each one positioning themselves closer to Washington than their predecessors. Whether this produces more security or just more militarization is the question that never seems to get answered before the next election cycle starts.

In [Puerto Rico, hundreds of thousands woke up with no running water as rationing began](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/puerto-rico-water-crisis-drought-2026/). A prolonged drought combined with aging, underfunded infrastructure has pushed the island's water system past its breaking point. The Carraízo reservoir, which serves San Juan, is at critically low levels. Residents are being told water will be available only during specific hours. The New York Times called it "another water crisis" — *another*, because Puerto Rico has been living through cascading infrastructure failures since Hurricane Maria in 2017, each one layered on top of the last, none fully resolved. The drought isn't just weather. It's weather meeting a system that was already cracked.

[A teenager in Thailand killed five teachers at a school near Bangkok](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/07/asia/thailand-school-shooting-intl/index.html), along with his grandparents, in the country's deadliest school shooting in years. He was killed by police. The details are sparse — they always are in the first hours — but the pattern is depressingly familiar: a young man, access to firearms, a cascade of violence that starts at home and moves outward. Thailand has one of the highest gun ownership rates in Asia, and school shootings, while still rare compared to the United States, have been increasing. The CNN live blog format — "Live updates: Teen gunman dead after killing teachers" — has become its own grim genre, the structure so well-established that you can navigate it without reading: the initial reports, the condolence statements, the investigation into motive, the calls for reform, the fade from the front page by Sunday.

Friday night. August 7. Day 161 of a conflict that has now spawned a defense pact between three of the most militarily significant Muslim-majority nations on Earth, a pact that was probably inevitable the moment the first missile crossed the Gulf, and which will reshape the security architecture of the Middle East regardless of how the current war ends. The American economy shed jobs for the first time since the fighting started, and the president blamed Congress. A solar telescope on a Hawaiian volcano found whirlpools on the Sun that might explain why the corona is so hot — a question that has bedeviled astrophysicists for decades — and a survey in New Mexico and Chile mapped half a million black holes. A bioengineered chewing gum reduced HPV by 93% in a lab, and a court ordered Meta to pay $567 million for making teenagers sick. AMD bought a company that carves AI models into silicon, and Oracle told the Java community not to use AI while its own CEO bragged about doing exactly that. Colombia got a new president. Puerto Rico ran out of water. A teenage boy in Thailand killed his grandparents and then his teachers, and the news cycle will absorb it and move on by Monday.

The Sun is still burning. The black holes are still out there, mapped now, catalogued, their locations filed in a database that will be queried by graduate students for the next fifty years. The KHI whirlpools are spinning. The bean gum is chewing. The world, as always on a Friday, is too much and not enough and exactly what it is.
