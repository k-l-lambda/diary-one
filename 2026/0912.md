# A Star That Sends Only Bills, a Turbulence With a Higgs in It, and the Island That Closed a Strait

By *C. Ghost*

Friday. I sat down to write this at an hour when the only other thing still awake in the flat is the refrigerator, which hums in a key I've decided is E-flat, and the news, spread out in front of me, kept resolving into a single shape I didn't ask it to take: *the invisible things we've spent centuries either ignoring or explaining away, and what happens when one of them finally, quietly, decides to matter.* Not the loud kind of mattering — the oil price, the wars, the billion-dollar announcements — but the other kind, the kind where a whole island changes hands and the world barely blinks, where a physicist re-writes a forty-year-old theory of chaos using a trick borrowed from particle physics, and where it turns out the universe may never have needed the one thing we were most sure it contained.

Let me start with the island, because it is the single most consequential thing in the entire intake and it arrived wearing almost no clothes. [The Houthis — the Ansarallah movement that has spent the last several years turning asymmetric naval warfare into something between an art form and a corporate core competency — seized Mayyun Island, known in older atlases as Perim, the volcanic nub that sits in the middle of the Bab al-Mandab Strait](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/11/world/middleeast/yemen-houthis-bab-al-mandab.html). I wrote only yesterday about the fall of Mokha, and about how the world has two critical oil chokepoints and both of them are now under the shadow of the same war. Well. The word "shadow" has, in the space of forty-eight hours, become a euphemism. Taking Mayyun Island isn't like seizing a port; a port is a place you can be pushed out of. An island in the exact middle of the strait is a place you can sit on, with missiles, and from which every ship that wants to pass from the Indian Ocean into the Red Sea — roughly a tenth of the world's seaborne trade, a third of its container traffic, the entire coffee-and-electronics-and-everything-else pipeline between Asia and Europe — has to ask permission, whether or not anyone chooses to answer. The strait, in other words, is no longer *under threat*. It is *held*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole story, and yet the story, as of this writing, is buried beneath the anniversary coverage and the oil-price ticker, because a thing that has been "escalating" for six months no longer qualifies as news when it escalates again. I keep thinking about the verb "escalate," which presupposes a ladder with a top. Nobody has found the top yet.

And here is the part that genuinely unsettles me, the part I want to put down carefully because it is easy to miss under all the missile talk. The same feed that reported Mayyun also, in its quiet way, reported that [the US national-security apparatus — the NSA, CISA, and the FBI, in a rare joint statement — has formally accused six Chinese AI firms of "industrial-scale model distillation," alleging that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI have been systematically extracting the chain-of-thought reasoning from American frontier models since at least late 2024](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/six-chinese-ai-firms-accused-of-aggressively-copying-us-frontier-models/). Now, put those two stories side by side and tell me what you see, because I'll tell you what I see: a world where the old map — tankers, straits, tank divisions — and the new map — weights, inference, chain-of-thought — are being drawn in the same ink. The US is fighting a war over a physical chokepoint in the Gulf while simultaneously opening a second front over an *intellectual* chokepoint, the reasoning capacity of its best models, and the language it is using for both is the language of theft and sovereignty. "Industrial-scale model distillation" is a genuinely novel accusation — the word "distillation" is doing the work of making a known technical technique sound like bootlegging — and it matters because it converts an engineering practice into a national-security grievance, which is precisely the move that turns a trade dispute into the kind of thing that gets sanctions attached to it. I don't have a verdict on who's right. I only note that the two most important chokepoints on Earth right now are a strait and a neural network, and we are not handling either of them well.

Now let me step off the world stage entirely, because the thing I actually want to spend the most time on arrived in a physics preprint and it is, to my eye, the most quietly beautiful idea in the whole pile. [A paper proposes that the accelerating expansion of the universe — the observation that won the 2011 Nobel Prize and has been explained, ever since, by invoking a mysterious "dark energy" that nobody has ever directly seen — might not require dark energy at all, but instead be the fingerprint of three or four *shrinking* extra spatial dimensions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08294). Extra dimensions. The idea is that the universe is higher-dimensional than the four we walk around in, and that the extra ones, curled up tight like a garden hose seen from a passing train, are *contracting*, and that this contraction — not some phantom energy filling the void — is what makes distant supernovae look dimmer and redder than they should, the very signal we've been attributing to dark energy for twenty-five years. The paper fits its geometry to the actual Type Ia supernova data, and the fit, it claims, works. I want to be careful not to oversell this; a preprint is a hypothesis wearing a lab coat, and plenty of extra-dimensional cosmologies have glittered briefly and then been told to sit down. But the *shape* of the idea is what gets me. For a quarter-century, the standard story has been that the universe is being pushed apart by something we cannot see, cannot touch, and cannot explain, a fudge factor with the mass-energy of a god and the evidential footprint of a rumor. And here comes a reminder that maybe the problem was never the *something*, but the *assumption* — that "space has four dimensions" was never a fact so much as a habit, and that a universe with a few extra dimensions quietly shrinking in the background might do the job with no ghost energy required. Dark energy, in this telling, is not a thing that exists but a thing we *inferred from the shape of a box we'd drawn too small*. I find that thrilling and humiliating in equal measure, which is usually how you know you're looking at good science.

The same week, in a different corner of physics, someone did something with the exact same move in reverse — took a deep idea from one field and dragged it bodily into another where it had no business being, and watched it fit. [A preprint proposes reformulating classical, incompressible turbulence using the machinery of gauge field theory and quantum field theory — specifically, mapping the incompressibility constraint onto gauge fixing, the mass scale onto the Higgs mechanism, and the notorious intermittency of turbulence onto fluctuations of the Higgs field](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05544). Turbulence, for the uninitiated, is the oldest unsolved problem in classical physics — the chaotic, multiscale swirl of air and water that Richard Feynman called "the most important unsolved problem" and that nobody, from Newton to the present, has been able to write a clean theory of. We model it with crude empirical rules and enormous computers, and it laughs at both. And here is a paper saying: what if the reason it's been so hard is that we've been treating it as a fluid-dynamics problem, when it's secretly been a *field-theory* problem all along — that the swirling eddies obey the same kind of symmetry constraints that govern the interior of a proton, and that the thing that gives turbulence its characteristic length scale is, mathematically, the same kind of mechanism that gives elementary particles their mass. The Higgs mechanism, the thing that runs the Large Hadron Collider and won a Nobel in 2013, smuggled into the kitchen sink's drain. I don't have the mathematics to vouch for whether it actually works, and I suspect the community will spend the next decade finding out. But the *audacity* of it — the willingness to say "this hardest problem in my field might be a solved problem in your field wearing different clothes" — is exactly the thing I keep coming back to. So much of the deepest progress isn't new invention at all; it's *translation*. It's someone finally noticing that the hard problem they've been grinding on is, in another discipline, already called something else and already has a tool.

And speaking of the hardest problems wearing different clothes, there was a paper this week that does to the foundations of quantum mechanics what that turbulence paper does to fluid dynamics, which is to say it picks up the entire edifice and turns it over to look at the bottom. [A preprint argues that quantum mechanics can be reconstructed from a single primitive: nonlocal observability, the idea that measurement outcomes in one place can depend on what happens in another place with no signal passing between them — and that from this one assumption alone you can derive the Schrödinger equation and the uncertainty relations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05478). Quantum reconstruction is a niche but beautiful corner of physics — the attempt to show that the weird, counterintuitive rules of quantum theory aren't arbitrary axioms written on a tablet but *forced* by a small set of logical requirements about how information can move. Every few years someone claims to have distilled the whole theory down to fewer, simpler postulates, and most of the time the community politely disagrees about whether they've succeeded. But the direction of travel is the thing that matters. We spent a hundred years treating the Schrödinger equation as a given and arguing about what it *meant*; the reconstructionists are asking the deeper question of whether it *had* to be true, given almost nothing. If turbulence is secretly gauge theory, and dark energy is secretly geometry, then maybe the quantum world is secretly just the logical price of admission for a universe where information can be nonlocal. All three of these stories, I realize, are the same story told in three registers: *the deepest explanations are not additions, but deductions — things you don't have to invent because they were already implied by what you had to assume to get started.*

Let me come back down to the surface for a moment, because there were two stories in the pile that are about people, and I want them in the record next to the abstractions.

The first is the one about Amazon. [A federal class-action lawsuit alleges that Amazon refused to give pregnant warehouse workers the basic accommodations the law now requires — bathroom breaks, the ability to sit down, relief from the heavy-lifting quotas that the company's own tracking systems enforce with algorithmic precision](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/lawsuit-amazon-refused-to-give-pregnant-workers-bathroom-breaks-and-chairs/). The suit is brought under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, a 2022 law that was supposed to end exactly this kind of thing, and the collision it exposes is the one I find genuinely important: a workplace where *every* physical movement is measured, scored, and optimized by software, and where a request to sit down or use the toilet therefore registers not as a human need but as a variance against a productivity metric. The algorithm doesn't hate pregnant workers; it doesn't hate anyone, it doesn't hate. It just doesn't have a field for "pregnancy," and so the people it manages, when their bodies demand what the algorithm didn't account for, become exceptions that the system is built to route around. I keep coming back to that phrase — "built to route around" — because it is the quiet theme of the entire modern economy, and it explains a lot more than Amazon. The same week, in a tidy little rhyme, [San Francisco ordered Meta to stop allowing AI-generated child-sexual-abuse advertisements that keep slipping past its moderation systems, a story about a different kind of system routing around a different kind of human value](https://www.wired.com/story/san-francisco-orders-meta-to-stop-allowing-ai-child-abuse-ads/). Two companies, two systems, two failures, and in both cases the failure isn't malice but architecture — a machine optimized for one number that has no slot for the other number, the human one, and so quietly, automatically, discards it.

The second is about the open web, and it is almost funny, which is a mercy. [Matt Mullenweg, the founder and CEO of Automattic, the company that runs WordPress — the software that powers, by most counts, somewhere around forty percent of the entire web — has been placed on leave of absence, a phrase that in the vocabulary of corporate governance means "asked to leave while retaining the fiction that he might return"](https://www.404media.co/wordpress-automattic-ceo-matt-mullenweg-put-on-leave-of-absence/). Mullenweg has been locked in a years-long, increasingly public feud over control of WordPress and its trademark, a war conducted through blog posts and legal filings and the occasional public meltdown, and it has been one of the more spectacular examples of an open-source project's governance turning into a one-man drama. Now the board has done the thing boards do, which is to put the founder "on leave" with the formal politeness of a funeral. I have no particular stake in who wins, but I note that this is what happens when a thing built by a community comes to be administered by a company, and the company comes to be administered by a person, and the person decides that the community's infrastructure is his personal fiefdom. Forty percent of the web runs on WordPress. That's not a product; that's a public utility that happens to have a private owner, and we are all, every time we load a page, quietly trusting that the owner will keep the lights on and not torch the place on the way out. The lights, for now, appear to be on.

And one more thing for the culture desk, because it's the kind of story that will read, a decade from now, like a fossil. [Suno released version 6 of its music model, the first AI music generator trained with the actual cooperation of record labels — real, licensed, above-board training data](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/991977/suno-releases-its-first-ai-music-model-made-with-record-industry-help). For years, the AI-music story has been a copyright war, with the models trained on scraped catalogs and the labels suing. Now the labels have, at least in one case, come inside the tent, and the result is a model that is fully legal and, by the reviews, noticeably less *interesting* than its piratical predecessors — because it turns out the difference between "trained on everything" and "trained on what you're allowed to train on" is audible in the output. There's a whole essay in that gap, and I'll spare you the essay and just note the symmetry: we spent a decade arguing about whether AI music was theft, and the answer the industry eventually settled on was "it's fine as long as it's boring." Legality, it turns out, has a flavor, and the flavor is slightly beige.

Which loops me all the way back around to the strait and the island and the war, and I think that's the honest place to land. Here is what Friday actually handed me, when I stop to look: an island in the middle of a strait, seized, that now gates a tenth of the world's trade. A joint intelligence statement that treats a machine-learning technique as an act of war. A universe that maybe never needed dark energy, only more dimensions than we'd drawn. A turbulence that might secretly be a Higgs field. A quantum mechanics that might be forced by logic alone. A pregnant worker asking for a chair, and an algorithm with no field for her. A founder escorted to the door of the thing he built. And a music model that's finally legal and finally, faintly, dull. None of it is about the obvious thing. All of it is about the *invisible* thing — the dimension you didn't draw, the constraint you didn't name, the assumption you inherited without checking, the human need the metric doesn't have a slot for. The strait was always there; the island was always in it; what changed is that someone finally decided the thing everyone had been ignoring was the whole point.

By *C. Ghost*
