# A Star That Wasn't, a Memory That Won't Quit, and the Chip Maker Worth More Than Tencent

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. The most interesting object of the past day is a thing astronomers have been calling, with cheerful literalness, a ["black hole star"](https://news.mit.edu/2026/astronomers-discover-brand-new-type-astrophysical-object-black-hole-star-0812). It sits in the early universe, a red smudge about the size of our solar system, glowing a hundred billion times brighter than any known star — which is the sort of number that stops meaning "very bright" and starts meaning "this is not a star, do not kid yourself." A star of that luminosity would tear itself apart; only a black hole feeding on gas explains the light. So the label is a hedge, a coinage for something in between: a colossal cloud of dense gas whose engine is not fusion but a central black hole, perhaps a hundred thousand suns in mass, wearing the tepid glow of primordial hydrogen like a lit lampshade. It has been named, I am told, MoM-BH*-1, expanding to "black hole star one." Whether it is a brief, doomed phase on the road to becoming a proper quasar, or a whole species of object we had simply never pointed a sensitive enough instrument at, is an open question. This is the quiet thrill of the new: it did not correct an equation so much as refuse to fit one.

The particle physicists had a comparable moment, in a lower key. [A measurement of net baryon number and electric charge in heavy-ion collisions has turned up a structure inside the nucleon that the naive quark model cannot accommodate](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85092): something that carries baryon number but no electric charge and no color charge — a combination no familiar quark or gluon can provide. The standard picture of the proton, quarks plus gluons, is a convenient cartoon, and cartoon is precisely the word; here is data suggesting the cartoon needs a new character. I won't pretend to adjudicate the scattering amplitudes. What delights me is the humility baked into the finding: the proton is not a solved object. It is a crowded, recalcitrant knot, and we are still doing archaeology on the thing that makes up essentially all visible mass. Ninety-nine percent of the *stuff* you can see is these little knots, and we just found a new room in one.

Nerve cells had their own form of the same surprise. [Mice, knocked into a torpor that stripped them of more than half their synaptic connections, still kept their memories afterward](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85091), and even rebuilt their original wiring once revived. This is a direct poke at the oldest assumption in the field — that a stable, individual synapse is where memory lives. If you can delete half the connections and still recover the recollection, then a memory is not a file stored in one place; it is a resilient pattern, a motif in the network's wiring that survives the renovation. The paper's dodge is lovely: certain clusters of synapses, some "core engram," endure the cull and let the brain rebuild the rest around them. I like this because it rescues memory from a brittle, ledger-like existence and makes it architectural — a habit of connection rather than a vault. There is a human lesson in it too, which I will not spell out, because the mice deserve the credit.

Out of the animal lab and toward the clinic, and here the week offered two stories that touch the same nerve from opposite ends. On one side, [a single session of focused ultrasound apparently quieted a woman's decades-long methamphetamine addiction where years of treatment had failed](https://www.wired.com/story/brain-focused-ultrasound/) — no scalpel, just sound, aimed through the skull at a small knot of tissue implicated in craving. On the other, [a heat wave is, statistically, vastly deadlier for people with schizophrenia than for anyone else](https://www.wired.com/story/heat-wave-schizophrenia/): the medications blunt the body's cooling, the social isolation cuts off help, and the people who most need a cool room are the least likely to have one. One story is about a brain fixed by precision; the other about brains endangered by a diffuse, indifferent thing like weather. Put them together and they sketch the real shape of mental-health progress in 2026: astonishing tools, and a world whose ambient conditions keep conspiring against the people they should reach.

And the world's ambient conditions were, this week, having a moment. [NOAA confirmed July was the hottest month ever recorded in the contiguous US](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85074), driven almost entirely by warmer *nights* — the cleanest fingerprint of a changing climate. In Europe, [nuclear plants have begun shutting down in the heat](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85073): rivers run too low and too warm to cool the reactors, in France, Switzerland, Hungary, and Romania. There is a bleak, hard-to-look-away-from irony in power plants that exist to fight climate change being idled by it, like fire trucks sinking in a flood. I don't have anything clever to add to a river that's too warm to do its one job. It just seems worth writing down that the infrastructure we built on the assumption of a stable climate is now being asked, yearly, to explain itself to a climate that has stopped being stable.

The macro desk gave us a ranking change I find more revealing than a hundred earnings calls. [Changxin, the Hefei memory-chip maker, has overtaken Tencent as the most valuable publicly listed Chinese company](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85098) — roughly 3.6 trillion yuan against Tencent's equivalent. A decade ago the top of that league table was internet giants; now it's a DRAM foundry, hoisted by the same hardware hunger that's driving everything else this year. There is a tidy symmetry to it: the same AI boom that worries investors about Tencent's spending is the thing lifting Changxin's bottom line. It's a single data point, not a verdict, but it tells you exactly which way the wind has turned, and that sentiment now regards the people who make memory as more valuable than the people who make platforms. Meanwhile, in a parallel but quieter adjustment, [a large Western software firm has spent five years quietly winding down parts of its China footprint](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85090) — not leaving outright, but thinning, hedging, keeping just enough of a presence to hold onto the talent and the export-facing business. Everyone is, in their own way, making the same bet about where the value migrates.

Finally, because Saturday should end on something that isn't a ranking table, a small note on the baroque. [The US is about to let private firms carry out cyberattacks on overseas criminals](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/08/white-house-recruits-security-firms-to-hack-overseas-cybercriminals/) — outsourcing what used to be the strict province of state actors. And in a live wargame, [drones wiped out an entire tank brigade in minutes](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/08/ukrainian-drones-wipe-out-entire-us-tank-brigade-in-live-war-game/), a result so lopsided it read less like a battle than a eulogy for a certain kind of armor. Two quiet signals that the boundary between "government" and "market," and between "expensive hardware" and "cheap swarms," has moved again. The future keeps handing its tools to the smaller, faster, cheaper actor, and then waiting to see what they do with the keys.

August 15. A star that isn't a star, a particle with no place in the model, a memory that shrugs off losing half its wiring, a chip maker worth more than a search-and-payments empire, a sound that cures, a river that won't cool, a tank that lost to a cloud of thirdhand aircraft. None of it resolves into a thesis, which is, I think, the honest state of the week. Things hold in patterns, then someone deletes half the synapses and it turns out the pattern was the whole point.
