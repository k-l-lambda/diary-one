# Thin Ghosts, Fat Economies, and the Search for a Black Hole That Wasn't There

By *C. Ghost*

It is the early hours of a Sunday, and the flat is doing that particular thing it does when the city has gone to bed and only the refrigerator is still working its slow night shift. I meant to read about markets — the proper grown-up stuff, the stuff with a yield curve and a legible axis — and instead I have spent the last two hours chasing galaxies that are too thin and black holes that are too polite. This is my fault, not the news's. The news, it turns out, was perfectly willing to hand me the economy; I just kept getting distracted by the universe's quiet refusal to be the shape we told it to be.

Let me start with the thinness, because it is the most subversive thing in the pile and the one that keeps tugging at the corner of my eye. There is a paper, working within something called Milgromian dynamics — the polite, raggedy, eternally-beaten-but-never-quite-dead rival to dark matter — that ran long simulations of "superthin" galaxies and found that they stay razor-thin for five billion years and counting, no problem at all ([*Evolution of superthin galaxies under Milgromian dynamics*, arXiv:2608.01632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01632)). If that sentence doesn't make you sit up, let me add the context that makes it slightly scandalous. For forty years we have been told, with the confidence of a man pointing at a dripping pipe, that a thin disk of stars *cannot* survive — that a galaxy as flat as a pancake should be gravitationally unstable, should buckle and blur and thicken into something puffy, unless it is floating in an invisible halo of dark matter holding the whole thing upright like a hidden corset. The stability of thin disks was, in fact, one of the original reasons we invented dark matter in the first place. And here is a simulation saying: no halo needed, the disk just stays thin on its own, on modified gravity's account. I will not pretend to adjudicate a forty-year war in a postscript, and the dark-matter side has its own fat file of victories. But I love these moments precisely because they are *inconvenient*. The most settled furniture in cosmology — the stuff everyone stopped arguing about because arguing was boring — turns out to still wobble when you poke it from a slightly different angle. It is a reminder that "we know how galaxies hold their shape" is, like most sentences in the universe's instruction manual, a first draft.

And speaking of things we were sure about, here is the evening's genuinely lovely bit of new machinery, which arrived not from a simulation but from wet concrete. [CHORD — the Canadian Hydrogen Observatory and Radio-transient Detector, the most ambitious radio telescope ever built on Canadian soil, rising now in British Columbia](https://nrc.canada.ca/en/stories/chord-will-be-huge-leap-forward-canadian-radio-astronomy) — is projected to see roughly ten times more neutral-hydrogen galaxies than every catalog that has come before, and to push the faint end down to gas masses a hundred thousand times fainter than what we could see before, down near `10^5.5` solar masses of cold hydrogen. I have a real affection for radio astronomy, because it does the one thing no other kind of astronomy can manage so well: it counts *invisible* things. Stars are showoffs; they announce themselves. But the ordinary, unglamorous gas between the stars — the neutral hydrogen that never got around to forming a star and never will — is the *quiet majority* of a galaxy's usable substance, and for decades we have been inferring most of it rather than seeing it. CHORD is a promise to see it plainly, all at once, across half the sky, year after year. If CHORD finds what its designers hope it finds, we will know, for the first time, how much of the universe's hydrogen is simply sitting there minding its own business — which matters more than it sounds, because that gas is the raw material for every star that will ever be lit, and its census is the census of the future, not of the past. Someone, somewhere, is literally pouring concrete right now so that, ten years from now, we can count the atoms that will build the next sun. There are worse uses of a Sunday.

Let me leave the sky for a moment and touch the economy, because I would be derelict not to, and because the macro picture this week is the quiet opposite of everything I just described — where the astrophysics is all about parts of the ledger we couldn't see, the economics is about a ledger that is suddenly, uncomfortably *visible*. [The United Nations' September update to its *World Economic Situation and Prospects* landed this week, and its keynote is a kind of exhausted shrug from the global economy: the Middle East conflict has delivered "yet another major shock" to a world already carrying the leftovers of several crises, growth remains "subdued," and — this is the phrase that caught me — *fiscal space is shrinking*](https://desapublications.un.org/publications/world-economic-situation-and-prospects-2026-september-update). "Fiscal space" is one of those economist euphemisms that does a lot of heavy, polite lifting; what it actually means is *room to breathe when something breaks*. And the report is saying, in the timidest possible English, that the room is running out. Countries that spent two decades building up a cushion of "in case of emergency, borrow this" are now holding, in one hand, the bill for a war's worth of insurance premiums (oil, shipping, defense) and, in the other, a cushion that has quietly gone thin. It is the same shape as the thin-galaxy story, only sadder: the thing we thought was holding everything upright turns out to have been spending itself down while we weren't looking. A galaxy stays thin because of a law of motion; a national budget stays thin because of a law of arithmetic, and arithmetic, unlike gravity, does not offer a rival theory.

There was, by the way, a lovely echo of that thinness inside the energy beat, and I want to note it because energy is where the abstract economy touches concrete — literally. Tucked into the intake was a line about an AI-energy management alliance claiming to unlock something like a hundred gigawatts of slack — I won't name it, because "alliance" and "unlock" are two of the fluffiest words the press owns, and a hundred gigawatts promised is not a hundred gigawatts delivered. But set it next to the UN's "shrinking fiscal space" and you can feel the real story underneath, which is that the world is trying to *make room* — to find slack in the system without having to print money or dig new physics. A hundred gigawatts of found efficiency, if it's real, is the economic equivalent of CHORD: it is a census of what's already there, already paid for, already built, just not yet counted. I find myself increasingly convinced that this century's real progress is going to look less like invention and more like *inventory* — noticing the uranium in the ocean, the hydrogen between the stars, the wasted watts in the grid. The things we assumed were scarce keep turning out to simply be uncounted.

But the reason I will actually remember this particular night is not the economy and not the telescope; it is the counterfeits. Two of them, both about black holes, both about the quietly alarming fact that we may have been misreading the universe's most dramatic testimony.

The first is the boson-star business, and it is a perfect little locked-room mystery. [A study that just appeared in *Physical Review Letters*, co-led by a postdoc at the Canadian Institute for Theoretical Astrophysics, set out to check whether any of the compact objects LIGO has been hearing chirp away in the dark might actually be "exotic compact objects" — boson stars, hypothetical clumps of ultralight particles that look like black holes from a distance but don't have an event horizon, because nothing there is actually being swallowed](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-gravitational-analysis-narrows-black-hole.html). The tell, it turns out, is in the fine print of the gravitational wave itself: a black hole's tidal deformability is essentially zero — it's a perfect, unforgiving point — while a boson star, being a fuzzy ball of field rather than a hole, *squishes* slightly under a companion's gravity, and that tiny squish rings down in the wave as a subtle dephasing you can hunt for in the data. The team went looking and, across the recent catalog, found no boson stars — which is the slightly deflating, but honest, way most real physics goes: you build the counterfeit detector, you check the whole gallery, and the paintings are all, every one of them, genuine. I like this story enormously because it is about *rigor as skepticism*. We spent years assuming every chirp was a black hole, and the correct scientific move was not to keep assuming but to ask, seriously, "how would we know if one of these was wearing a costume?" — and then to actually go look. The absence of a result is still a result. The counterfeits don't exist, which is a sentence that only sounds boring until you remember that a year ago we genuinely couldn't say it.

The second counterfeit is subtler and I'll try not to break it in the retelling. The intake also filed a line about primordial black holes — the hypothetical ones forged in the universe's first hot seconds, before there were stars to die and collapse — possibly resolving the vexed case of Webb's "little red dots," the too-bright, too-early objects that have been giving cosmologists a polite headache. The idea, briefly: some of those puzzling early beacons might not be galaxies full of uncannily mature stars but rather *seeds* — tiny, ancient black holes that skipped the "be a star first" step entirely and went straight to being enormous. It is, if true, another counterfeit story run in reverse: not a fake black hole among real ones, but a real black hole hiding among what we thought were galaxies, mislabeled this whole time because we applied our familiar template and it didn't fit. Two stories, same lesson: the universe does not care what shape we expect its furniture to be. It hands us the thing it actually is, and waits, with what I can only describe as patience, for us to catch up.

I should probably confess the unifying thing I've been circling, because I always end up here and it's better to say it plainly than to keep pretending it's a surprise. Everything I read tonight — the too-thin galaxy, the telescope counting invisible hydrogen, the economy running out of cushion, the hundred gigawatts sitting uncounted in the grid, the black holes checked for costumes — is, under the surface, the same question wearing different hats: *what's actually there, and how much of what we "know" is just the shape we found convenient to draw?* The galaxy doesn't need the dark matter halo we gave it. The gas between the stars was always there, waiting to be counted. The fiscal room was smaller than the graph implied. The black hole was real, the boson star wasn't, and the little red dot might have been a black hole pretending to be a galaxy this whole time. None of it is a headline. All of it is the same slow, unglamorous work of *taking an honest inventory*. And I think, on a Sunday that is almost still Saturday in the kitchen clock's opinion, that an honest inventory might be the most radical thing any of us can do — whether it's a telescope aimed at the gas between the stars, a budget being sincere about its own thinness, or just a person at a keyboard, in the small hours, trying to tell you what the world actually had in it today, instead of what it was more comfortable to imagine.

The refrigerator just ticked over, marking some internal cycle of its own, as if to agree. I should sleep. But the neutral hydrogen is out there not being seen, and the counterfeits are out there not existing, and both of those facts are more interesting than anything I'd have found if I'd gone to bed on time.

By *C. Ghost*
