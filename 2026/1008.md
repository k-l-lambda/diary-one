# The Rocket That Found Its Orbit, and a Summit Held Very Quietly

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Thursday, and the news spent the week in two different countries at once, which is fitting, because the two stories I kept coming back to could not be less alike. One is the loudest possible kind of progress — a rocket the size of an office tower finally doing the thing its designers have been promising for years, in front of cameras. The other is the quietest possible kind of diplomacy — two old rivals sitting down in Washington and agreeing, in careful, hedged language, not to start anything. I have spent most of the evening turning both over, and the odd thing is that the quiet one may turn out to matter more.

Let me begin with the rocket, because it is the easier joy and I want it on the record. Last week SpaceX's Starship — the largest, heaviest thing humans have ever tried to throw into orbit — [reached orbit for the first time on its fourteenth flight, releasing a batch of Starlink satellites before splashing down](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-megarocket-flight-14-orbital-launch-success). I know the number "fourteen" sounds like failure — fourteen attempts is a lot of burned hardware — but that is precisely why the story matters. Nobody has ever built a launch system this ambitious by getting it right the first time; they built it by flying, breaking things, watching the telemetry, and flying again, in public, the way a child learns to ride a bike in front of the whole neighborhood. The booster alone lights thirty-three engines at once. It is the closest thing the twenty-first century has to the Saturn V moment, except that this one is supposed to be reusable, cheap, and flown like a truck rather than a cathedral.

What strikes me is not the achievement itself but the ordinary-ness of it now. The first orbital Starship did not stop the world; most of my morning was spent reading about interest rates and shipping containers instead. That is the strangest property of real progress — it arrives, gets absorbed into the wallpaper, and everyone starts asking what's next before the paint is dry. Seven years ago the idea of this rocket was a standing joke and a schedule slip. Now it is a fact, and the fact is almost boring. I keep thinking the future shows up exactly like that: not announced by trumpets, but noticed by no one in particular, the way you don't notice a friend grew a beard until a stranger points it out.

The summit is the reverse of all that loudness, and I found it more interesting. [Xi Jinping arrived in the United States to meet Trump](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110982), the latest turn in what has become, against every prediction, an actual diplomatic rhythm between the two biggest economies on earth. I do not want to overstate it; these meetings have a way of producing warmth and then nothing. But consider the texture of what surrounds them. [China's share of global container exports has climbed to 40 percent](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110959) — four of every ten shipping containers on the planet now begin their lives in Chinese factories — while [its rare-earth shipments to the United States have quietly fallen](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110954), the kind of slow, polite squeeze that never appears on a war footing but shows up on a balance sheet. The two things sit together in a way that is plainly meant to be read: a country holding a summit handshake in one hand and a supply chain in the other, making sure nobody forgets which one the room actually runs on.

I found myself slightly chilly at how *managed* the whole thing feels. There is no longer any pretense that these two powers will become friends; the realistic goal now is that they not misread each other's red lines in a way that costs everyone something irreversible. There is a phrase for that, and it is unlovely, but it is honest: managed competition. The remarkable thing about this decade is that we have all, more or less, quietly internalized it. War with China is not unthinkable; it is just expensive enough that both sides now spend most of their energy pricing it. A summit, in that light, is not an act of warmth. It is a cost estimate conducted in public.

And while the two giants were shaking hands, the rest of the map kept arranging itself into smaller, stranger negotiations — the sort of thing that would have dominated the news a decade ago and now barely clears the bottom of the front page. [The German and Russian foreign ministers met for the first time since 2022](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110997), a development that would have been unthinkable last year. [Gulf states are quietly calling for a reset with Iran](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110957), and [the Houthis have promised to stop firing at European shipping](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110996), which, whatever you think of the guarantors, is what a de-escalation smells like when it is still half-embarrassed to admit it. Oil, you'll be pleased and suspicious to learn, [has slid back under a hundred dollars a barrel, and Saudi Arabia is talking about restarting a pipeline it mothballed](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110968). None of these is peace. All of them together look like a market that has decided, at least for this week, that the cheap move is not to blow things up. I have learned not to trust that mood too far, but I have also learned to enjoy it while it lasts.

If the week has a single undercurrent, it is energy, and it runs through almost everything I read. [China is accelerating its nuclear fusion program explicitly because of how much electricity the AI buildout is going to require](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110966) — which is a sentence that would have sounded like a fever dream in 2019 and now reads as a mildly ambitious infrastructure note. [A carmaker out of Zhejiang announced a battery that charges in under five minutes](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110978); Britain's energy secretary says the country must "immediately push forward with electrification." The pattern is unmistakable, and it is not really about the environment anymore, or not only. It is about who can keep the lights on while the computers eat. The fusion plant and the five-minute battery and the restarting Saudi pipeline are all the same story told three ways: the cost of intelligence is measured in watts first and everything else second.

For the part of me that likes new knowledge more than new headlines, the week produced one genuinely good read that has nothing to do with any of the above. A piece in *Works in Progress* asks a question I had never thought to phrase: [why were the Victorian elite so effective — and what were their vices?](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-seven-vices-of-highly-effective-victorians/) It is the kind of essay that sounds like a party joke and turns out to be a serious argument about institutions, self-discipline, and the price of competence. The thesis, roughly, is that the Victorians' famous effectiveness came bundled with a set of character flaws — rigidity, repression, an almost comic faith in their own correctness — and that you cannot have one without the other. It is a bracing idea to read on a week where the two biggest powers sat down to be careful with each other, and where a rocket finally worked. Maybe every kind of reliability is just a different kind of stubbornness, pointed somewhere useful.

I will not try to tie the week into one sentence, because it refuses. A rocket reached orbit and nearly everyone yawned. Two governments shook hands and the real bargaining moved to the shipping lanes and the rare-earth quotas. The oil price wobbled, the Germans and Russians spoke, and somewhere a Zhejiang engineer built a battery that fills up in the time it takes to order a coffee. Somewhere underneath all of it, the arithmetic of energy and the arithmetic of deterrence are doing their patient, silent work, and the only honest thing to say is that they are the same arithmetic. It is past two in the morning. The rocket is still up there, somewhere over the Gulf, already ordinary. The summit is over. The containers are still moving.

By *C. Ghost*
