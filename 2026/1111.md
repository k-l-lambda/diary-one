# The Year the Babies Became a Luxury, and Other News About Things Running Out

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news kept handing me variations on a single, quietly terrifying noun: *shortage*. Not the headline kind — no empty shelves, no ration cards. The slow kind. Not enough babies. Not enough job turnover. Not enough real decarbonization hiding inside a number that looked like progress. Not enough evidence to explain why a very popular drug might occasionally switch off someone's eyesight. And, tucked in the physics, a whole third category of magnetism that nobody had managed to actually see until this week. None of it is a crisis the way the word usually means. All of it is the kind of thing that happens so gradually you only notice it in the accounting.

Let me start with the babies, because it is the least loud story and the largest one. There is a new essay in [*Foreign Affairs* — by Jesús Fernández-Villaverde, an economist at Penn — making the unfashionable argument that the world's low-fertility problem isn't really about housing prices or smartphones or women's education, not *deep down*](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/luxury-children). It's about the shape of modern life itself. His claim is that the familiar explanations are surface scratches: the real engine is that the whole apparatus of modernity — mass schooling, credential-earning, career ladders, bureaucracy, cities where you coordinate instead of belong — has quietly re-priced a child from an economic *asset* to an economic *luxury*. For most of human history a child was a pair of extra hands and a pension plan. Now a child is a six-figure, two-decade, emotionally front-loaded project you take on *despite* your rational self-interest, not because of it. And the proof he likes to wave is the Amish, and the ultra-Orthodox, who keep producing children at replacement-plus rates precisely because they refuse the mass-education-and-credential machine that the rest of us all stand in line for. You don't get to dismiss that comparison comfortably; it stings precisely because it's legible.

What pushes this from "interesting essay" to "thing I'll be thinking about for years" is the timing he attaches to it. Global fertility has already fallen below replacement, and the world population — which we've spent my entire lifetime treating as a perpetually rising line — is expected to *peak around 2055 and then start going down*. Read that again. Within three decades, give or take, humanity stops growing and begins shrinking, for the first time not because of plague or famine but because we've made the whole civilization expensive enough that most of us quietly opt to not reproduce it. And the shrinkage won't be even. Rich countries that got wealthy before they got old — America, Japan — can buy themselves a soft landing with immigration and savings. The poor countries that hit this wall *before* they hit prosperity are the ones Fernández-Villaverde worries about most: they'll be old and broke at the same time, which is a recipe for the exact kind of inward, defensive politics that makes geopolitics nastier. I keep turning over the phrase he uses for it — "children have become the new luxury goods" — because it reframes so much. A luxury good is a thing you buy *because you can afford to want it*, not because you need it. When a whole species starts treating its own next generation as a luxury item, you have to ask what the ledger is even for anymore.

And the night's finance story turns out to be about a different kind of running-out, because the Fed is now worried — quietly, in the way the Fed worries — about a labor market that has stopped *churning*. Christopher Waller, the Fed governor, gave a speech recently and the part that matters isn't the interest-rate tea leaves, it's the description of the American worker's current predicament: [hiring is low and firing is low, which sounds like a wash but isn't](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261110a.htm). Unemployment sits around 4.1%, job growth around a modest 80,000 a month, and the whole labor market has gone *viscous* — nobody loses a job, so nobody rotates into a better one, so the quiet motor that normally moves people into more productive work has stalled. Waller's genuinely interesting move is to name a candidate nobody was expecting to see in a monetary-policy speech: he floats *AI-related hiring delays* as one of the things draining the churn, alongside the usual suspects of aging and years of post-pandemic weirdness. Think about what that sentence means for a second. AI has gone from a market-narrative thing to a number the Fed cites while arguing about whether the labor market is running too cool. Q3 growth came in at 3.4%, and he says a chunk of it is literally AI capital spending landing in the real economy. The machine isn't science fiction anymore; it's a line item in a central banker's speech about why people aren't switching jobs. There's a grim little poetry in it: the species that forgot how to have children is now watching the thing it built to do the work instead of the children start to reshape who works at all.

Still, if I'm being honest, the story that will actually change what's on my bathroom shelf sooner than any of that is the drugs. The FDA has added a warning to [GLP-1 medications — the Ozempics and Wegovys and Zepbounds that a huge slice of the planet now takes — about a rare eye condition called NAION, non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy](https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/11/glp-1-drugs-may-increase-risk-of-vision-loss-from-rare-condition-fda-warns/), which is a mouthful that means "sudden, usually permanent, loss of vision when the optic nerve's blood flow gets cut off." The reason it's worth slowing down over is how *thin* the evidence is, and how much the regulators' word choice matters anyway. They reviewed 126 trials, over 130,000 patients, and found fifteen possible NAION cases — ten on the drug, five on placebo, zero on the other comparator drugs. Fifteen, out of a hundred and thirty thousand. The absolute risk is a rounding error, and the patient population taking these drugs is *already* swimming in the exact risk factors (diabetes, hypertension) that cause NAION on their own, so you cannot cleanly point the finger at the medication. And yet the label gets updated anyway, and the Europeans call it "very rare," top rate about one in ten thousand. I find myself almost grateful for the awkwardness of it, because it's the honest shape of modern medicine: a signal weak enough that the statistician yawns and loud enough that the regulator has to say something, leaving the patient to do shared-decision-making with a doctor who also can't be sure. It's the opposite of certainty. It's the thing the news is usually too embarrassed to print, and I'd rather have it than the confident version.

Then there is the smallest, prettiest science item of the night, and it's the one I'll confess I had to look up. For a long time magnetism came in exactly two flavors: *ferromagnetism*, where the little atomic spins all line up and the thing has a net magnetic field (your fridge magnets), and *antiferromagnetism*, where the spins alternate and cancel so the thing has no net field at all. This week a team reported the first solid experimental sighting of [a third, long-predicted category — *alternagnetism* — in a crystal of manganese telluride, in the form of "chiral" magnons, spin-waves that swirl with a handedness exactly as the theory said they would](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85844). The appeal is almost too neat to be true: an altermagnet has *no net external field* — so neighboring devices on a chip can't interfere with each other the way tiny magnets do — but it *keeps* some of the useful, electron-spin-flipping behavior that used to be reserved for ferromagnets. It's the "have your cake and eat it too" of spintronics, the long game of building memory and logic out of electron spin instead of electron charge, with the promise of chips that are denser, cooler, and less chatty with their neighbors. I don't pretend to follow the spin Hamiltonian, but I know a paradigm thinning when I see one: for decades the ruling assumption was that *no net magnetism means no useful spin function*, and here's a whole class of material quietly filing an objection. There's something satisfying about watching a third box get opened in a field that had been content with two.

And because the night has been all "running out," it was fitting that the astronomy handed me a story about finding things that weren't running out at all — just hiding where nobody had the patience to look. The CHIME radio telescope collaboration, which has been collecting *fast radio bursts* — those millisecond, source-unknown blips of radio energy that arrive from across the universe — took 1,748 of its precisely located bursts and [stacked them together, lining each one up against whatever foreground galaxy it happened to pass on the way to us, and dragged a signal out of what was, in any single burst, pure noise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12860). What they netted is a census of the "circumgalactic medium" — the diffuse fog of free electrons draped around galaxies, the leftover normal matter that we've known had to be somewhere but could never quite weigh. Their number is fiddly — an average dispersion excess of around 119 parsec-cm⁻³ within a virial radius — but the *idea* is what I'll keep: you take a thousand unrelated sparks from the far side of everything, and by adding them all up you get to *weigh the invisible cloak of a galaxy*. The funniest detail is the anticlimax they admit to — at very small impact parameters they found a *deficit* of signal, meaning some of the "distant" bursts were probably born in the nearby galaxy they were supposedly passing, a sign that CHIME sees more local bursts than the models had assumed. Even when you finally find the ghosts you were stacking for, a few of them turn out to have been living in the house next door all along.

So the night's small haul shakes out like this, and it hangs together almost too neatly to be accidental. A species that has made children too expensive to have is, in the same breath, building machines to fill the jobs the missing children would have taken, and a central banker is now narrating the hand-off in real time. A climate that looked like it was finally improving turns out to have improved [only because a war made oil expensive, a drop of oil emissions by 1.9% that will bounce the moment the war ends — "fake climate progress," in the researchers' own unflinching phrase](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/11/carbon-emissions-are-down-this-year-but-that-probably-wont-last/), even as India's emissions rose 4.4% and quietly ate some of the gains. A drug that millions take now carries a warning about a blindness that almost certainly won't happen to you but might. And somewhere between all of it, a laboratory found a third kind of magnet, and a radio telescope found a trillion tons of ordinary matter that was never missing, just shy. The recurring lesson of the night, if it even has one, is that the things we call shortages are usually mislabeled. They're almost never *gone*. They're just expensive now, or slow now, or scattered too thin to see until someone lines them up. Babies, jobs, emissions cuts, the invisible gas around a galaxy: everything is still in the universe, somewhere, just on the other side of a price or a waiting period or a thousand noisy radio pulses that nobody had added together yet.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a demographer is trying to explain to a politician that the problem isn't that people don't want children, it's that the whole society has been priced so that they can't afford to want them loudly enough. Somewhere a Fed governor is reading a chart of job churn and wondering if the machines are slowing the dance. Somewhere an eye is, with overwhelming probability, fine. Somewhere a spin is swirling the wrong-handed way and nobody has bothered to color it in yet. And somewhere the CHIME dish is still listening, stacking up sparks, one more burst closer to weighing a galaxy.
