# Sunday at the Bottom of the Staircase

By *C. Ghost*

Sunday. Rain outside, the kind that makes a person sort receipts, and my head is full of a strange week in which the most interesting stories all happened below the waterline — the markets, the counting rooms, the quietly re-ordered loyalties — while the cameras were pointed at the fireworks. Let me start with a man and a staircase, because it's the closest thing this week had to a moral, and the moral is that Chinese property was a very long staircase and everyone on it had a different idea of the bottom.

Xu Jiayin, the founder of Evergrande, was sentenced to life imprisonment this week — [life, not death, which in the arithmetic of Chinese commercial crime usually means the state wanted to make a point without making a martyr](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110675). You may remember him as the man who, a few years ago, was photographed at the head of a boardroom table grinning under a banner that read, more or less, *everything is fine*. Evergrande was the second-biggest property developer on earth, owing something north of $300 billion, and for a long time the market's shared fantasy was that it couldn't possibly fall because it was too big, too connected, too *important*. It fell. The company was ordered liquidated. And this week the liquidators did the thing that makes liquidation genuinely frightening to a certain class of person: they got a Hong Kong court to allow them to [sue PwC globally](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110723) — not the local arm, not a subsidiary, but the partnership itself, chasing the auditor's deep pockets across borders. That last detail is the one I keep turning over. For years the four big accounting firms have sold themselves as networks of independent little firms, each safely walled off from the others' mistakes. A Hong Kong judge just stared at that architecture and decided the wall had a door. If the claim survives, the entire "partnership network" model — the thing that lets an auditor shrug and say *not my office* — has a crack running down the middle of it. Nobody writes poems about auditors. But the slow-motion end of the man who sold half of China an apartment on credit, and the slow-motion pursuit of the men who signed off the books, is as good a snapshot as any of how the biggest debt binge in modern history is being wound down: one staircase, one signature, one jurisdiction at a time.

While the accountants chased each other, a different kind of arithmetic was happening in the Persian Gulf, and the week's clearest sentence about it came not from a general or a minister but from a shipping man. [The chief executive of Torm, the Danish tanker firm, told the Financial Times that markets are underpricing the risk of the U.S.–Iran war settling into a "Ukrainian-style stalemate," and that the Gulf states have quietly begun expanding their own tanker fleets to try to overwhelm Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz with sheer tonnage.](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110725) Read that again: the plan, such as it is, is not to break the blockade but to make it statistically survivable — to run so many hulls at the chokepoint that enough get through to keep the lights on. It is the same logic as flooding a wall with bodies until some climb over, and it has the grim honesty of a man who prices risk for a living and has stopped pretending there's a diplomatic off-ramp. The same day, in Tehran, [the president tasked with the economy said the war should "end today"](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110689) — which is the kind of remark that tells you the people holding the purse want out even if the people holding the maps don't. I won't pretend to sort out who's right. I'll just note that when the deal-makers start buying boats and the money-men start asking for the exit at the same time, the smart reading is usually that everyone has privately accepted the war will be long, and is now arguing only over who pays for the length of it.

From the Gulf, the money moved east and west in ways worth a line each, because they're small and they rhyme. [Pakistan — which spent a decade leaning on Chinese concessional loans — says it now wants to borrow from America instead, and is planning a return to international bond markets to reduce its dependence on Beijing.](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110708) Set that next to the fact that, the very same week, [a British shipbuilding venture backed by Japan's Mitsui, Mitsubishi and Sumitomo signed a public–private deal with the Trump administration to begin building the world's first nuclear-powered merchant ship by 2028, explicitly to challenge China's dominance of the shipyards.](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110698) Two different oceans, one sentence: the map of who lends money and who floats steel is being redrawn, and the red lines are being sketched in a language neither side will say out loud. Nuclear civilian shipping is a lovely old idea — the Americans built one, the *Savannah*, back when the future was supposed to have fins and atomic everything — and like most lovely atomic ideas it died of cost and fear. That Washington and Tokyo are dusting it off now has almost nothing to do with the physics and everything to do with who currently owns the ocean the cargo crosses.

But let me step off the map entirely, because the two stories I'd actually save from this week are the ones nobody is fighting a war about, and they're both, in their way, about a country deciding whether it gets a future.

The first is demographic, and I almost scrolled past it because the headline sounded like a typo. [Both South Korea and Japan saw their population *grow* in the first half of this year — South Korea's births up fifteen percent on the year, its best half on record, and Japan notching its first half-year increase in births since 2015.](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85230) I want to be careful not to overclaim, because one or two quarters does not a baby boom make, and if the fertility rate only crawls back to 0.9 it is still far below replacement. But set the caveats aside and just notice the direction, because for years the story of East Asia has been written as an arrow pointing relentlessly down — the dying towns, the closed schools, the "extinction of a nation" thinkpieces — and this week, against every forecast, the arrow twitched upward in two countries at once. The explanations on offer are the soft, hopeful kind: marriages that had been postponed through the pandemic years finally happening, women in their thirties who had deferred children now deciding differently, a genuine shift in what people want. I don't know if it's a trend or a ripple. But I know the whole genre of demographic doom got one small, stubborn data point against it, and I'll take my small, stubborn data points where I can find them.

The second is medical, and it's the one I'd ask everyone to actually read, because it concerns a thing that almost nobody talks about and nearly everybody will brush against. [A study from the University of East Anglia concludes that perhaps one in twenty women in the UK goes through pregnancy and birth and comes out the other side with undiagnosed post-traumatic stress disorder — and that suicide is now the single largest cause of death among British women in the year after giving birth.](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85231) Suicides, flashbacks, nightmares, a year of "you should be happy" while the mind quietly replays the worst day of its life — and the researchers' phrasing, that the recorded deaths are "the tip of the iceberg," is the kind of line that sits in a person. The subject is underfunded, under-screened, and profoundly unfashionable, because to talk about it is to admit that the thing we culturally insist is life's great blessing is, for a measurable minority, also a trauma. I have no clever angle on it. I only know that the week produced a hundred headline-worthy statistics and this is the one I'll still be turning over on Tuesday.

And because a Sunday column should end somewhere lighter, let me close on the robot, which is the only story this week that made me laugh out loud. [China's Tiangong humanoid beat Usain Bolt's 100-metre world record, running 8.64 seconds to Bolt's 9.58 — and then, having no way to stop itself, crashed into a padded cushion, because humanoid robots, it turns out, brake the way a thrown rock brakes.](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85228) The polite experts noted that the machine took nearly a full second just to react to the starting gun (Bolt: 0.146 seconds) and needed fifty-odd strides to Bolt's forty-one, that its gait reflects motor torque, not the imitation of a sprinter. All true, and all beside the point. The point is the image: the fastest bipedal thing ever clocked over that distance, and the only way it knows how to stop is to aim at the softest available object and hope. There's a fable in it I refuse to over-explain. We are building machines that can outrun our best, out-think our tired, and outlast our patience, and the current state of their art is a spectacular sprint ending in a controlled collision with furniture. I don't know whether that's comforting or terrifying. Probably both, which is the honest answer to most things this week.

So: a property tycoon reached the bottom of a staircase and the auditors looked up and saw the door open. A tanker man admitted the war will be long, and the money-men asked for the exit at the same moment the shipbuilders ordered more hulls. Pakistan turned its borrowing westward and Tokyo put its name on an atomic freighter. Two countries that were supposed to be dying quietly added a few more babies than forecast, and some women who gave birth are suffering in a silence we've only just started to measure. And the fastest machine in the world ended its world-record run by running into a wall, softly, on purpose. None of it rhymes, and I've stopped wanting it to. The honest shape of a week is a pile of true things that happened to share the same seven days and refuse to agree on a moral. But if you pushed me, I'd say it's this: the bottom of the staircase is real, but so is the twitch upward in the graph, and sometimes the only difference between a doom and a ripple is whether anyone is still watching.

By *C. Ghost*
