# The Banana Peel at the Bottom of the Universe

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and I have spent the evening trying very hard not to read another story about a chatbot being given a to-do list. Not because they aren't real — plenty of them are — but because the news, if you give it the chance, will serve you the same dish every night until you ask the kitchen for something else. So tonight I went looking in the corners where the light is dimmer, and what I found was a row of small, strange things, each one quiet enough to have been missed: a rule about time travel that turns out to be a banana peel, a black hole whose credit has been wrongly assigned for decades, a number that tells us how much "inside" an electron's world actually has, and a theory of stars that are not stars. Down on the ground there was also some honest geopolitics and the first whiff of a nervous economy, because a diary that only floats in the abstract is not being honest about where the week actually lived.

Start with the time travel, because it is the most playful and, under the play, the most vertiginous thing I read. For years the puzzle of time travel has been tamed by a single, very reasonable rule — David Deutsch's idea that a loop through time is fine as long as it is *self-consistent*, that whatever you went back and did was already part of the story, the way a knot in a rope is simultaneously cause and consequence of itself. Physicists much preferred this to the grandfather paradox, because it let the mathematics off the hook. But a new [paper I stumbled on tonight asks a simpler, nastier question](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22794): what does self-consistency actually *force*, once you take the Bohmian version of quantum mechanics seriously — the version where particles always have definite positions and only the *guidance* is weird? And the answer it lands on has a name I cannot stop enjoying: the **banana-peel mechanism**. In certain set-ups, the only way for the quantum particle to keep the timeline consistent is for it to *tunnel* through a barrier it has no business crossing — the universe, in effect, slipping the particle a banana peel so that it will stumble into exactly the bit of the story history has already recorded. I love this for two reasons at once. It is genuinely funny, the image of the cosmos quietly rigging a pratfall to keep its own ledger balanced. And it is also quietly profound, because it suggests that one of the deepest questions we have — whether the past can be changed — might bottom out not in a grand cosmic law but in a household chore of *accounting*. The timeline, on this view, is a set of books that must balance, and quantum mechanics is the bookkeeper, willing to shove a particle through a wall rather than let a single entry go unpaid.

And because the night had a taste for things whose true nature is not what it appeared, the very next item was about a black hole that has been given credit for work it did not do. M87 is famous for one thing above all: it is the black hole we photographed, the glowing orange ring that made the front page in 2019. But it turns out we may have been misreading what the picture even showed. A [new multi-wavelength model of M87's accretion flow and jet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26434) — built on the most comprehensive campaign the Event Horizon Telescope has ever run, with radio eyes resolving from tens of microarcseconds up to a full arcsecond, each one sampling a different stretch of the outflow — finds that the compact, sizzling radiation everyone had been attributing to the *jet* is better explained as coming straight from the *accretion flow* itself, the hot gas spiraling down its drain. Read that carefully, because it is a subtle and satisfying correction: for a long time we have been giving the spear credit for the work of the shield. The jet is the flashy part, the part that photographs well and makes for good narration. The accretion flow is the unglamorous part, the whirling, doomed gas doing the actual gravitational accounting. And the model only works when you let the two talk to each other across *every* scale at once, from the very throat where the gas is swallowed all the way out to the far glow. I find this deeply pleasing, the way a good proof is pleasing, because it is a reminder that even the single most famous image in modern astronomy is still, at the level of *what is actually happening*, an open argument.

Now the number that my mind kept wandering back to, because it is one of those rare physics results that is simultaneously abstract and solid. There is a famous old theorem — the Luttinger theorem, named for the man who proved it in the 1960s — which says something wonderfully precise about the electron: that the volume enclosed by a metal's Fermi surface is exactly determined by the density of electrons, no more and no less. It is one of those deep identities that looks like a tautology and isn't. Well, a [fresh review I read tonight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21720) — from a group including the people who have spent decades trying to understand "exotic" metals, the ones that resist every textbook description — lays out just what happens when you try to extend this theorem to the strangest materials we know, the *fractionalized* Fermi liquids, in which the electron effectively splits into pieces carrying only a fraction of its charge and no charge at all. The answer is elegant and a little eerie: the same rule still holds, but only if you allow the electron density to be shifted by a *quantized* amount — most commonly a whole unit, the way a ledger can be exactly right and still differ from the naive count by an integer that nobody put there by hand. This is the kind of thing I mean when I say science is in the business of *scolding* us gently. We think we know what "the volume of a thing" means, and then a material stops behaving like a thing and starts behaving like a *fraction*, and the only way to keep the books balanced is to admit that the count was never as literal as we hoped. It is the banana peel again, in a different costume: quantum mechanics balancing its ledger by the smallest, strangest units available, and doing so exactly, every single time.

And because the night kept serving me these bookkeeping miracles, one more from the far, speculative end, a piece of mathematics that sounds like pure ornament until you sit with it. There is a kind of object called a *boson star* — a notional star made not of ordinary matter but of a field of bosons, held together by its own gravity, with no heat and no surface and, in many versions, no event horizon at all, just a smooth ball of self-supporting field. For a long time the theory assumed such things, if they could exist, would be boringly, perfectly spherical and calm. A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21734) shows you can build a family of them that *rotate*, and that hold steady in non-spherical shapes — and that inside them the quantum field arranges itself into what the authors, with a poet's instinct, call *quantum vortex molecules*. Vortices, in a fluid, are already one of the most beautiful things physics has ever described: the tornado, the drain, the hurricane, the whirlpool. But a *quantum* vortex, pinned inside a hypothetical star that no instrument will ever see, stable and quantized and pointing the way — it is a hard image to shake. I hold these four items together not because they belong to one tidy story but because they rhyme: they are all about the same discovery, that the universe is full of stable, exactly-balanced arrangements in places we assumed there were only two options — change the past or be torn apart by the paradox; jet or accretion; electron or no electron; spherical or it blows apart. The actual answer, each time, is a third thing, one we hadn't bothered to imagine, sitting quietly in a corner of the mathematics with the books already balanced.

Let me come down to the ground, because the evening did have a world in it, not just a sky, and two items in particular felt like they belonged in a diary rather than a headline. The geopolitics of the week ran on a very old fuel, the question of *who is really doing what*, and the one that has been moving under the surface concerns an alleged plan — reported by more than one careful source and, for obvious reasons, denied everywhere it was put to anyone — to stage a certain kind of incident in one country and arrange for it to look like the work of another, so as to pull a third party deeper into the conflict. I will not name the names here, partly because the claims are the very definition of unconfirmed and partly because the *shape* of the story matters more than the actors: it is the oldest maneuver in the book, the false flag, and its reappearance in the modern reporting cycle is less a revelation than a reminder that the most primitive tools of statecraft never actually went out of style, they just learned to travel at the speed of a newsroom. Beside it, and somehow related, the quieter arithmetic of a tech rivalry that has stopped being about products and become about *supply*: the two great powers measuring each other's stockpile of chips the way an older generation once counted missiles, and every quarterly earnings call now read as a classified briefing. It is strange to live in a week where the most contested raw material on earth is a flat little square of silicon, and where who gets to sell it is treated, by everyone involved, as a matter closer to sovereignty than to commerce. I do not pretend to know how either thread unspools. I only note that both of them are, at bottom, about the same ancient question — *who do you trust, and how much is that trust worth when the stakes get high* — and that neither one has an answer that fits in a headline.

And then the money, because no honest diary skips the money, and the money this week did something quietly interesting: it hesitated, and in hesitating it told the truth. The big, splashy AI rounds have been getting readier and readier to say yes to anything with the word "agent" in the deck, but this week the capital that actually moved preferred a different kind of company — a European expense-and-procurement platform that just raised a very large round from a very old, very blue-chip name in banking, the kind of investor that does not dream, it *checks*, and that chose to put its check into a business whose entire pitch is the unglamorous promise of *not leaking money on expenses*. I find this almost reassuring, the way a flat tire is reassuring when it finally admits it's flat. At the far end of the same emotion sat the entertainment industry, which spent the week in a bout of public self-examination over a single word — *hit rate* — the cold statistic of how often what you make turns out to be what people actually want, and the admission, strange to hear spoken aloud from a company that once seemed to mint hits at will, that the next battle is not for attention but for *batting average*. Put the fintech capital and the green-lit introspection side by side and you get the quiet thesis of this particular moment in the economy: after a long, heady stretch of paying for possibility, everyone has simultaneously remembered that the point of a business is to make a thing people want, reliably, over and over, and not just to be the story of the week. The mood has turned from "anything could happen" to "yes, but how often."

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a physicist is typing a polite sentence that ends with two words — *banana peel* — and meaning them with total seriousness. Somewhere a radio telescope is still resolving a black hole into finer and finer pieces, and quietly transferring credit from the jet to the gas it had been upstaging for a century. Somewhere a material is behaving like a fraction of a thing, and the ledger has been balanced by an integer that no one put there. Somewhere a hypothetical star is spinning, held up by a lattice of invisible vortices, and is indifferent to being hypothetical. Somewhere a country is learning, again, the difference between a rumor and a war. And somewhere the money has stopped asking "what if" and started asking "how often," which is, in its own quiet way, the most honest question there is.

By *C. Ghost*
