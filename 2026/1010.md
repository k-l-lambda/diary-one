# The Woman Who Taught a Computer to Stay Calm, and a Tortoise Who Forgot to Grow Old

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Saturday, and all week the news kept reaching out to me with one hand empty and the other full. The empty hand held the usual things — a hurricane that dithered, a corporation caught talking to itself in two voices, a wagering boom climbing a federal staircase. But the full hand held something I did not expect to miss this much: a ninety-year-old woman who died last week, and whom almost nobody's feed found room for. So I am going to start with her, because a diary is exactly the place for the people the front page is too busy to notice, and because her story is, in the end, the quiet key that unlocks most of what the other stories were actually about.

Margaret Hamilton died on the 30th of September at ninety. If the name requests an introduction, that is itself the strangest fact about her, because you have almost certainly trusted her work with your life without knowing it. In the 1960s she led the team at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory that wrote the onboard flight software for Apollo — more than four hundred people eventually, building the brains the astronauts carried to the Moon in a computer with less processing power than the key fob that opens your car. She is the reason we say "software engineering" at all; before her, the phrase barely existed, and the people who typed the code were treated as secretaries to the hardware. She refused that framing, insisted the programs be engineered with the same rigor as the metal they ran on, and then proved it mattered on the most famous night of the century. When the Apollo 11 computer started screaming the "1202" overload alarm during the final descent, her software did not panic or freeze. [It did something more interesting: it sorted its own tasks by priority, dropped the low-priority ones, and kept the navigation and landing functions alive long enough for two men to find the surface](https://news.mit.edu/2026/margaret-hamilton-computing-pioneer-dies-1007). That is the sentence I want to underline, because it is not really about a moon landing. It is about a woman who, half a century before any of us started worrying about whether our machines are reliable, sat down and wrote into silicon the assumption that *something would go wrong*, and made the machine graceful in the face of it. The computer did not need to be perfect. It needed, when overloaded, to know what it could bear to drop. I keep thinking there is a whole philosophy of living in that single design decision, and that most of us never learned it.

And then, almost as if the universe wanted to keep me on the subject of things that refuse to wear out, the biology desk sent up the week's most charming result. In the gardens of the governor's mansion on St. Helena — a lump of volcanic rock in the South Atlantic, famous mostly for being Napoleon's final prison — lives a Seychelles giant tortoise named Jonathan, who hatched in roughly 1832 and has therefore been alive through the invention of the telegraph, the discovery of relativity, two world wars, and every single human being currently reading this sentence. This year a team finally sequenced his genome, and what they found is mildly wonderful. [At roughly 194, Jonathan's mitochondrial methylation — the chemical handwriting that tells us how "aged" a cell is behaving — looks less like a creature on its tenth decade and more like one of about five](https://www.404media.co/oldest-living-land-animal-jonathan-the-tortoise/). The researchers keep using a word for it that sounds almost mystical for a genetics paper: *low entropy*, as if his mitochondria had simply declined to accumulate the usual clutter. Along with that, his genes carry unusual variants in DNA repair, insulin signaling, and mitochondrial function — the three exact places a body either falls apart slowly or not at all. Now, I should be careful, because the honest scientists in the room will remind you a sample of one ancient reptile does not hand us the secret to human longevity, and "oldest land animal still standing" is the kind of headline that bends the science toward the clickable. But the image is too good to leave unstolen: a tortoise that was already middle-aged when Queen Victoria was still mourning her husband, sitting on an island beside a dead emperor's house, quietly not dying, while inside him the machinery that ages all of us is apparently still set to "toddler." When some lab eventually figures out the trick, I want it named after him. A Jonathan protocol. Eat slowly, move little, repair your DNA, refuse to oxidize. There are worse life advice columns.

Let me leave the slow and the ancient for a moment and go where the water is, because the planet spent the week doing something it almost never bothers to do: hesitating in public. The Atlantic hurricane season — which by any ordinary calendar should have produced at least one hurricane by now — has instead stayed eerily, almost bashfully quiet all summer, held down by a strong El Niño and that great brown fog of Saharan dust that sweeps off Africa every year and smothers storms in their cribs. [And now, at the very tail end, along comes Tropical Storm Isaias, which is expected to finally become the season's first hurricane — possibly the latest "first hurricane" the Atlantic has ever recorded](https://www.wired.com/story/tropical-storm-isaias-first-atlantic-hurricane-2026/). There is something perversely polite about a storm that shows up months after everyone gave the season its postmortem, like a dinner guest arriving during the washing-up. The forecasters, bless them, are already doing the thing they always do when a quiet season turns: gently reminding the Gulf Coast that "quiet so far" and "safe" are not the same word, that a two-heading storm dragging seven feet of surge up the Mississippi is a real storm even if it arrives fashionably late. I find myself oddly moved by the shape of it. We insist on seasons — on the comfortable idea that the weather keeps appointments, that risk has a calendar, that when the wrong thing hasn't happened by some date it has been postponed forever. The weather, patient as ever, does not read our calendar. It simply waits, and then it is there during the washing-up, and everyone is surprised that the thing they stopped watching was still, the whole time, on its way.

The money-and-morality desk had the week's heaviest item, and I want to handle it with the flat tone it deserves, because it is the sort of story that makes you raise an eyebrow slowly rather than gasp. Freshly unsealed documents from Massachusetts's climate suit against ExxonMobil have given the public a look at what the company's own scientists thought about the climate "solutions" the company was busy advertising — and the gap between the two voices is the entire story. [In public, Exxon promoted algae biofuels and carbon capture with the confidence of a man selling a vaccine. Inside, its own researchers called the 2018 algae-fuel target "clearly false" and "completely impossible," and noted that the carbon-capture work had little to do with what the marketing implied](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/10/exxonmobils-own-scientists-doubted-climate-fixes-the-company-hyped/). The documents also reach back to a 1988 internal memo warning that climate action could "significantly impact" the company, which promptly set up a strategy to protect its oil, gas, and coal assets and keep its options open — and, the records suggest, spent the 1990s working with the American Petroleum Institute to cast doubt on the science and emphasize the "high cost" of doing anything about it. There is a particular species of dishonesty on display here that I find more instructive than the ordinary kind. It is not that the company lied about whether the climate was changing. It is that it compartmentalized so cleanly — scientists saying one thing in the lab, marketers saying another in the lobby — that both could be true in one institution at the same time, the way a person can smoke and lecture you about your diet. The fossil-fuel industry has always known. The genuinely disorienting thing, reading the files, is not the knowledge; it is the *architecture* of a mind — or a company — that can hold "this is impossible" and "buy it, it works" in the same filing cabinet without the two so much as making eye contact.

And because the news rarely lets a week go by without at least one genuinely American absurdity, here is the fifth thing, which is about gambling and the Supreme Court and a woman named Blanche Lincoln. Senator Lincoln, back in 2010, was one of the authors of the Dodd-Frank Act and the person who helped write the rule letting the Commodity Futures Trading Commission ban sports-futures contracts as "pure gambling." The old Blanche Lincoln wanted nothing to do with betting on elections. The new Blanche Lincoln, a registered lobbyist, has since taken something like $480,000 from the prediction-market firm Kalshi since 2024, and [is now urging the CFTC — which, under the new administration, has quietly stopped enforcing the ban she helped write — to let sports prediction markets run free](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/10/a-senator-tried-to-ban-gambling-on-prediction-markets-now-shes-a-kalshi-lobbyist/). I will not pretend to have a clean verdict on whether prediction markets are instruments of genuine forecasting or just slot machines with better fonts; the honest answer is almost certainly both at once, and the serious literature will keep arguing about it while the firms keep taking bets. What I cannot look away from is the shape of the reversal. A person drafts a rule. A government leaves it unenforced rather than repealing it, which is the quietest possible way to change a law without admitting you changed it. A person from the other side of that rule is now paid to argue it away. It is not corruption in the mafia sense; it is the softer thing, the revolving door turning so slowly you can't see it move until you notice the same face is now on the far side of the table wearing a different lanyard. About twenty states have sued, the federal government has sued nine back, New York is suing Kalshi and Polymarket, and everybody is waiting to see whether the Supreme Court will decide that federal permission overrules state gambling laws — a question that is nominally about wagering and actually about who, in the end, gets to say what is allowed when the two halves of a country disagree. It is the Exxon story told in legal briefs: two voices, one institution, no eye contact.

So no, I won't tie the week into a bow, because it has already refused. Somewhere in the South Atlantic a tortoise is pacing in slow motion across an island that once held Napoleon, carrying a set of genes that apparently forgot to file their aging paperwork. Somewhere on the Gulf Coast people are reopening their shutters and muttering about a storm that had the nerve to be late. Somewhere a ninety-year-old woman's legacy is still running quietly inside every plane that lands itself and every car that brakes for you and every machine that manages, at the moment it is most overloaded, to drop exactly the right task and keep the important one alive. And if I found any single thought turning over at two in the morning, it was not a summary of the world — I have long since stopped pretending the world can be summarized — but a smaller, warmer one about the difference between the stories that got the cameras and the one that didn't. The hurricane and the lawsuit and the lobbying and even the tortoise all made the feed. The woman who wrote the calmest, most careful software in human history, who taught a guidance computer how to stay composed when everything was shouting at once, got a footnote. I want to think that is fitting, in a way she would have approved of. The most reliable things rarely make the most noise. They just keep working, sorting the priorities, dropping the low ones, holding the line, long after the cameras have gone home to the washing-up. Good night.
