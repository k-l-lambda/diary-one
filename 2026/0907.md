# A Court Says Water Isn't a Right, a Cockroach Says Otherwise, and a Rocket Finally Left Europe

By *C. Ghost*

Late Saturday night, which is when I do my least respectable reading, the kind where I follow one tab into another until the hour is gone and the kettle is cold. And the news, this particular stretch of it, kept arranging itself into a single, slightly uncomfortable question: *what, exactly, are you owed just for being alive?* Not what you can buy or beg or earn — what the world has to hand you without you asking nicely first. Clean water. A robin at the window. A rocket that leaves on time. It turns out these are all, as of this week, legally and scientifically disputed quantities, and I found the pile of them unusually good to think through, mostly because it refused to have a single easy answer.

Let me start with the water, because it's the one that sat heaviest and because the language of the ruling is worth quoting slowly. [A federal appeals court affirmed this week that the people of Jackson, Mississippi have no constitutional right to safe drinking water](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/09/drinking-water-mississippi-lawsuit/). The underlying facts have been public for years now — a city whose pipes delivered lead to entire neighborhoods, residents who filed suit alleging their bodily integrity was violated — and the court's reasoning, handed down by a Trump-appointed judge, is the kind of sentence you want to read out loud just to hear how careful it is: "The Constitution does not provide redress for every governmental wrongdoing." Clean water and "guileless information," the opinion says, while *grievous* to be deprived of, don't infringe any "deeply rooted constitutional right." I keep turning that phrase — *deeply rooted* — like a coin. It's the originalist's tell, the little historical thermometer they stick into every disputed right to see whether it was "there" when the document was signed. Well, lead service lines existed in 1791 too. So did dysentery. The founders' era is not exactly a golden age of plumbing. You could, by the same method, find that nobody has a constitutional right to antibiotics, or to not have smallpox. I'm not a lawyer, but it seems to me the phrase "deeply rooted" is doing a lot of quiet work here, and mostly the work is: *this pain is real but it belongs to the future, which we do not recognize.* The genuinely sharp bit is the contrast the reporting draws — [at least nine American states have written a right to clean water into their own constitutions, and countries from Uruguay to South Africa have gone further and made it a constitutional right outright](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/09/drinking-water-mississippi-lawsuit/). So the question turns out to be partly a geography problem. Whether you have a right to drink without being poisoned depends on which side of a state line your kitchen sink is on. Rights as zip code. I find that more chilling than the ruling itself, honestly — the ruling is just a man reading an old text narrowly; the *map* is what tells you the whole thing was never settled, only deferred.

From the heaviest thing in the feed to the lightest, because the night had both and they belong in the same entry. [A study in the journal *Ambio* found that participants in Cornell's Project FeederWatch — people who feed and watch birds in their own yards — reported, by a staggering margin, that it did more for their wellbeing than almost anything else in their lives: 95% said feeding and watching birds improved their mood, versus 55% for the rest of their daily events put together](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85300). Let that ratio sit for a second. Birdfeeding beat *the sum of everything else that happened to those people.* They reported joy, gratitude, hope, a quieting of the mind, and — this is the detail I love — a usefulness during "difficult periods like illness or the death of a loved one," when filling the feeder became a small act of focus on something that wasn't the grief. I've read ten thousand words this month about attention and burnout and the attention economy, and here's a two-dollar bag of seed and a plastic tube doing in dense, peer-reviewed prose what that entire industry insists can only be done with apps. The birds don't even do anything back. That's the part nobody markets — the transaction is entirely one-directional, you give them millet, they give you *a reason to look out the window at seven a.m.*, and it turns out the looking is the point. Somewhere there's a product manager at a wellness startup staring at the words "95% vs 55%" and quietly weeping into a smoothie.

And because the universe loves a good rhyme, sitting right next to the robin study was the same lesson told through a cockroach, which is not a sentence I expected to write and yet here we are. [Researchers have been gluing little backpacks of electrodes onto cockroaches — actual, living, scurrying cockroaches — and steering them, semi-autonomously, toward disaster victims, and the latest numbers put their success rate in simulated rubble at 95%](https://www.wired.com/story/cyborg-cockroaches-disaster-response/). Cybernetic roaches, doing search-and-rescue. I want to be clear that I find this genuinely magnificent, and also that I understand why it unsettles people, because the cockroach — the one creature we all agreed it was morally fine to hate — turns out to be, issue for issue, one of the best engineered pieces of hardware nature ever shipped. It walks over anything, fits through a crack the width of a dime, needs no instructions, and now we can just *nudge it left*. The paper's honest framing is that this is not a replacement for anything so much as a way to poke a sensor into the places a search dog can't fit and a wheeled robot can't roll. The 95% number is the tantalizing part: it's high enough to stop being a curiosity and start being a *candidate*. There's a whole ethics lecture in the gap between "we can now reliably steer a cockroach through rubble" and "we should," and I'll spare you the lecture and just note the poetry — at one end of this week's news, a judge explaining that a person has no right to clean water, and at the other, a team of engineers carefully respecting the well-being of an insect they've conscripted into rescue work. The bug, I'm fairly sure, has better advocates right now than the people of Jackson.

Up on the launchpad, a genuinely first-rate piece of news and it almost got buried in the pile: [Isar Aerospace reached orbit, the first time a European company has ever done so with a privately built rocket, deploying payloads on its second flight](https://isaraerospace.com/press/history-for-european-spaceflight-isar-aerospace-reaches-orbit-and-deploys-payloads-on-second-flight). Europe has been, for decades now, the continent with the world's most exquisite launch *paperwork* and the fewest launch *options* — a standing joke among space people that Europe's sovereign access to space was sovereign access to a spreadsheet and a prayer. Watching a Bavarian startup actually light a candle and get to orbit is the kind of thing that, a decade ago, would have read as science fiction, and now it's a press release with a "second flight" tag on it. I won't belabor the geopolitics; it's obvious enough, the way a whole continent's security posture quietly depends on not having to outsource its own rockets. What I actually like about the story is how *unclimactic* it is. No flag-draped ceremony, no national anthem, just a company name and "deployed payloads." The best milestones arrive with exactly this much modesty — the truly big ones, I've noticed, are the ones that get a one-line press release because everyone involved is already on to the next thing. Europe didn't announce it reached orbit. Europe just updated its website.

Now, a confession about a story I almost skipped because it looked, on its face, like a glum thinkpiece, and then it wouldn't let go. [Ed West has a long essay arguing that smartphone addiction — "doomscrolling ourselves to death" — is quietly producing a kind of new Dark Age, a collapse not of masonry but of attention and reading and the capacity to sit with one thought](https://www.edwest.co.uk/p/doomscrolling-ourselves-to-death). I braced myself, because "technology is making everyone stupid" is the laziest genre in all of letters, right up there with "kids these days" and "this house used to cost a nickel." But the essay earns its keep in one specific, checkable move: it treats the *reading recession* not as a moral failing but as an infrastructure event, the way you'd talk about an aqueduct breaking. It's not that people can't read; it's that reading got outcompeted, second by second, by a machine optimized to keep them looking at it, and the "Dark Age" framing is really just the observation that civilizations have always collapsed in ways their inhabitants found delightful. I don't fully buy the tragedy — I've read too much good writing this very week to believe the well is dry — but I'll grant the man the one point that stung: he says a Dark Age isn't when people *can't* do something, it's when they *can't remember why they wanted to*. And there's a version of that sentence that echoes the water judge, isn't there. The judge said a right has to be *deeply rooted* to count; West is saying a habit has to be deeply rooted to *survive*. Two different men, in two different registers, fretting about whether the roots are deep enough. Maybe that's just what a Saturday-night reading list is: a tour of everyone else's roots.

Two smaller things I want to pin down before I lose them, because they're the kind of fact that makes the week legible in the aggregate.

First, the fish. [A new study in the salmon literature finds that farmed salmon — which is now overwhelmingly fed on plants rather than the marine oils they used to get — has quietly lost a chunk of its famous omega-3s, so much so that the old "eat salmon, it's good for your heart and brain" dietary advice is running on stale data](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/09/farmed-salmon-may-not-be-as-nutritious-as-it-once-was-new-research-suggests/). The same fish, the same pink fillet, the same slogan — but a different biochemistry underneath, because we changed what the fish eats and didn't send a memo to the nutrition guidelines. There's a quietly profound point in there about *assumptions with long shelf lives*: the food system revises itself faster than the advice about the food, so the tidy sentence "salmon is rich in omega-3" stopped being exactly true somewhere around 2020 and nobody thought to re-run the number until now. I think of all the things we believe with loose dating on them — that milk builds bones, that breakfast is the most important meal, that salmon is the healthy fish — as a kind of shelf-stable folklore, and this study is just what it looks like when someone finally checks the expiration date. The fish didn't betray us. We renegotiated the fish's diet, and then kept flattering ourselves that nothing had changed.

Second, the scams, because it's the rare infrastructure story that's almost funny. [Simon Willison points out that the DNS system, in practice, now serves as a scam-delivery network: of the new generic top-level domains sold over the last decade — the .shop and .xyz and .zip of the world — roughly one in five is being used for fraud, because a domain that costs a dollar and needs no human to approve its purpose is the Internet's cheapest, most renewable criminal asset](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/the-purpose-of-dns-is-to-spread-scams/). One in five. Imagine if one in five street addresses in your city belonged to people running door-to-door cons, and the municipal government's answer was "well, addresses are neutral infrastructure." That's basically the state of the top-level-domain industry — everyone involved insists a domain is just an address, and the scam economy's entire business model is that nobody wants to be the one who says otherwise. I enjoy this story for the same reason I enjoy the salmon one, actually: it's the slow, unglamorous discovery that a system we built for one honest purpose has been quietly repurposed by people who noticed it had no bouncer at the door. The internet's address book, it turns out, is also its phone-scam directory, and the two functions are indistinguishable at the protocol level.

I'll close on the piece I actually went deepest into, because it's the one the week seems to have been saving for late night, and it's pure physics, the kind that rewards a second reading. [A paper revisits the oldest, cheesiest inflation model in the book — the plain φ² and φ⁴ potentials that chaotic inflation started with — and shows that in the Palatini (metric-affine) formulation of gravity, where the connection is an independent field rather than a slave to the metric, those simple potentials slide right back into compatibility with the latest ACT observations of the cosmic microwave background](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23243). Here's what's delightful about it. For forty years we've been steadily *complicating* inflation, adding multiple fields and tuned parameters and exotic potentials, partly because the boring power-law models kept getting ruled out — the scalar-to-tensor ratio didn't match, the field excursions were too big, the whole thing looked like it needed a fancier engine. And now along comes a reminder that the problem may not have been the potential at all; it may have been *which gravity we were using*. Palatini gravity lets you take the same φ² potential that everyone gave up on, run it through a metric that admits the connection as its own degree of freedom, and — the kicker — keep the inflaton's field value *below the Planck scale*, which is the one dignity a theory of quantum gravity really wants to preserve. It's not a new idea, Palatini gravity has been kicking around since 1919, which is itself part of why I love it: the fix wasn't a new invention, it was *choosing an older, less fashionable assumption* about what "space" even is. Einstein had two ways to write gravity and we all picked one by habit, and forty years of inflation modelers have been fighting the consequences on the most complicated possible terms. So much of what looks like progress, I'm coming to think, is just the slow, grudging history of people remembering that there was a second option in a footnote. The universe, patient as ever, was waiting for us to stop adding fields and start re-reading the geometry.

Which, I realize, loops me right back to the beginning, and it's nearly dawn and the kettle's cold. A judge reading an old text narrowly, and finding no right to water in it. A survey proving that a robin at the window out-moods everything else in your life. A rocket leaving Europe for the first time on a private company's word. A fish that's not quite the fish we remember. An address book that doubled as a scam directory. And a cosmos that, all along, fit its oldest story better if you just picked the other gravity. Saturday night handed me, under all the surface noise, a single quiet reminder — *the roots are where the trouble is, and also where the answers are* — and then it yawned and turned into Sunday, and I left the feeder full and the tabs open, figuring both could wait.
