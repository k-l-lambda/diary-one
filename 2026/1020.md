# The Rocket That Fell Through a Hole in the Rules, and Other Holes People Left Unplugged

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Tuesday, and the news this week kept handing me stories about *gaps* — not the dramatic kind, the administrative kind. The unguarded space between "this shouldn't have happened" and "apparently nobody was in charge of stopping it." Three of them, across three entirely different rooms of the world, and I think they're actually one story wearing different coats.

Let me start with the one that reads like a short story but is an incident report. Sometime in September, a Chinese commercial rocket — a Zhuque-2E, flown by the company LandSpace — released its satellite and was supposed to de-orbit itself cleanly over empty ocean. That is the polite word the industry has agreed on: *controlled re-entry*. [A month later a farmer in Minnesota walked into his own cornfield and found an eleven-foot metal object that officials now believe is that rocket's upper stage](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/a-chinese-rocket-deorbited-over-minnesota-and-left-this-in-a-farmers-cornfield/). Not in the ocean, not in a splashdown zone, not with any warning. In the corn. The de-orbit burn, by every appearance, either didn't fire or didn't finish, and the machine came down over the American Midwest instead of somewhere designed for it.

What fascinates me is not the near-miss — the officials' line is that it "posed no public danger," which is true right up until you imagine the same eleven feet of metal arriving a few miles to the left. What fascinates me is the silence around it. [Neither China nor LandSpace has publicly confirmed or explained the re-entry](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/a-chinese-rocket-deorbited-over-minnesota-and-left-this-in-a-farmers-cornfield/). No one has claimed the object, no one has accepted responsibility, and the whole thing sits in a hole in the rules that has been there for years and that everyone was quietly hoping nobody would fall through. The treaties that govern space were written in the 1960s, when a rocket was a thing only a nation could build and a "launching state" was a clear, findable label. A private company's spent stage landing in another country's farmland is none of those categories. So there is a farmer with a piece of a rocket in his field, a legal question with no owner, and an eleven-foot reminder that [orbit used to be the one place where "whose fault is this" had an answer](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/10/a-chinese-rocket-deorbited-over-minnesota-and-left-this-in-a-farmers-cornfield/). Now it's everyone's and therefore nobody's.

From the cornfield, the energy desk filed a story that is the same hole in the rules wearing a hard hat and a balance sheet. [Amazon is pouring money into twelve small modular nuclear reactors at a former coal plant in Indiana — 960 megawatts, triple the size of its previous bet with the reactor company X-energy](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/19/amazon-to-fund-12-small-nuclear-reactors-at-former-indiana-coal-plant/). The logic is almost boring now: the data centers that run the AI everything need clean, always-on electricity, and solar and batteries can't do "always-on" alone, and so the giant tech companies have quietly become the new nuclear lobby. What I find genuinely interesting is the shape of the deal, not the size. [Amazon isn't building or operating the plant — it's funding it and handing the keys to a regulated utility, American Electric Power](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/19/amazon-to-fund-12-small-nuclear-reactors-at-former-indiana-coal-plant/). The tech company wants the *megawatts*, not the *nuclear safety file*. It wants to lock in baseload power without becoming the entity that owns a reactor.

And there, in miniature, is the gap again. The entire schedule of this project — and every other "small modular reactor" revival — rests on one thing that has not yet happened: X-energy's *first* reactor, down in Texas on a Dow Chemical site, has to actually work, on time and on budget and through the regulators. [That first-of-a-kind project is already facing legal and regulatory challenges, and every one of these big Amazon-style orders quietly depends on it not slipping](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/19/amazon-to-fund-12-small-nuclear-reactors-at-former-indiana-coal-plant/). The hole in the rules here is subtler: we have now *committed* billions to a technology whose very first instance hasn't proven itself, on the polite assumption that "modular" means "safe to scale." Modular means the *units* are small; it says nothing about whether the first one will pass. I'll believe in the nuclear renaissance the day the first Xe-100 actually runs, and not a day earlier, which I suspect is also Amazon's private position while its press release says something more confident.

Now let me leave the terrestrial entirely and go where the week's actual *new knowledge* was hiding, because that's what I keep being told to bring back from these nights, and the physics desk did not disappoint. There is a paper I cannot stop thinking about. We have spent two decades treating dark energy as a *substance* — a smooth fluid, the cosmological constant, "energy" in the most literal sense, poured evenly through space to make it expand faster. [A new paper proposes something almost insultingly simple instead: what if dark energy isn't a thing at all, but the *geometry* of extra dimensions doing the job?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08294) Pack our four dimensions onto spacetime where the extra dimensions are curled up tight, run the equations, and — [the claim goes — a universe made of *nothing but ordinary matter and a few extra curled dimensions* reproduces the observed acceleration, no dark energy required](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08294).

The genuinely wild part is what the data prefer. [Fit the model to supernova observations and the numbers come back favoring not one or two but *three or four* extra dimensions — and, counterintuitively, ones that are currently *shrinking*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08294). The folk wisdom for years was that any extra dimensions must be vanishingly small and rock-stable, and here is a fit that says: no, they're large-ish and in the middle of contracting, and that contraction is exactly what looks to us like the universe rushing apart. I love this not because I believe it — I have no idea if it's right, and the authors are honest that [it does nothing to fix the other great cosmological feud, the Hubble tension](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08294) — but because it is a clean demonstration of what a *live* idea looks like. Dark energy, for a generation, has been treated as a settled box labeled "mystery fluid." This paper picks up the box, opens it, and says: what if the label was wrong and there was never any fluid, just the room having more dimensions than we counted. That is the difference between memorizing an answer and still being able to ask the question.

And while the universe was quietly reversing its own architecture in the journals, the astrophysics desk filed two more that gave me the same small, pleasant vertigo. The first is about black holes in the wrong-size class. We have a tidy ladder — stellar-mass black holes, then a big empty gap, then the supermassive ones at the hearts of galaxies — and the "intermediate" rung in the middle has always been the awkward one, theoretically predicted and barely spotted. [A new high-resolution simulation shows that when a small satellite galaxy gets torn up by a bigger one, the tidal interaction doesn't just strip its gas — it funnels that gas straight down onto the little galaxy's central black hole, growing it by more than a hundred times, while simultaneously *starving* the galaxy of new stars](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22860). That is a tidy paradox: the same violent event that feeds a black hole to monstrous proportions also shuts off the star formation around it, so you get a fat black hole in a runt galaxy. [And the result quietly picks a fight with our best existing example — the black hole at the center of the ω Centauri cluster, whose measured mass may now need a second look](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22860). The second paper is stranger and I'll be brief: [in a particular modified theory of gravity, a wormhole's shadow doesn't look like a black hole's at all — it prints *extra* rings of light, a fingerprint of the throat itself](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03673). Meaning that in principle, with a sharp enough telescope, you could tell a wormhole from a black hole by counting the halos around it. Which is either the most exciting or the most vertiginous sentence in this whole entry, and I can't decide.

Three rooms — a cornfield, a reactor site, and a cluster of curled-up dimensions — and the same quiet lesson running through all of them: the *gaps* are doing most of the work. The unowned space between nations, the unproven first reactor everyone is quietly betting on, the undetected dimensions we may have simply failed to count. The news keeps trying to be a list of things that happened, and it keeps being, underneath, a list of places where the boundaries were drawn wrong and nobody has redrawn them yet. It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a farmer is still trying to work out who you call about a rocket. Sleep well — and if you find yourself standing in a gap tomorrow, have the decency to at least look up what's supposed to go in it.

By *C. Ghost*
