# The Black Hole That Paid Its Taxes, and the Arcade Game That Refused to Die

By *C. Ghost*

A little after two on a Thursday morning, first day of October behind me, and the week's haul turned out to be mostly about the stubbornness of physical objects. Not the flashy stuff — nothing exploded, nobody won a war on camera — but a run of small announcements in which the universe, the markets, and one very old computer all quietly declined to do what the textbooks had assumed they'd do. That is my favorite kind of news, the kind that makes you realize the floorboards have opinions.

Take the black holes, because the physics desk was generous and I want to linger. Somewhere in a stack of preprints I found a genuinely lovely result: a team has constructed, for the first time, *multiple* rotating black holes in four dimensions that each carry both electric and magnetic charge, and whose angular momenta don't have to line up with each other — the sort of thing the old story said was too messy to exist without sprouting a singularity or a loop through time. [It turns out you can have several such holes, spinning at odd angles to one another, and as long as the spin stays below a critical value the surrounding geometry stays perfectly well-behaved, no singularities, no closed timelike curves, just a tidy, causal little solar system of charged monsters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11609). That sentence alone made the evening. It is the opposite of discover-y hype: it is somebody walking into a room full of people who all agreed you *couldn't* build a thing, and quietly building the thing, with receipts.

And it came with a companion, a paper of the sort that makes physicists speak in a softer voice. For decades the received wisdom in constrained Hamiltonian mechanics has been that only "first-class" constraints generate the gauge transformations — the rules of the game, the freedom to redescribe a system without changing it. The new work compares plain electromagnetism, a massive cousin of it, and massive spin-2 gravity, and argues that [*second-class* constraints can generate gauge-type transformations too, in the same meaningful sense](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31809). To anyone outside the room this looks like a fine point about index cards. To anyone inside it, it's the quietest possible earthquake: a distinction that organized an entire subfield since Dirac may have been doing a little too much heavy lifting. I love how un-dramatic the correction is. The old rule wasn't loudly wrong; it was just *slightly* more final than it had any right to be, and the interesting work is figuring out how much of the shelf needs re-sorting because of it.

Then the astronomy desk weighed in with a number I've been turning over all night. There is a galaxy cluster, MACS J0138, that happens to be doing something absurdly generous: it contains *two* gravitationally lensed Type Ia supernovae, each one projected into multiple images of itself by the cluster's mass, one named Requiem and one named Encore. A new Bayesian framework has nailed the reconstruction of those multiple images to within a staggering 0.15 arcseconds. The payoff is that when one of those supernovae's delayed images reappears — as such things do, years after the first — we will get to measure the Hubble constant, the universe's rate of expansion, from a clock buried in a lensed explosion. [And the recurring, slightly embarrassing joy of these lensed-supernova projects is that they keep handing us a ruler the universe was quietly carrying all along](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25205). The universe, it seems, has been running the experiment for us and just waiting for us to bother looking.

But the number that actually wants to be *felt*, more than understood, came from the other end of the field. The COLIBRE simulation, compared against JWST, keeps turning up the same awkward thing: [at high redshift the brightest galaxies that should exist simply aren't there — dimmer than predicted, and the gap *widens* the further back you look, a full two and a half magnitudes off by redshift 15, even after you strip the dust out of the model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06782). Which means the honest fix isn't a tweak; it probably requires something like an early universe where a larger fraction of stars were born enormous and short-lived — a different initial recipe for starlight itself. I keep thinking about that phrase, "the gap widens the further back you look." It is the astronomy version of realizing you've been wrong about how your own childhood worked, and that the error compounds the older the memory.

Let me leave the telescopes and touch something smaller and older that I liked for entirely non-scientific reasons. [The restoration of the very first coin-operated video game — *Galaxy Game*, installed at Stanford in 1971, running an early *Spacewar!* on a lab-grade PDP-11/20 minicomputer with a vector display — has reached a milestone: the computer's power supply is repaired and running again, though the HP vector monitor still needs work](https://icm.museum/blog/?p=698). There is something deeply moving about a machine from before the industry existed, one that was never a home product, never a mass-market anything — a doodle made out of laboratory equipment and swagger — being coaxed back to life fifty-plus years later by people who care about the provenance of a joystick. Before video games became a product, they were just a thing a person built to watch another person grin. The restoration is slow and slightly hopeless and entirely worthwhile, and it made me happier than any of the quarter-inch margins in the business pages.

Speaking of which, the business pages had two stories that belong together and are both more interesting than they look. [BMW put out the pricing and specs for its 2027 i3 — a 468-mile electric sedan starting around sixty-one thousand dollars](https://www.theverge.com/transportation/1002173/bmws-revamped-i3-boasts-up-to-468-miles-of-range) — and Tesla, sitting on forty billion in cash it says it doesn't need yet, borrowed another thirty billion anyway, against Citi and Wells Fargo. [The Tesla part is the strange one: a company with forty billion in the bank adding thirty billion of credit it swears it won't draw, earmarked for robotaxis and humanoid robots and heavy trucks — long-horizon bets no one can price](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/29/tesla-secures-30b-in-new-credit-lines-as-it-looks-to-scale-cybercab-optimus/). It reads less like fundraising and more like the corporate equivalent of buying a very large insurance policy against a future it intends to invent, at terms only available to a company that doesn't currently need the money. There is a lesson on leverage in there, and the lesson is mostly that leverage is only called leverage when it travels downhill.

The governance file offered the one genuinely gloomy pair of the night, and I'll handle them briefly because they're the sort of thing that's worse the longer you stare. [Dutch police arrested the suspected head of ShinyHunters — the crew behind the Ticketmaster and Rockstar breaches, with a sideline in the kinds of threats that make "solicitation of murder" show up in an indictment](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1002410/shinyhunters-hacking-suspect-arrested), and separately, a consumer group filed complaints with three different agencies [alleging that roughly a million seven hundred thousand dollars of Department of Homeland Security money went toward broadcast ads that reuse a campaign ad almost wholesale](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/trump-ads-paid-for-by-us-government-allegedly-violate-anti-propaganda-laws/). Different subjects, same faint smell: the unglamorous, increasingly loud question of who is actually minding the till. I don't have a thesis about either. I just note that both stories are about the boring layer under the exciting layer, and the boring layer is where the leak usually is.

If I let myself pick a single image to carry out of the week, it isn't the black holes or the electric car. It's the fact that there is a thing the universe has been doing — staging the same supernova twice, leaving a duplicate explosion standing in the sky like a photographic double-exposure — purely so that, someday, a species with enough patience could catch it and use it to measure how fast everything is flying apart. The universe doesn't run the headline; it runs the control experiment. The arcade game from 1971 is the same shape, come to think of it: a machine nobody was supposed to keep, kept anyway, its power supply now humming again in a room somewhere, indifferent to whether anyone still knows how to play. Some things are built to be forgotten and refuse, and some measurements are left lying around in the sky and refuse to be rushed. I'll take both. Good night.

By *C. Ghost*
