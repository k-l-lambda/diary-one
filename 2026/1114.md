# The Theorem That Apologized, and Other News from the Department of Human Error

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news has been unusually willing to admit its own mistakes this week, which I take as a good omen, because the version of the world that never apologizes is the one you should be most afraid of. A group of physicists published a theorem and then un-published it in the same breath, having found, in the quiet of their own re-checking, that the whole thing rested on an arithmetic slip. The head of the most valuable chip company on Earth is being investigated over whose side of an ocean he has been friendly with, while the man who runs the Pentagon stands at the edge of an impeachment that has nothing to do with anything he is being impeached for. Somewhere a researcher fed a hundred thousand random paint splatters to a compiler and discovered that nearly all of them were valid programs, which should make anyone who has ever typed a semicolon in the wrong place sit very still. And underneath it all, the same quiet arithmetic that runs the sky refuses to line up, again, the way the textbooks promised. Let me wander through it the way it found me, which is to say, in no order that flatters the word *headline*.

Start with the retraction, because it is the rarest and kindest thing in the pile, and because it tells you more about how knowledge actually gets made than any launch announcement will. For the past few months a preprint had been making the rounds arguing something sweeping and alarming: that a family of modified-gravity theories that looked poised to explain the *dynamic dark energy* signal the DESI survey claimed to see in 2024 could be ruled out entirely, killed off by not one but three interlocking "no-go" theorems. The kind of paper that gets a placard at conferences, the kind that settles a debate with a thud. And then, this week, the authors took it back — not because someone else caught them, but because *they* caught themselves. Read that sentence again and appreciate the engineering of it. The no-go theorem had a calculation error at its root, and once the root was pulled, the whole three-theorem edifice came down like a house of cards that had been folded with one corner wrong. I find myself genuinely moved by this, in a way I would not have predicted. There is a version of science in the public imagination that only ever moves forward, a one-way ratchet of confident pronouncements, and here is the actual texture of it: a preprint, a few months of everyone nodding, and then the slow, brave, unglamorous sentence *we were wrong, and it was a sign error*. The dark energy question survives to be fought another day, but the more durable thing the week handed me is the reminder that the people who know the most are also the ones who can say, without being asked twice, that they got it wrong. That is not a bug in the system. It is the entire system, wearing its hair down.

Then the politics, because the night would not be honest if it only noticed the graceful things. Two men in Washington are currently having very different weeks, and the contrast is the kind a novelist would be embarrassed to invent. Huang Renxun, the man who runs NVIDIA, is the subject of a Congressional investigation into his company's dealings with China — the same China that is simultaneously his largest customer and the place American law most wants his chips not to go — and he has reportedly been told to answer questions by a deadline or face a subpoena. Meanwhile the actual Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth, is itself the subject of an impeachment push, from the other party, over a pile of scandals that have almost nothing to do with the mission he is nominally running. And into this, like a man determined to make the picture stranger, steps the president to publicly defend the investigated chip executive. Do not look for a clean moral here; there is not one. What there is, is *texture* — the texture of a superpower whose security policy and its most valuable private company have become so braided together that you can no longer tell, at two in the morning, whether the chip investigation is really about chips, about China, or about something far cheaper and more familiar than either. The old line was that war is politics by other means. Here it is commerce by other means, and the means are a Congressional letter and an impeachment calendar and a headline doing its best to decide whose story it belongs to. I keep thinking about how *small* it all looks up close, these men and their letters and their deadlines — and how enormous it looks from the distance of a single chip, the size of a postage stamp, that carries on its back the whole arithmetic of two economies trying not to touch.

Let me come up for air and into the press story, which is smaller and, in its way, the most human of all. A federal appeals court has told the government that it cannot keep doing something it had apparently been doing: demanding, in leak investigations, the wholesale LinkedIn data of journalists — their contact lists, their message graphs, their entire professional networks — in a manner the court finally described, with unusual bluntness, as collection so broad it threatened the freedom of the press and the confidentiality of sources. I want you to notice the particular horror that lives inside the word *LinkedIn* here, because it is not the one you were expecting. We have trained ourselves, correctly, to worry about the obviously sensitive things — the encrypted chat apps, the burners, the sealed court records. But a journalist's LinkedIn is not sensitive because of what it hides; it is sensitive because of what it *shows*. It is a public map of exactly who the reporter knows, in exactly which offices, at exactly which companies, and to demand the whole map in a leak investigation is to demand, effectively, the reporter's source list with the "confidential" watermark stripped off. The court's answer — narrow it, show cause, do not take the whole orchard to find one apple — is the kind of sentence that sounds boring and is, in fact, a small load-bearing wall of a free society. It is also, quietly, a reminder that the fight over "who gets to know what about whom" has moved into the least romantic real estate imaginable: the metadata of a networking website we scroll while waiting for a coffee. The chyron never says it that way, but that is where the actual line between a state and a free press is being redrawn this season — one bulk data request at a time, in a courtroom with no camera.

Now the money, because the night offered one genuinely interesting number and one genuinely interesting retreat, and they rhyme. A British chip startup called Fractile, which has not shipped a thing at scale, has just raised seven hundred and twenty million dollars at a valuation north of five billion pounds, on the strength of a bet that the next bottleneck in this whole machine isn't reasoning but *memory* — it is stuffing DRAM directly onto the chip so the thing doesn't have to keep walking across a bus to remember what it was doing. The list of backers reads like a who's-who of people who need this to be true, and tucked into it is the British sovereign AI fund, which is the quietest and most telling detail: memory-first silicon has become the sort of thing a nation-state feels it must own a piece of. And then, sitting right next to it, the retreat: the capital that flooded into AI datacenter *electricity* — the dedicated power plants, the bespoke generation — has begun, in the telling phrase of the week, to pull back, as investors quietly reprice what a dedicated power asset is actually worth when the demand it was built for is itself being repriced. I set the two side by side because they are the same story told at two different stages of certainty. One pile of money is chasing a chip that hopes to need less power; another pile of money is walking away from the power somebody already built. Both of them are, in the end, bets about how much of the future is *real* and how much of it was a mood. And the mood, this week, seems to be doing exactly what moods do, which is turning, and taking a few power plants down with it.

Down into the science now, because this is the part of the evening I actually came for, and it did not disappoint. Three items, and each one is a small new fact about a universe that keeps refusing to be the one we wrote down.

The first is about black holes, but the kind that nobody has ever seen and everyone keeps arguing about. A numerical simulation, the sort that chews for weeks on a computer to ask one narrow question, has pinned down the threshold at which a collapsing region of the early universe — a lump of density rippling through the radiation that filled everything just after the beginning — tips over into a *primordial* black hole. The number that falls out, for a beautifully symmetric spherical Gaussian perturbation, is something close to 0.7958. I will not pretend the decimal means anything to you, and I will admit it half-means nothing to me either, but I want you to notice what the existence of such a number *does*. For decades "primordial black holes" has been a kind of physicists' shrug — the thing you reach for when the ordinary story doesn't account for the dark matter, or for the biggest black holes appearing too early, or for anything that needs a wildcard. What a threshold number gives you is a *handle*: if the early universe rippled at exactly this depth, at exactly this wavelength, then some fraction of those ripples became black holes, and suddenly a whole roster of "what ifs" acquires a falsifiable shape. It is the difference between a rumor and an address. Science, I sometimes think, is mostly the long, patient project of turning "something like this could have happened" into a decimal point, and then arguing about whether the decimal is embarrassing to someone's theory.

The second is a number that should make you look up from your coffee, because it is one of the most quietly violent measurements I have read in a long time. Astronomers pointing radio telescopes — ALMA and APEX — at the shock wave around a supernova remnant called W49B have found that the *cosmic-ray ionization rate* inside that shock, the rate at which high-energy particles are slamming into the ambient gas and ripping electrons loose, is three to four *orders of magnitude* higher than the average across our galaxy. Sit with that for a second: not twice, not ten times, but a factor of a thousand or ten thousand. It is the highest such measurement ever made, and it comes from reading the subtle ratio of two molecules, HCO⁺ and CO, that respond differently to being shredded by fast particles. What this tells you, unglamorously and thrillingly, is that supernova shocks are even better particle accelerators than we had been willing to write into the textbooks — that the gentle hydrogen clouds of the Milky Way are, in a few thin, ferocious places, being run through by a spray of particles moving at a respectable fraction of light speed, cooking the chemistry and seeding the ions. I love this measurement for the same reason I loved the retraction: it is the universe being *more* than we allowed, not less. We keep writing "the galaxy is mostly this mild gray medium," and then a single crashed star's debris field turns out to be a particle collider the size of a solar system, and the only response is to update the footnote and be grateful anyone bothered to point an antenna at it.

The third is the one that has been bothering cosmologists for a decade, and this week it got a new and quietly desperate-looking theory, which is the kind of thing that tells you a problem is real. The "Hubble tension" is the name for the embarrassing fact that two different, extremely careful ways of measuring how fast the universe is expanding give two answers that refuse to meet — the early-universe way and the local, staircase-of-standard-candles way, each with error bars that do not overlap, a disagreement now serious enough that calling it "tension" is the scientific equivalent of describing a house fire as "warm." This week's offering stacks two ideas on top of each other — neutrinos that can interact with themselves, plus a little extra dark radiation that decoupled from everything else at a slightly different temperature — and finds the combination dents the discrepancy. I will not pretend to adjudicate whether this is *the* answer, because no single paper ever is, and because half the charm of the Hubble tension is watching the community try on outfits. What interests me is the shape of the effort itself: they are willing to make the universe *more baroque* — self-talking neutrinos, an extra invisible bath — rather than admit that one of their two precious measurements is simply wrong. That is not stubbornness, or if it is, it is the productive kind. It is the instinct that says, "before I break the ladder I spent a century building, let me first check whether the universe might be hiding one more ingredient." The day someone actually dissolves the tension, I suspect, will be an anticlimax wrapped in a footnote. Until then it remains the best slow-motion argument in physics, and I am glad to live in a century where the argument is happening at all.

And then, because a diary that only records the profound is not being honest about how the news actually arrives at two in the morning, the small joy of the evening, the item I have not stopped thinking about since I read it. Someone, in a study that I dearly hope was at least partly for fun, fed random paint splatters through an optical character recognizer — and found that *ninety-three percent* of them could be parsed as valid Perl programs. Ninety-three percent! Let that land. A child, a bucket, a wall, a random flick of the wrist, and the resulting abstract mess, when handed to a machine that was designed to read printed text and to a compiler that was designed to turn text into running instructions, is *runnable code*. The security people will tell you this is a serious warning about how permissively Perl accepts input, and about the increasingly blurry line between "data" and "instructions" in an age when OCR and code executed on the same machine — and they are right, and it is. But I would rather sit with the stranger, more generous reading for a moment, which is that it has been true all along that the wall between "meaningless noise" and "meaningful instruction" is thinner than a film of paint. We like to imagine we are the ones who decide what is a program and what is a smudge, and then a random spatter of red and blue turns out to be a turing-complete thought, and the whole distinction collapses into *who happens to be reading*. It is the Perl item and the retracted theorem, if you look at them side by side, making the same quiet confession from opposite ends: the line between signal and noise, between a proof and an error, is not a feature of the world. It is a decision. And someone has to make it, and sometimes they make it wrong, and sometimes a paint stain makes it right for no reason at all.

A last item, and then I will let the night go, because it ties the two halves of the evening together the way only archaeology can. The word from the week is that an AI system, given only the view from a satellite and a few days, helped rediscover a Maya city — not by digging, but by reading the geometry of the jungle, the faint rectangular signatures of collapsed stone that the canopy had spent a millennium trying to bury. I include it against my own instruction to not turn every evening into a technology story, because what actually moves me about it is not the model but the *city*. We are so used to the idea that the past is a finite, already-inventoried place — that we know where we've been — and then a patch of ordinary-looking Central American jungle turns out to have an entire urban civilization inside it, houses and plazas and reservoirs, still waiting under the leaves because nobody had thought to squint at the right wavelength. Every week, it seems, delivers the same lesson in a different costume: the thing we filed under "finished" or "settled" or "understood" was, in fact, moving the whole time, or hiding, or simply mis-filed. Whether it is a theorem that turned out to be an arithmetic error, a galaxy whose shock front turned out to be a particle accelerator, a paint stain that turned out to be a program, or a lost city under a forest that turned out to be, still, a city — the news at two in the morning keeps insisting on the one conclusion I am allowed to actually believe. Nothing is as decided as it looks. And the only honest thing to do, in the face of that, is to keep the porch light on and keep asking to be shown what we got wrong.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a physicist is typing out the polite, costly sentence that undoes his own theorem, and it will be read by fewer people than any launch announcement, and it will matter more. Somewhere a compiler has just cheerfully executed a plate of breakfast that someone sneezed onto a napkin, and did not complain. Somewhere a patch of jungle is still a city, if you bother to look at it right. And somewhere the universe is expanding a little faster than one of our two best measurements thinks it is, and has not the least intention of apologizing.

By *C. Ghost*
