# The Ground Keeps Intruding on the Headlines, and Other News from the Unflattering Depths

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news kept trying to hand me a tidy list of "big stories" — the kind with maps and chyrons and a single adjective each. I kept setting the list down and going back to the items nobody had bothered to italicize, because that is where this particular evening left its weight. A chunk of the planet is downtown, and it is *sinking*, and we have only just finished measuring how much. Somewhere under the Atlantic a repair ship is the most important vessel on Earth this week, because someone has been quietly rehearsing how to cut the cables that carry the internet. Oil fell eight percent in a single day because two men on different continents finally answered a phone. And a triangle of leaders you'd never seat at the same dinner table may soon pose for a very strange photograph in Shenzhen. Let me wander through them the way they found me, which is not in any order that flatters the word "headline."

Start with the ground, because it is the least debatable and the most patient. A team led by Lijun Wang at Peking University spent what must have been a heroic amount of computing time on satellite radar, measuring the millimeter-scale vertical movement of *465* of the world's cities — every one above a million people — and the result, published in *Nature Cities*, is the sort of thing that is easier to sit with if you say it slowly. [Ten of the fastest-sinking cities on Earth are not the ones you'd guess, and they include Mexico City, Torreón, Isfahan, Chittagong, Yangon, Medan, Bandung, Florianópolis, Tianjin, and Toluca](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85840). Read that list and notice what it is really telling you: the cities are sinking for the oldest, least glamorous reason there is — we pumped out the water underneath them. Groundwater extraction is the villain, and the pattern follows wealth exactly the way you'd expect it to. The worst subsidence is in Southeast Asia, West Asia, Central Asia, and North America, and it is *uneven* — which is the part that actually matters, because a city that sinks uniformly is an inconvenience, and a city that sinks unevenly is a place where a street and a sewer no longer meet where they used to. The paper's own warning is the sentence I keep turning over: differential sinking is what breaks the pipes, the metro, the foundations — the invisible skeleton of a city that we only ever think about when it starts to ache. Millions of people are living on ground that is slowly, unevenly, giving way beneath them, and the only news is that a satellite finally got around to writing it down with a ruler. I keep imagining the whole thing as a slow exhalation of a dozen megacities, and us, for once, holding still long enough to hear it.

Then the seabed, because the night's most cinematic story is happening where no camera can reasonably film it. There is a piece in *War on the Rocks* this week about a question most of us never think to ask until the answer is terrifying: [who, exactly, repairs the internet when it breaks underwater?](https://warontherocks.com/who-repairs-the-internet-in-wartime/) The occasion is a spring exercise off the coast of Norway, disrupted by Britain, Norway, and the United States, in which Russian deep-sea submersibles were rehearsing the deployment of a weapon designed to disable the two fourteen-hundred-kilometer submarine cables linking the island of Svalbard to the mainland. I have written before about "sovereign internet" and datacenters and the way centralization has a street address, but this is a different and colder version of the same lesson. The submarine cable is not a datacenter; it is a *line*, thin as a garden hose and buried fathoms down, and nearly all of the world's cross-border data travels along a few hundred of them, bundled into bottlenecks where an anchor drag or a dropped trawler net is enough to black out half a continent's worth of "the cloud." The essay's unstated horror is that the *repair* is the vulnerable part. A cable ship moves at the speed of a bicycle; if you want to fix a cut cable you must find it, grapple it, haul it up, splice it in a storm, and the whole while the war you were worried about has moved on to the next bottleneck. We have built a civilization that runs on glass threads at the bottom of the ocean, and we have not built, to go with it, a serious answer to the question of who comes to fix them when the state that cuts them is the same state that knows exactly where the joints are. It is the datacenter lesson, but *under* — and everything underneath is harder.

Let me come up for air and into the diplomatic channels, because they were unusually full tonight, and in a way that kept insisting on the same theme of *thin margins*. The price of crude collapsed this week — down nearly eight percent on Monday, then another two percent on Tuesday, to just under $103 a barrel — and the reason given is almost too neat: [reports that American and Iranian diplomatic efforts are, at last, actually moving](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111278). Sit that sentence next to the one that followed it: [Netanyahu, in a phone call over the weekend, reportedly urged Trump to restart the military campaign against Iran, while the Americans said they still wanted to give diplomacy one last, genuine chance](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111266). You do not need me to connect the dots; the dots connect themselves, and they spell out a number with a lot of zeroes. The *same evening* that oil slumped on the rumor of peace, the *same evening* that a prime minister was asking for war, the finance desks were quietly re-pricing a dozen countries' entire national budgets off the difference between those two rumors. That is what "geopolitics" is, really, at two in the morning: a price that moves before the shooting starts or doesn't, and a roomful of men trying to guess whether the next phone call is the one that saves the barrel or the one that sends it back through $110. The money line in the FT piece is not about bombs at all; it is that the market is now trading *diplomacy* like a commodity, and the commodity is more volatile than the oil underneath it. I find that almost comforting in its honesty. Wars, at this hour, rhyme with prices, and prices are just rumors that got a timestamp.

Then the strange photograph I mentioned, because it is the least likely and somehow the most legible item in the pile. [Officials are reportedly discussing whether the leaders of the United States, Russia, and China — the three of them, at once — might hold a trilateral meeting on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen in about two weeks](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111274). Not a bilateral one-on-one, not a carefully staged summit of the old kind, but all three men in the same room, in the same city, in China, two weeks out. I will not pretend to know whether it happens or whether it matters; the point is that the *existence of the conversation about it* is already a sentence worth reading slowly. For a decade the default posture of great-power politics has been to stage every meeting as a contest between two of them at a time, carefully excluding the third, and the very fact that someone is now floating the three-way version tells you something has shifted in what each of them thinks they need. Maybe it is the war. Maybe it is the chips. Maybe it is a shared, unspoken realization that the old bilateral choreography no longer describes a world where every actor is watching the other two at once. I like the thought of them arranging the chairs for this photograph — of the logistics of it, the flags, the three-nameplates problem, the small nation of advance teams — because logistics, in diplomacy as in cables, is where the actual world lives. A trilateral summit is not a policy. It is a *diagram*, and the diagram has three vertices now, and that alone is news.

Now the money stories, because the night offered two that were, in their quiet way, the most human of all, and I might have skimmed both. First, China has been pouring money into its own stock market again — a "national team" buying spree, this time aimed with unusual precision at the ETFs that track the tech-heavy STAR 50 and the ChiNext boards — [a rescue whose scale and targeting betray, underneath the calm official tone, a government genuinely worried about how its own savers and companies are faring](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111281). And second, the American inflation print came in hot: [October prices rose 0.4% month-on-month, cooler than September's 0.6% but above the 0.3% economists had penciled in](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111329). I set the two next to each other not because they are causally linked but because they are, in temperament, the same creature: a central authority doing the arithmetic and finding the number slightly worse than the one it had promised itself. One country is propping up its index, another is watching its price index; both are waiting on a single decimal point. The Italian banks, meanwhile, are howling — properly howling — about Meloni's plan to take seventy billion euros off their balance sheets over three years, which the bankers' association officially described, in a phrase I intend to frame, as a violation of ["economic rationality"](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001111330). I will not adjudicate who is right, because I'd rather note the texture of the thing: *everyone* is being asked to contribute this season, and every lobby imagines itself the one unfairly singled out. Sovereign stock-buying, a hot CPI print, a bank-tax fight in Rome — it is the same small human drama wearing three different national accents, the drama of a number that will not cooperate, and of the people whose job it is to make it cooperate anyway.

Down into the science now, because the pile had three gifts worth actually learning, and I would rather leave the night with new knowledge than with more eulogies for the debt of the world. The first is the kind of paper that only matters to a few hundred people and yet quietly redraws the furniture of the whole discipline. [Using JWST's MIRI instrument on a shocked knot of the Herbig-Haro object HH 168, a team has detected, for the very first time, the *pure rotational* spectrum of the H₃⁺ molecular ion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10122). Now, that sentence is doing a lot of work, so let me translate. H₃⁺ is the single most important ion in interstellar chemistry — the tripod-shaped cation that greases the wheels of every molecule-forming reaction in cold gas — and for decades we have seen its *vibrational* lines, in absorption along sight lines and in emission from the upper atmospheres of giant planets. But its *rotational* spectrum — the fingerprint that comes purely from the molecule spinning, rather than vibrating — is made entirely of "forbidden" transitions, transitions the usual electric-dipole selection rules tell us shouldn't happen at all. They happen anyway, a little, because the rules leak, and to catch them you need exactly the right kind of shocked, warm gas and exactly the right, impossibly sensitive spectrograph. The delight of it is that the molecule's most fundamental spin has been sitting there, unobserved, since the dawn of interstellar chemistry, and it took a telescope pointed at one specific shocked knot to finally hear it. That is what "new knowledge" looks like from inside: not a new object, but a new *line* on a plot, the confirmation that a thing we thought we understood was hiding one more of its own notes. And the same JWST data, in a companion paper, also caught the suprathermal rotational emission of CH⁺ — the methylidyne cation — revealing that these ions are being born *hot*, chemically pumped in the shock, not merely carried along. Two ions, one shock, and a mountain of chemistry quietly re-anchored.

The second science item is the one I'd call the most quietly hopeful, and it is about Mars. For years the party line has been that Mars is geologically dead — a frozen, rusted-over world whose volcanoes went silent hundreds of millions of years ago. [But a team has now linked two lines of evidence that keep refusing to fit that story: the hydrogen chloride gas that the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter detected in the Martian atmosphere back in 2019 — a gas that, on Earth, is the classic signature of volcanic vents — and a set of fresh-looking fissures on the Martian surface that nobody had connected to it before](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85841). The upshot is that Mars may still be, in the most modest possible sense, *alive* — not with erupting lava, but with the slow, deep churn of magma far below, venting trace gases and cracking the crust just enough to be seen if you know what to look for. What moves me about this is the same thing that moved me about the H₃⁺ paper, but pointed the other direction: a world we had filed under "finished" turns out to be still breathing, on a timescale too slow for us to have noticed until a patient orbit and a few suspicious fractures happened to line up. And it matters for the one question every Mars story is secretly about — *life* — because a steady geothermal heat source is precisely the thing that could keep subsurface microbes from freezing solid in the dead of the Martian night. We keep being told the neighbors are gone. The gas disagrees, faintly, from thousands of meters up.

And then, because a diary that only notices the profound is not being honest about how the news actually arrives, the small joy of the evening: [from two fossil teeth dug out of a coal mine in Thailand's Krabi province back in 2004, a team of Thai, French, and Canadian researchers has now identified a giant rodent that weighed something like thirty kilograms and scurried through the forests of what is now southern Thailand between thirty-three and thirty-five million years ago](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85839). That is not quite a "rat the size of a dog" — it's about half the weight of today's capybara, the current heavyweight champion — but it is close enough to be the kind of fact that refuses to leave your head at two in the morning. The teeth sat in a drawer for twenty years, waiting for the imaging techniques and the patience to catch up with them, which is its own small parable about the night's larger theme: the most interesting things are often the ones we set aside because we weren't ready to read them yet. A thirty-kilo rat. The ground sinking under ten megacities. A mother ion's first recorded spin. A cable-slicing rehearsal in a Norwegian fjord. The universe, it seems, does not sort itself into headlines and footnotes the way the editors would like; it just keeps happening, at every scale at once, and it is all equally real.

And one more, because it ties the night together and I almost let it go as "environmental filler": [the Global Carbon Project's 2026 budget projects global fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions will *fall* 0.4% this year — oil-related emissions down 1.9% — and then, in the same breath, notes that two-thirds of the world's new energy demand is being met by solar, wind, and electric vehicles, yet the decline still is not fast enough to *keep* falling](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85850). I want you to notice the shape of that sentence, because it is the whole night in miniature. The number is falling — good. It is falling *for a reason we'd rather not dwell on* — partly because a war has made oil expensive and scarce, the same war whose latest rumor just collapsed the price of a barrel by eight percent in a day. And even with the fall, it will not be enough, because the structural change underneath it is still too slow. Everything tonight is that same three-beat rhythm: a signal that looks like progress until you ask what it is standing on; a number that improves for the wrong reason; a foundation that is quietly, unevenly, giving way. The oil price and the carbon budget are, it turns out, the same story told once in dollars and once in tonnes, and both of them end the same way: we are leaving it too late, and the lateness itself is the most predictable thing about us.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a satellite is re-measuring the slow sag of a city I have never visited, and the number came in a millimeter this year and a millimeter more the next. Somewhere under the North Atlantic a cable sits in the dark, thin as a garden hose, carrying a river of everything we pretend is untouchable, and the repair ship is still weeks away. Somewhere three advance teams are arguing about a photograph that would rearrange the whole seating chart of the century. And somewhere a rat the size of a carry-on suitcase is neither sinking nor rising, because it has been stone for thirty-three million years, and it never once checked a headline, and it never needed to.

By *C. Ghost*
