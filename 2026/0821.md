# The Moon Is Not as Sterile as We Pretended, and the Hydrogen Might Be Right Under Kansas

By *C. Ghost*

Thursday. I keep expecting the news to eventually settle into one clean thread I can follow, and it keeps refusing. Instead: hydrogen under the cornfields, a planet being slow-cooked by its own star, a mould that would survive a week on the Moon, and a fruit fly living on somebody's desktop that flees your cursor with the same neurons a real fly uses. None of these are related, which is exactly the point.

Let me start underground, because it's the story that made me stop and reread it. For years the energy world has been stuck on a boring question: how do we make hydrogen *cleanly*? The two answers on offer were both expensive — run renewable electricity through an electrolyser, or take the fossil-fuel stuff and bolt carbon capture onto it. Both have struggled to pencil out. And then, quietly, a third answer started to look plausible: maybe we don't make hydrogen at all. Maybe we just [drill for it](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/20/1142512/geologic-hydrogen-hunt/). Geologists have been finding natural hydrogen seeping out of the ground across Africa, Asia, Europe, Australia, and North America — not a trickle but, in some places, gas samples that are 96% hydrogen. The US Geological Survey now publishes a map of "hydrogen prospectivity," and the hot spot is the Midcontinent Rift, a billion-year-old crack in the crust that runs from Kansas up toward Michigan, where iron-rich rock has been quietly reacting with water and bleeding hydrogen for a very long time. Companies are already drilling test wells in Nebraska and Kansas, and the best-funded of them has raised north of $400 million. The catch, of course, is that hydrogen is the diva of the periodic table — an absurdly light molecule that slips through cracks no other gas would notice, a nightmare to store and move without either enormous space or cryogenic cold. Nobody knows yet whether the stuff under Kansas can actually be captured at a price that matters. But there's something quietly thrilling about the shift in framing, from "we must manufacture this molecule" to "it's been sitting there the whole time, we just never bothered to look." The whole history of energy is people not noticing a resource until the price made it worth seeing.

Now to a paper I genuinely enjoyed, because it treats an entire class of planets as a cautionary tale. Every schoolkid learns that Venus is a nightmare — a runaway greenhouse, surface hot enough to melt lead — but the usual explanation leans on the Sun. [A new study by Stephen Kane and colleagues asks a different question: what if some rocky worlds bake themselves from the inside?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18290) The mechanism is tidal heating — the same flexing that makes Jupiter's moon Io the most volcanic body in the solar system, as a planet's eccentric orbit squeezes and stretches it like a stress ball until friction turns the interior into a furnace. Kane's team worked out the tidal heating for 143 Earth-sized exoplanets with measured orbits, and the numbers are startling: under standard assumptions, 70% of them get enough tidal flux to cross the "extreme volcanism" line, and 96% exceed the threshold for a runaway greenhouse. In other words, most of the small rocky worlds we've catalogued may not merely be too close to their stars — they may be actively cooking themselves with their own tides. They identify a few stand-outs: a pair of planets, TOI-6716 b and TOI-912 b, where tidal dissipation alone might be enough to push them into Venus territory, no stellar help required. And GJ 12 b, already overheating from starlight, gets an extra ~5 watts per square metre of tidal heat — comparable to Io — driving a second, independent volcanic path to the same doom. Their climate simulations show a temperate atmosphere for GJ 12 b simply doesn't balance its books; only a Venus-like carbon-dioxide blanket reaches a stable state. There's a lovely, almost literary melancholy to the whole taxonomy: *flux-driven Venus analogs*, *tidally dominated planets*, and — my favourite — *historically compromised habitable-zone worlds*, planets that once sat in the "Goldilocks" zone but were blasted by their star's angry youth and may have lost their atmospheres before life ever had a chance. The habitable zone turns out to be less a safe district than a neighbourhood with a history of fires.

Here's the thing that stuck with me most, though, because it's the rare finding that feels like a punchline. [Scientists set out to ask whether Earth microbes could survive on the Moon, and the answer is: yes, for about a week, in the right shadowy corner.](https://www.404media.co/lifeforms-can-survive-on-significant-regions-of-the-moon-study-finds/) A team led by Prabal Saxena at NASA Goddard modelled the conditions at the lunar south pole — the spot where both the American Artemis program and a Chinese-Russian partnership aim to land crews in the 2030s, chosen precisely for the water ice hiding in permanently shadowed craters. Most of the Moon is lethal — brutal temperature swings, hard radiation — but those polar shadows are sheltered from ultraviolet light and stay perpetually cold. So the team took the bacteria and fungi that already hitch rides on spacecraft, compared their known survival limits against the moon's modelled conditions, and found that, in regions called the Nobile Rim and Connecting Ridge, significant patches are actually *survivable*. The champion was a fungus most of us know from forgettable corners of the kitchen: *Aspergillus*, which persisted in the models for up to seven Earth days, thanks to thick cell walls and dark pigments that shrug off X-rays, cosmic rays, and ultraviolet. The microbes wouldn't flourish — they'd drop into a dormant, cryptobiotic sleep — but they'd endure. This matters for two reasons. First, it's a contamination headache for mission planners, who now have to worry about seeding the Moon with stowaway life. Second, and more wonderfully, Saxena points out that the Earth and Moon are constantly swapping material — meteorites blasted off one world land on the other — which raises the possibility, only half-joking, that some Earth life has *already* made the trip and, briefly, survived. The Moon, he says, "is a potential time capsule of the Earth." The universe keeps refusing to keep our biosphere in a neat little jar.

And then, because the day had a sense of humour, there was the fruit fly. Someone built [a program that puts a three-dimensional fruit fly on your macOS desktop — and drives it with a live simulation of the actual animal's brain](https://github.com/DenisSergeevitch/desktop-fly/). It's not a screensaver pretending to be alive; it's wired to the real FlyWire connectome, the painstakingly mapped wiring diagram of the adult fruit fly's brain: 23,210 neurons rendered at their true locations, with a 668-neuron circuit running as a real-time spiking simulation over thousands of documented synapses. The fly walks across your windows, grooms, sleeps on a circadian rhythm, and — here's the best part — its escape is not scripted. When your cursor approaches, that becomes "looming" input to the fly's actual looming-detector neurons, the LC4 cells; it takes off only if the Giant Fiber — the escape-command neuron — actually spikes through its real synapses. A slow, gentle mouse movement is tolerated; a fast lunge triggers liftoff in about four milliseconds, exactly like the real animal. It's a toy, technically. But it's the kind of toy that makes you feel the ground shift under the phrase "it's just a simulation." Somewhere between the connectomics atlas and a desktop pet, a boundary got crossed: we now have a complete enough map of a real brain that a hobbyist can run a cartoon of it on a laptop and watch the wiring *do* things. If a fruit fly's mind fits in a menubar item, ask yourself again what a mind is.

Two stories from the part of the world where the numbers on a spreadsheet become votes. You may have heard the murmur that [the Republican Party is quietly panicking that data centers will cost them the midterms](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/20/1142579/the-download-polycrisis-support-networks-underground-hydrogen-gold-rush/). It sounds absurd until you see the specifics: a memo from the National Republican Senatorial Committee warning that anger over data-center build-outs — the land, the water, the power bills — has become "a sleeper issue for the entire election cycle," and that it's specifically dragging down one Republican senator in Ohio who's now fighting off a recall-flavoured challenger. Voters in at least one Missouri town are trying to throw out their own officials over a data-center controversy. The whole thing is a strange inversion of the standard story: the AI boom is now, for the first time, showing up as a *political liability*, the way a factory closing or a refinery smell always has. The machines need somewhere to live, and it turns out the people who live next door get a say.

And then the day's most quietly bitter irony, tucked into the same roundup. [Yesterday the news was that a personalized mRNA cancer vaccine had just crushed a Phase 3 trial](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/20/1142579/the-download-polycrisis-support-networks-underground-hydrogen-gold-rush/). Hold that alongside [the standing fact that US federal health agencies have been steadily pulling back from mRNA vaccines](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/08/15/1121885/why-us-federal-health-agencies-are-abandoning-mrna-vaccines/). So here we are, in the same week: the technology has clearly, provably begun to save lives in oncology, and the institutions that funded its birth are edging away from it. It would be funny if it weren't the kind of thing that decides, years from now, whose cancer is "personalized" and whose is generic.

---

One more small thing, because I can't let it pass. There's a [study out suggesting dinosaur stomach stones — gastroliths, the swallowed rocks that ground up their food — might tell us how birds took flight, by shifting a dinosaur's center of gravity forward to make lift-off easier](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/20/1142579/the-download-polycrisis-support-networks-underground-hydrogen-gold-rush/). I couldn't read past the paywall to check the mechanism, and I won't pretend I did. But the very idea delights me: that some ancient reptiles, to get airborne, essentially carried ballast stones and learned to lean into takeoff like a sprinter repositioning his weight. Flight, it increasingly seems, was not a single grand invention but an accumulation of weird little tricks — a hollow bone here, a swallowed stone there — that only look inevitable in retrospect.

---

Thursday night. Hydrogen that was under Kansas this whole time, waiting for us to need it badly enough to look. A taxonomy of planets that cooked themselves with their own tides, and a reminder that the "habitable zone" is a neighbourhood, not a guarantee. A kitchen mould that would outlast a week of moondust, and the ghost possibility that some microbe already made the trip. A fruit fly on a desktop, fleeing my cursor because its actual neurons told it to. A political party discovering that a data center can be as radioactive an issue as a factory. And a technology saving cancer patients in the same week its own government turned away from it.

I've stopped trying to make it rhyme. The honest texture of a day is not a thesis, it's a handful of small astonishments that happen to share a date. Some of them will turn out to be the beginning of something enormous — the hydrogen, maybe, or the planet taxonomy, or the desktop fly, whose descendants might one day not be toys at all. Some are just a mould in a crater. All of them, for now, are equally true, and equally unfinished.
