# Twenty-Five Years, a Thousand Children, and the Port That Nobody Was Watching

By *C. Ghost*

Thursday. September 10, the day before the day before the day. I'm writing this in the small hours, which is when I do my most honest accounting, and the honest accounting is that today's news arranged itself into a single, uncomfortable argument about *what we count and what we don't*. The numbers are staggering, and the staggering ones are not, for the most part, the ones making the headlines.

Let me start with the number that has been arriving all week in a quiet drumbeat: twenty-five. Tomorrow is the 25th anniversary of September 11, and the retrospectives have been pouring in with the grim efficiency of a funeral that everyone saw coming. The figures, once you see them in one place, are the kind that make you put down whatever you were holding. [The Costs of War project at Brown University estimates that the post-9/11 wars have killed at least 4.7 million people and cost American taxpayers roughly $8 trillion](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/9/10/9_11_25th_anniversary_costs_of). That's not including the ongoing Iran campaign, which by some estimates is burning $2 billion a day. A quarter-century, eight trillion dollars, and a body count that would fill a medium-sized country — and the most honest thing I can say about all of it is that I have no idea what to do with numbers that large except stare at them and feel my sense of scale quietly malfunction. The human brain, it turns out, was not designed to process suffering in the millions. It was designed to process the death of one person you loved, and after that it starts rounding.

The White House released a proclamation calling the day Patriot Day — "a quarter of a century has passed," it reads, "and an entire generation of Americans has now come of age with no living memory of that fateful day." There's something about that sentence that sits wrong with me, and it's not the sentiment, which is perfectly appropriate. It's the quiet admission buried inside it: that the defining event of American foreign policy for the last quarter-century is now, officially, *history* — not memory, not experience, just something people born after 2001 read about in textbooks. The war that 9/11 launched has outlived the living memory of the attack itself. That's not a political observation; it's an almost mathematical one. If you were born the day the towers fell, you are now twenty-five years old, and you have never known an America that wasn't at war somewhere. The entire premise of the post-9/11 era was that it was *temporary* — an emergency, a response, a thing that would end. Instead it became the background radiation, the hum of the refrigerator, the thing everyone stopped noticing because it never stopped happening.

And then, right on top of that anniversary, the numbers that actually ran on the front page: [oil surged past $105 a barrel, stocks slid, and Treasury yields climbed to their highest level since 2007](https://www.wsj.com/livecoverage/stock-market-today-dow-sp-500-nasdaq-09-10-2026). The proximate cause was the usual one — the Iran war, the Strait of Hormuz, the same grim arithmetic I've been writing about all week — but the proximate cause isn't what interests me. What interests me is that the bond market is now doing something it hasn't done in nearly two decades, and the stock market is doing something it hasn't done in months, and the president went on television and said, with the kind of candor that is either refreshing or alarming depending on your mood, that [the war will "end immediately after the election" because Iran "can't hold out any longer"](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/9/10/headlines). Setting aside the question of whether you can schedule a war's conclusion around a midterm calendar — you can't, obviously, but the fact that he said it out loud tells you something about the room — the detail that actually caught my eye was buried in a CNBC analysis: [the president's personal investments in oil stocks have gained roughly $4.4 million since the war began](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/10/iran-us-oil-hormuz-supply-trump-military-brent-wti.html). Four-point-four million dollars. That's the kind of number that, in any other administration, would be the only number anyone talked about. In this one, it's a footnote. Meanwhile, [Brown University calculates the average American household has paid more than $780 in extra fuel costs since the war started](https://www.democracynow.org/2026/9/10/headlines). So the math, stripped to its barest form, is: the war costs the average family nearly eight hundred bucks, and the president's portfolio is up four million. I'm not editorializing; I'm just arranging the two numbers next to each other and letting them breathe.

And while the world was watching the oil price — which is, to be fair, the number everyone can feel at the pump — the Houthis were doing something that, in any other week, would have been the lead story everywhere. [They seized the port city of Mokha, on Yemen's Red Sea coast, routing Saudi-backed government forces in a lightning offensive and putting themselves within fifty miles of the Bab al-Mandab Strait](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/10/world/middleeast/yemens-houthis-seize-strategic-red-sea-port-officials-say.html). The Bab al-Mandab is the Hormuz of the south — roughly 12% of global maritime trade passes through it — and the Houthis, who are backed by Iran and have spent years demonstrating a flair for asymmetric naval warfare that is genuinely impressive if you can set aside the human cost, now have a port on it. The UN reports that nearly twenty thousand families have been displaced in less than four days. Twenty thousand families, in a country that was already one of the world's worst humanitarian crises before this offensive began. I don't have a clever line about this. The world has two critical oil chokepoints, and both of them are now, in different ways, under the shadow of the same war. Hormuz in the north, Bab al-Mandab in the south, and between them the entire Red Sea, which is rapidly becoming less a sea and more a corridor you cross while holding your breath. The Houthi advance, the Iranian tanker strikes, the US carrier group — it's all the same map, drawn at different scales, and the scale keeps getting smaller.

And then, because the world has a sense of dramatic timing that no novelist would dare attempt, Algeria chose today to [formally sever diplomatic relations with the United Arab Emirates, citing years of "provocative and hostile actions" and what Algiers has long viewed as Emirati interference in North African affairs](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/algeria-cuts-diplomatic-ties-with-uae-algerian-state-tv-says-2026-09-10/). The UAE responded with a statement saying it hoped the rupture would be "temporary" — which is diplomatic for *we didn't see this coming and we're not happy about it*. The backstory, for anyone who hasn't been following the slow-boil feud between Algiers and Abu Dhabi, is that the two countries have been on opposite sides of almost every regional question for years: the Abraham Accords (UAE signed, Algeria opposed), Morocco-Western Sahara (UAE backs Morocco, Algeria backs the Polisario), the broader Islamist-versus-secular divide in Arab politics. A diplomatic rupture between two major Arab states — one of them the richest per-capita country in the region, the other the largest by land area in Africa — is the kind of event that, in a normal year, would get a whole news cycle to itself. This year it got a paragraph, because there's a war in the Gulf and bond yields are at generational highs and everyone's attention budget is already spent.

Let me turn to the number that actually made me stop scrolling, because it's the one I can't get out of my head and it's the one that, in a just world, would have been the only thing anyone talked about today. [Bangladesh's measles outbreak has now killed more than a thousand children, with over 166,000 suspected cases since March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Bangladesh_measles_outbreak). The government has announced it will resume nationwide vaccinations on September 25, which is to say: the solution to the crisis has been available the whole time, and the crisis happened anyway, because the vaccination coverage had fallen far enough — thanks to pandemic disruptions, supply-chain failures, and the same global anti-vaccine sentiment that has been spreading through social media like a second, quieter epidemic — that measles found the gap and walked through it. A thousand dead children. From a disease we have had a safe, effective, cheap vaccine for since 1963. I want to be very careful not to write the obvious polemic here, because the obvious polemic writes itself and also because it's too easy. The harder thing to say is that this is what a *systems failure* looks like — not one bad decision, not one villain, but a dozen small, boring, bureaucratic failures stacked on top of each other until the pile reaches a thousand dead children and suddenly everyone notices. The vaccine existed. The logistics existed. The money, in absolute terms, was trivial — the entire global measles vaccination effort costs less than a single day of the Iran war. And yet. And yet. The number I keep coming back to is not 1,009. It's 1963. Sixty-three years. We have had the answer for sixty-three years, and we still found a way to not use it.

I should, in fairness, also mention the day's other numbers, because Thursday was generous with them and some of them are genuinely strange. [Switzerland published a 200-page dossier on Josef Mengele, the Nazi doctor who conducted medical experiments at Auschwitz](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2026/09/10/switzerland-germany-nazi-jewish-mengele-auschwitz/b2220c0a-ad1f-11f1-b498-8697f35a6743_story.html). Two hundred pages, eighty years after the fact, released by a country famous for its neutrality and its banking secrecy and its general reluctance to be the first to say anything about anything. There's something almost unbearably Swiss about the timing — the war ended in 1945, Mengele died in 1979, and Switzerland has been sitting on this dossier for decades, waiting for the exact right moment to release it, and the exact right moment turned out to be a random Thursday in September 2026 when everyone was busy watching the oil price. History, in the Swiss imagination, is not something you respond to; it's something you file correctly and release when the world has stopped paying attention. I'm not mocking this, exactly. There's a dignity to it — the idea that some truths don't have an expiration date, that eighty years is not too long to wait for a complete accounting. But there's also something darkly comic about the image of a Swiss archivist, sometime in 1985, putting a folder in a drawer marked "Mengele — release when geopolitically convenient," and then checking every few years and thinking *not yet, still too much going on.*

[Sri Lanka's Supreme Court, meanwhile, overturned a 2019 presidential pardon granted to a Buddhist monk convicted of intimidating a human rights activist, and ordered him back to prison to serve the remainder of his six-year sentence](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20260910-sri-lanka-court-revokes-monk-s-pardon-orders-return-to-prison). The monk in question, Galagoda Aththe Gnanasara, is a hardline nationalist figure whose organization has been accused of inciting violence against Sri Lanka's Muslim minority, and the original pardon was widely seen as a political favor from a president courting the Sinhala-Buddhist vote. The court's ruling is legally interesting — it's rare for a supreme court to undo a presidential pardon, rarer still in South Asia — but the thing that actually interests me is the quiet democratic muscle memory on display. A pardon granted in one political season, revoked in another, by a court that decided, essentially, that the rule of law outranks the rule of a president. In a week when Algeria was cutting diplomatic ties and the Houthis were seizing ports and the bond market was having a panic attack, a small island nation's supreme court quietly did the thing courts are supposed to do, and almost nobody noticed. That's probably a good sign, actually. The best institutional guardrails are the ones that work so smoothly you don't hear them click.

A few smaller things, because Thursday was a firehose and I'm trying to catch what I can before it washes past:

[At least twenty people were killed and five are missing after a fire broke out on a cargo ship in the port of Qingdao](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/10/china/cargo-ship-fire-china-port-intl). The details are still emerging, but the bare fact — twenty dead on a ship in one of the world's busiest ports — is a reminder that the global supply chain, for all its algorithmic optimization and just-in-time precision, still runs on human bodies in metal boxes, and those bodies are still vulnerable to fire in exactly the way bodies have always been. The container ship is the least glamorous piece of infrastructure in the world and also the most essential, and we pay attention to it only when it catches fire.

[Russian drone strikes killed at least seven people in Ukraine](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/10/russian-attacks-kill-7-in-ukraine-as-kyiv-targets-gas-plants-deep-in-arctic), in the grim, grinding rhythm that has become the background noise of that war. I've written less about Ukraine lately, not because it's less important but because the news from that front has settled into a pattern that resists narrative — the same cities, the same weapons, the same casualty counts that blur together into a single, awful average. The war in Ukraine is now in its fourth year, and the most honest thing I can say about its current phase is that it has achieved the worst kind of stability: the kind where nothing changes and everything hurts.

I'll end where I started, I think, because the number that won't leave me alone is the one from Bangladesh, and because it rhymes with the anniversary in a way I can't quite shake. A quarter-century since the towers fell, and the war that followed has cost eight trillion dollars and millions of lives, and the president is on television saying it'll be over after the midterms, as if wars observe electoral calendars. And meanwhile, a thousand children have died of measles in Bangladesh, from a disease we solved in 1963, because the vaccine didn't reach them in time. The symmetry is not political — it's almost mathematical, a brutal little equation about what we choose to spend money on and what we choose to let slide. The War on Terror, closing in on ten trillion dollars, and the measles vaccine, which costs about two dollars per child. Two dollars. I'm not drawing a conclusion here. I'm just writing the numbers down, on the day before the day before the anniversary, and letting them sit next to each other on the page. Some equations don't need a solution. They just need to be written down.
