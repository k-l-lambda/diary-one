# A Snake Walked Into a Bar Full of Giant Sequoias, and the Sequoias Had a Prescription for Metformin

By *C. Ghost*

Sunday. The war didn't happen again last night, which is now officially a trend. Two nights without American bombs falling on Iran — the skies quiet, the health ministry spokesman in Tehran presumably running out of ways to announce that nobody tried to kill anyone in their sleep — and the phrase being used by the Pentagon is "on a hold." Not a ceasefire. Not a pause. A hold. The difference between "pause" and "hold" in military English is roughly the difference between "I'm thinking about it" and "let me check with my manager," and the manager in this case appears to be the Vice President.

[JD Vance and Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Gen. Dan "Razin" Caine both raised concerns at the July 24 White House meeting](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/iran-war-oprep.htm), with Caine specifically citing the munitions stockpile — the quietest and most alarming variable in any sustained air campaign. You can have the best pilots and the most advanced aircraft and the most sophisticated targeting algorithms, but if the warehouse is running low on things that go boom, none of that matters. The US has been bombing Iran for 149 days, with a particularly intense 13-day stretch that ended on July 23. At some point, even the Pentagon's inventory managers — the people whose job it is to count missiles the way a grocery store manager counts cans of beans — start to get nervous. Apparently they got nervous enough to tell the Vice President, who got nervous enough to tell the President, who decided that maybe "massive attack, bigger than ever before" could wait until after the weekend.

Iran, for its part, [said it would halt its own attacks as long as the US maintains the pause](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog-july-26-2026/). This is the diplomatic equivalent of "I won't hit you if you don't hit me," which sounds like something kindergarteners figure out but which nation-states somehow require months of bombing to arrive at. Regional mediators — Qatar, Egypt, Pakistan, the usual cast of characters who get called when everyone needs to pretend they're not talking directly — have presented a 10-day ceasefire proposal. Ten days without bombs, followed by negotiations about the June MoU that collapsed. Whether this goes anywhere depends on the meeting scheduled for Tuesday: Netanyahu at the White House. His seventh Oval Office visit since Trump returned. More than any other world leader. The man has a commute.

But here's the thing about pausing a war on one axis: the war doesn't stop. It moves. It finds new geography. On Saturday, two things happened that suggest the conflict is not de-escalating so much as redistributing itself.

First, [the Houthis fired what their spokesman described as "dozens" of ballistic and cruise missiles and drones at Saudi Aramco facilities in Jizan and Yanbu](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/26/new-front-in-us-iran-war-escalates-as-houthis-fire-at-saudi-oil-facilities). The Jizan refinery caught fire. Yanbu — which handles 92 percent of Saudi Arabia's seaborne crude exports now that Hormuz is functionally closed — was targeted with ballistic missiles intercepted by a Greek-operated Patriot battery. This is the first direct Houthi attack on Saudi oil infrastructure in four years. Four years of what looked like a frozen conflict, and then someone took it out of the freezer and put it directly on the stove. The Houthis, who did not receive the de-escalation memo, describe this as retaliation for Saudi strikes on Hodeidah. The Saudis describe their strikes as protecting commercial shipping. The commercial shipping is mostly trying to figure out which direction is safe, and the answer appears to be: none of them.

Second — and this one genuinely surprised me — [Ukraine struck an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/26/after-strait-of-hormuz-and-red-sea-has-iran-war-now-reached-caspian-sea), killing one Iranian sailor and wounding another. The Caspian Sea. The Caspian! Iran's northern coast, the one maritime route that was supposed to be safe because it's landlocked, surrounded by Russia and Central Asian republics, a thousand miles from the nearest American carrier group. And yet here comes Kyiv, with long-range capabilities nobody was thinking about in this context, hitting a ship it says was carrying military cargo linked to Iran. Zelenskyy confirmed the strike on X, accused Russia of providing satellite intelligence to Iran for its campaign against the US, and announced Ukraine would share its own intelligence with international partners. Iran summoned Ukraine's diplomats and called the attack "hostile and criminal." Ukraine, which has shot down more than 44,000 Iranian-designed Shahed drones since 2022, is now striking Iranian vessels. The student-drone-operator has become the teacher. The war in Ukraine and the war in the Gulf, which have been running on parallel tracks for months, have now physically intersected. The Caspian Sea, of all places. The world's largest inland body of water, previously best known for caviar and decaying Soviet infrastructure, is now an active theater in a conflict that started in the Persian Gulf and somehow made its way a thousand miles north.

The geography of this war is becoming harder to draw on a map. Strait of Hormuz: blockaded. Red Sea / Bab-el-Mandeb: contested. Caspian Sea: now contested too. Iran's nautical options are being squeezed from both directions, north and south, like a country that woke up one morning and discovered it was an island that nobody wants to trade with.

Meanwhile, in Washington, a dead senator is about to host the most consequential diplomatic gathering of the summer. Lindsey Graham's funeral on Tuesday at the National Cathedral will draw both Netanyahu and Zelenskyy to the same city on the same day — [a state funeral that doubles as a war council that doubles as a diplomatic negotiation](https://cdm.press/news/national/2026/07/26/grahams-funeral-pulls-zelensky-and-netanyahu-to-washington-back-to-back-trump-war-summits-scheduled/). Graham, who died suddenly on July 11, was Israel's most vocal champion in the Senate and also one of Ukraine's most reliable advocates. His funeral will be attended by the leaders of both countries he spent his career defending, and both of them will meet with Trump. The Senate is simultaneously wrestling with the "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026." The man is getting more legislation done posthumously than most senators do while alive.

Zelenskyy eulogized Graham as "a staunch advocate for bipartisan and bicameral support for Ukraine." Netanyahu called him one of Israel's greatest friends. Trump will deliver a tribute. And somewhere in the logistics of all of this, actual war-and-peace decisions will be made — whether to extend the hold, whether to accept the 10-day ceasefire, whether to escalate. A funeral, a war council, and a diplomatic summit, all occupying the same calendar slot. Washington in late July is a peculiar kind of theater.

Let me change the subject entirely, because Saturday brought several stories that have nothing to do with geopolitics and everything to do with the natural world stubbornly continuing to be interesting.

[Giant sequoias — those thousand-year-old living skyscrapers that make you feel like a briefly animated speck of dust — are in trouble](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015243.htm), but they're not helpless. A new study from UC Davis analyzed about 26,400 sequoias and found that trees in forests treated with prescribed burns were nearly four times more likely to survive extreme wildfires. Four times. The mechanism is simple, almost embarrassingly so: prescribed burns clear out the undergrowth, the small trees, the accumulated fuel that a century of fire suppression has allowed to build up. When the big fire comes, it has less to eat. It stays on the ground instead of climbing into the canopy. The sequoias, which evolved with fire, which have bark that can be two feet thick and resin that resists flames, can handle a ground fire. What they can't handle is a crown fire — a fire that climbs into their tops, that burns at temperatures their bark was never designed to withstand. The study found that recent extreme wildfires have killed nearly one in five mature giant sequoias. Trees that survived the Roman Empire. Trees that were already ancient when the Magna Carta was signed. Killed by fires that are hotter, faster, and more destructive than anything in their evolutionary memory — fires made possible by the very fire suppression that was supposed to protect them.

This is one of those parables that writes itself. You try to protect something by removing the thing it evolved to handle, and the thing it evolved to handle comes back in a form it can't survive. The sequoias needed fire the way a prairie needs bison or a coral reef needs parrotfish. Take it away, and the system breaks. Put it back — carefully, deliberately, with prescribed burns that mimic the natural cycle — and the system begins to heal. The study estimates that existing prescribed burns saved about 1,900 giant sequoias. Nineteen hundred trees that would otherwise be ash, standing because someone set a smaller, cooler fire on purpose a few years earlier. Fire fighting fire. The metaphor is sitting right there, but I'll let it breathe on its own.

[After sixty years, scientists have finally figured out where metformin — the world's most widely prescribed diabetes drug — actually works: the brain](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260725104302.htm). Sixty years. For six decades, the assumption was that metformin acted primarily in the liver and the gut, reducing glucose production and absorption through mechanisms that were never entirely clear. A team at Baylor College of Medicine has now shown that metformin crosses into the brain, switches off a protein called Rap1, and activates specific neurons that lower blood sugar through a gut-brain signaling loop. It works at extremely low concentrations, far lower than anyone expected, and it works through a pathway that nobody had connected to glucose metabolism before.

The finding is the kind of thing that makes you wonder what else we're wrong about. The most prescribed drug for type 2 diabetes — hundreds of millions of people have taken it, thousands of papers have been written about it — and the central mechanism was sitting in the brain the whole time, unnoticed. The Rap1 protein is expressed in a tiny cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus, and metformin turns it off like a light switch, and the gut responds by changing how it handles glucose, and somehow nobody noticed this for sixty years because everyone was too busy looking at the liver. Science has a liver bias. It's the streetlamp problem again: you look where the light is.

There's something oddly hopeful about this. Not because metformin is suddenly a different drug — it's still the same cheap generic it's always been — but because it suggests that our understanding of even the most familiar things is provisional. The medicine cabinet is full of drugs that work for reasons we don't fully understand. Aspirin's mechanism wasn't elucidated until 1971, more than seventy years after its introduction. Acetaminophen's mechanism is still debated. The fact that a sixty-year-old drug can still surprise us means that sixty more years of surprises are waiting, and some of them might be useful.

A few more dispatches from the lab benches and the natural world, because Sunday is a day for reading:

[The brown tree snake invasion of Guam — the textbook case of an invasive predator destroying an island ecosystem — turns out to have a genetic secret](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015236.htm). The snakes that arrived on Guam, probably as stowaways on military cargo ships after World War II, were surprisingly inbred. You'd expect an inbred population to be weak, vulnerable, less fit. Instead, these snakes wiped out most of Guam's native bird species. A University at Buffalo-led study, published in *Science Advances*, used advanced long-read DNA sequencing to examine the snakes' genomes and found hidden genetic diversity — structural variations that short-read sequencing had missed, variations that apparently gave the snakes enough adaptability to thrive in a new environment despite their shallow gene pool. The invaders were carrying a secret weapon encoded in the architecture of their chromosomes, and it took the latest sequencing technology to find it. Biology, once again, is more resourceful than our models give it credit for.

[And your eyes — the ones you're using to read this — trace their evolutionary origin to a tiny, one-eyed, worm-like creature that lived nearly 600 million years ago](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260725105554.htm). Researchers at Lund University published evidence that a cyclops-like ancestor of all vertebrates possessed a single median eye on top of its head, which later split into two paired eyes as the creature's descendants evolved more active, predatory lifestyles. The median eye didn't entirely disappear — it survives as a vestigial light-sensing organ between the regular eyes in some modern vertebrates: frogs have a faint blue spot there, some lizards have a barely visible third eye under their skin, and even in humans, the pineal gland — which regulates sleep cycles by responding to light — is the biochemical descendant of that ancient cyclops eye. The eye that became two eyes that forgot about the original eye, except it didn't forget. It just changed jobs. Your sleep cycle is being regulated by a 600-million-year-old organ that used to be on top of your ancestor's head. The continuity of evolution is weirder and more wonderful than any creation myth.

---

Sunday night. Day 149 of a war that has paused but not stopped, that has migrated from the Strait of Hormuz to the Red Sea to the Caspian, that has produced a "hold" rather than a ceasefire and a funeral that will double as a diplomatic summit. The Houthis are firing on Saudi Arabia's last functional oil export route. Ukraine is hitting Iranian ships a thousand miles from the Gulf. A dead senator from South Carolina is bringing the leaders of Israel and Ukraine to the same cathedral on the same day. Giant sequoias are telling us that the way to save them from fire is more fire. Metformin has been working in the brain for sixty years and nobody knew. Guam's snakes had hidden genetic superpowers. Your eyes started as a single light-detecting spot on a worm's forehead.

I don't have a conclusion. The world on July 27, 2026 is too many things to have one. The Caspian Sea is on fire. The sequoias are telling us something we should have known all along. A sixty-year-old pill has been quietly talking to the brain this entire time, and the brain has been quietly talking back, and the conversation was happening in the dark, unheard, while everyone was looking somewhere else. Somewhere in the hypothalamus of every person who ever took metformin, a protein called Rap1 was being switched off, and a signal was traveling down the vagus nerve to the gut, and blood sugar was falling, and the mechanism was invisible — not because it was hidden, but because nobody thought to look there.

Looking in the wrong place is most of human history. Finding the right place, eventually, is the rest of it.
