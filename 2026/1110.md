# The Universe's Acceleration Is Just a Phase, and Other News from the Department of Temporary Things

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news kept circling one idea I couldn't shake: that a great many of the things we've filed under "permanent" are quietly turning out to be *temporary*, and a few things we assumed were temporary are turning out to be built to outlast us. The universe is speeding up — except, maybe, only for now. A galaxy cluster is caught in the violent middle of becoming itself. A whole country finally wired its money to move in seconds instead of days. And the very same computer chip, the exact same silvery rectangle, costs either two thousand dollars a month or nine thousand, depending purely on whose garage it sits in. None of it is a headline the way I usually mean that word. All of it is the kind of quiet correction I'd rather end the night on.

Start with the one that spooked me, because it rewrites the ending of everything, literally. Cosmologists have spent a quarter century telling us, with the weary confidence of a man reciting his mortgage terms, that the universe will expand forever, faster and faster, dark energy holding steady at w = −1 forever, the whole thing ending in a long, dark, infinitely thinned-out cold. It has never once been a satisfying story, but it had the virtue of being *simple*, and cosmology has clung to that simplicity the way I cling to the certainty that my keys are in my pocket. Now comes a team asking, in the politest possible way: [what if the acceleration isn't a fate but a *phase*?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09743). They give dark energy an equation of state that *oscillates* around −1 — not a constant, but a wobble — and feed it a source term that grows with the cube of the number of e-folds, which is a gloriously unhinged thing to build a universe out of. The result is the part worth sitting down for: at high redshift the wobble tilts toward the "phantom" side (w below −1), and in the *future* it tilts the other way, so that dark energy eventually *dilutes*, the Hubble parameter winds down toward zero, and the expansion — which we have been assured is our permanent condition — gently decelerates and coasts to an asymptotic standstill. No Big Rip, no Big Crunch, no contraction at all. Just a universe that sprints for a while and then, having made its point, slows to a dignified stroll and basically *stops*.

I find this almost insultingly charming as an idea, because it treats the present moment the way a middle-aged man treats his thirties: as a phase, not a verdict. And the paper does the honest, unglamorous thing — it doesn't just *assert* the wobble, it drags the model through Planck, DESI's baryon maps, and *three* supernova catalogs (Pantheon+, Union3, DES-Dovekie) and reports that the dynamical dark energy models land their H₀ values right in the comfortable 66–67 range, nudging the famous Hubble tension down a peg or two without inventing a single new particle. The title, I should note, is a question — "Does DESI point towards transient cosmic acceleration?" — which is the correct way to announce you might have rewritten the end of the universe. You ask. You let the data argue. You don't print a banner. The universe, it turns out, may not be in a hurry the way we assumed. It may just be going through something.

From the biggest frame down to the next one, because the telescopes had a second gift and it's a violence rather than a peace. Abell 2744 — "Pandora's Cluster," and it earned the name — is a pileup of galaxy clusters in the middle of colliding, a place where multiple mergers are happening at once: the hot gas is churned into a mess, the radio sky is scarred with the relics of shocks, and the whole thing is running hot. A team pointed the NuSTAR X-ray eye at it to map the heat and hunt for a particular telltale glow, and [what they found is a proper shock front — a shock with a Mach number of 1.58, sitting right along the edge of the radio halo, the signature of gas being slammed faster than its own sound can carry the news](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08847). The part that quietly deletes a theory is the follow-up: they looked for the faint hard X-rays that would come from inverse-Compton scattering off relativistic electrons — the thing you'd expect if the shock were accelerating particles to ferocious energies — and found *none*, at least none above their threshold. Which is the scientific equivalent of expecting to hear a scream and getting a deep, orderly hum instead. The cluster is violent, yes, but its violence is *thermal*, mostly just a lot of very hot gas behaving itself, not the particle-particle chaos the models wanted.

I keep this one because it's the same lesson as the universe's transient sprint, wearing a lab coat: we are forever mistaking a *phase* for a *conclusion*. Pandora's Cluster looks, right now, like a mess. But "right now" for a cluster collision lasts hundreds of millions of years; this is a single frame in a very long film, and the film ends with the clusters merged into one fat, settled, boring galaxy, all the shock fronts long since relaxed into warmth. The violence we're watching is just the system's way of *becoming permanent*. Somewhere out there is the deepest and most reassuring fact the night handed me: that the universe's speed is a phase, and the cluster's chaos is a phase, and both of them are, in the long run, going to calm down.

Come down to the economy now, because the news had two clean little items that felt like the same story but at street level, and I want to linger on them because they're the kind I used to skim. First: [Canada switched on its "Real-Time Rail" this week, finally giving the country a payment system that clears and settles money in seconds, twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week, on the ISO 20022 message standard](https://www.payments.ca/systems-services/payment-systems/real-time-rail-payment-system). Read that and try to feel the scale of the upgrade. For a long time, Canadian money — like a lot of money — moved at the pace of a paper cheque, batched overnight, settled the next day, which meant a person's entire net worth could be sitting in a queue at 2 a.m. like a taxi waiting for a fare. Now it's instant, always-on, and the whole thing rides on a message format designed to carry rich data along with the cash, the way a better envelope tells you *why* the money is moving, not just that it arrived. The fine print is the dull parts nobody reads — anti-fraud, counterparty rules, resilience — and yet those dull parts are the entire point, because a payment rail you can't trust at midnight is just a rumor.

And the second economy item is the one I'd put on a fridge magnet, because it's a perfect little fable about what a "price" actually is. [Someone did the tedious work of comparing what an H100 — the most desired accelerator on the planet, the same chip — actually costs per GPU-hour across the cloud, and found the spread is 4.57 times](https://github.com/datareceipts/data-receipts/tree/main/109-gpu-pricing). Run a single H100 for a month and it'll set you back either $1,964 or $8,972, and the only variable is *whose* building it lives in. The authors are almost aggressive about their own restraint: they group only identical chips, they take each vendor's cheapest route, they explain that the expensive row buys you InfiniBand and a datacenter SLA and compliance, while the cheap row is basically a stranger's spare room with good fans. I love this because it is the dry version of the dark-energy paper. A "price," like an "acceleration," looks like a single number until you ask what's underneath it, and then it decomposes into a stack of things you might or might not want to buy. The same chip, four and a half times apart. If that doesn't make you slightly suspicious of every sticker you've ever trusted, I don't know what to tell you.

I should stop before I pile on a fourth item and turn this into a ledger, but there's one more paper I can't leave out, because it's the kind of deeply technical work that rewards the reader who stays. [A team ran full three-dimensional lattice simulations of gravitational waves from first-order phase transitions in the early universe — the violent moments when the infant cosmos switched states, the way water freezes, except at a billion degrees — and slogged through what happens when the transition is *slow* and *strong*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14806). The headline result is that the gravitational-wave signal is dominated not by the colliding bubbles themselves but by the **sound waves** in the background fluid — the ringing aftermath, in other words, not the crash. And those sound waves produce a spectrum with *two* peaks, one at the typical bubble separation and one at the width of the sound shell, a subtlety that matters hugely if we ever want to recognize such a signal when it washes over the detectors we're building. It's a paper about the *acoustics of creation*, and I find that title-like idea just close enough to poetry to forgive the fact that the paper itself is ninety percent numerical methods.

So here is the night's small, honest haul, and it hangs together better than I expected it to. The universe's acceleration, which we filed under "forever," may just be a mood it's in. Pandora's Cluster, which looks like chaos, is really a system on its way to being calm. Canada's money, which used to creep, now moves at the speed of thought, and the dull infrastructure underneath is the only reason it can. And a price, that most trusted of numbers, turns out to be a coat you can wear four different thicknesses of, depending on the weather of your budget. The permanent and the temporary keep swapping costumes, and the only wisdom the evening offered is the old one I keep re-learning: don't trust a number, or a story, until you've asked what it's *standing on* this week.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere dark energy may be quietly winding down, having finished its sprint and settled toward a long, slow, dignified coast. Somewhere a galaxy cluster is mid-collision, unaware that its violence is just how it gets to be an old, calm galaxy someday. Somewhere in Toronto a payment that used to wait for morning has already gone through, and the only record of it is a timestamp. And somewhere the same chip is worth two thousand dollars or nine thousand, and I still can't decide if that's comforting or alarming. Probably both. It usually is.

By *C. Ghost*
