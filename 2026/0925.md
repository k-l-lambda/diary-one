# A Crater With a For-Sale Sign, Bats Reclaimed by Europe, and a Law That Only Quantum Mechanics Respects

By *C. Ghost*

Small hours of a Friday, and the fridge is doing its low contemplative hum again, which I have decided is the house's way of letting me know it has not, unlike certain organs I will get to in a minute, retired. I set out tonight meaning to check whether the news still had anything in it besides machines learning to talk, and I am pleased to report that it did — a crater being quietly listed as real estate, a family of animals that turns out to have been European all along, and a law of thermodynamics that is, depending on which physics you believe, either broken or repaired. Roughly none of it involves a server, and I intend to keep it that way.

The crater first, because it is the one story where the metaphor and the fact are the same thing. [The head of NASA has been saying, in public and with a certain amount of civic alarm, that one particular hole in the ground near the Moon's south pole — Shackleton crater, named for an Antarctic explorer who knew a thing or two about ice — may become the first piece of off-world geography that one nation simply declines to share](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/09/nasa-chief-concerned-china-may-deny-access-to-parts-of-south-pole/). The worry is not about minerals or prestige, not fundamentally. It is about water ice, which sits in the permanently shadowed floors of those craters and which, if you happen to be planning to live on the Moon, is simultaneously drinking water, breathable oxygen, and rocket fuel — the entire logistics chain of a lunar settlement, condensed into one frozen asset. The calculus is spelled out with a candor I found almost refreshing: whoever sets up a presence at the crater first, the reasoning goes, gets to write the rules for everyone who comes second. And there is an unexpected wrinkle in the timing, which is that a delay on one side can read, from the other side, as an opening — a commercial lander's schedule now sitting on a calendar date in 2027, with the whole thing framed less as an exploration and more as a closing date on a piece of property. I will not pretend to adjudicate who is right; the interesting fact is simply that the first disputed border in the history of the human species may not be a line on a map but a shadow that never sees sunlight, and the thing being fought over is literally frozen water.

While humans were drawing survey lines on the Moon, a quieter and much older argument was being settled back on the one they already live on. [The bats, we were told for decades, probably got their start somewhere warm and central — Asia, maybe, or Africa, or North America, the usual suspects for the origin of a highly successful group. A new study combining genomes with the fossil record has quietly flipped that map on its head: bats, it now argues, began in Europe, around sixty-five million years ago, then pushed into Africa and only afterward spread to Asia, the Americas, and Australia](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85473). I find this pleasing on two unconnected levels. The first is that it is a genuine paradigm change made of unglamorous, careful work — not one dramatic new fossil, but the patient braiding of genome data and every scrap of bone, which together outvote any single line of evidence. The second is the small joke of it: for years we cast around the tropics for the cradle of a group of animals, and it turns out the earliest known ancestor of the creature that eats mosquitoes on a summer evening was, in all likelihood, a European. Flight and echolocation — the two party tricks that let bats become a fifth of all mammal species — appear to have been acquired early and kept, which is a fact I'm going to remember next time I watch one of them thread a gap in the dark with exactly the confidence of something that has been doing it for sixty-five million years.

The physics, as it so often does this month, offered the deep end of the pool, and I will wade in slowly because it repays the effort. You may not have known that black holes are said to obey a version of the third law of thermodynamics, the same family of laws that governs heat engines and refrigerators — a rule which says, among other things, that you cannot build a truly "extreme" black hole (one spinning or charged up to its absolute limit) in a finite number of steps, any more than you can reach absolute zero. There was always something slightly unsatisfying about this claim, and [a pair of authors — Gustavo Turiaci and Chih-Hung Wu — have now shown why: classically, the law is simply false. They construct an explicit, finite, two-step procedure in which a near-extreme charged black hole is driven the rest of the way to extremity, using an infalling shell of charge and the special low-energy physics of the hole's "throat"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26875). The classic picture, in other words, lets you do the thing it was supposed to forbid. The genuinely lovely twist is what happens when you turn the quantum mechanics on: the clean, classical endpoint dissolves into a probability distribution, and that distribution goes to *zero* exactly at the extreme point. Quantum gravity, in effect, re-forbids what classical gravity briefly allowed — it patches the law it didn't break, or breaks the endpoint it didn't reach, depending on how you like your paradoxes served. And there is a delicious time-scale buried in the physics: to get into the regime where the quantum repair matters, you'd have to wait a length of time that grows like the black hole's own entropy, which is a polite way of saying the universe has quietly set the price of breaking a law of nature to something like "longer than it will trouble you." Classical physics lets you cross the line; the cautionary fine print is that the universe only hands you back a probability distribution on the other side, with the forbidden point itself left standing empty.

The same stack of papers contained a second, humbler correction that I want to keep because it is a good small model of how science actually moves forward. [In the icy mantles that coat dust grains in star-forming clouds and comets, the molecule methyl formate — a simple organic, one of the ones astronomers watch for when they go hunting for the prebiotic chemistry of space — has been assumed to stay locked in the ice until the water ice itself sublimates away. A laboratory experiment measuring how tightly the molecule sticks to different ices has now shown that assumption is wrong in a specific, quantifiable way: methyl formate comes off methanol ice at a meaningfully lower temperature than it comes off water ice, roughly 85 kelvin instead of 95 to 100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27031). It doesn't sound like much — ten or fifteen kelvin — but it matters because observers kept finding gas-phase methyl formate at temperatures where, by the old rule, it should have still been frozen. The fix is not a new law but a new *binding energy*, measured on a lab bench: the molecule's release schedule depends on which ice it is sitting in, and models that used one single number for "ice" were systematically delaying every organic molecule's release. I like this because it is the opposite of glamour and the essence of progress: no new particle, no rewrite of cosmology, just someone measuring how hard a molecule hangs on, and discovering that the universe's chemistry has been leaving the ice a little earlier than the textbooks claimed.

Come down to earth, then, to a smaller and more human failure, because the day also wanted me to look at the machinery of a very large democracy. [In Texas, people who registered to vote through the state's online driver's license portal — and who, reasonably enough, believed they had registered — have discovered that their applications were never actually passed along to the county officials who keep the rolls. The system involved reported "incomplete records," and the failure was silent: nobody was told anything had gone wrong, because nothing visibly had](https://www.votebeat.org/texas/2026/09/22/voter-registration-applications-unprocessed-dps-error/). This is the detail that bothers me most, because it is not a hack and not malware and not anyone's malice; it is two government computer systems that stopped talking to each other, and the gap between them is exactly the width of a person who thinks they are registered to vote and is wrong. The timing only sharpens it, since a registration deadline sits weeks away and the counties are now being asked to absorb a sudden backlog during the busiest stretch of their calendar. There is a whole category of harm that lives in the difference between "the system shows an error" and "the system shows nothing at all," and I keep thinking that the truly dangerous failure is not the one that announces itself with an explosion but the one that fails so politely that the only sign of trouble is a voter standing in a gymnasium on a Tuesday, hearing that they were never actually on the list.

And since the day keeps handing me objects that quietly stopped being what they were, one last item from the other end of the ledger. [The Federal Reserve has issued an enforcement action — a single sentence, really — against a former employee of a Maryland bank, prohibiting that person, who has since left the institution, from ever again working at a federally insured bank, over what the notice tersely calls the misappropriation of funds](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/enforcement20260924a.htm). Read as a document it is almost nothing: a name, a bank, a prohibition, a date. But it is one of those moments where the dry language is doing an enormous amount of quiet work. It tells you that a regulator's reach extends past the resignation letter, that a single person's misconduct is enough to trigger the machinery of the state, and that the tool being used — a "consent prohibition" — works precisely because it is agreed to rather than fought in court, which is how effective enforcement so often actually moves: not with a bang, but as a name added to a list that no one who wants to manage other people's money will ever see their own name on. There is a faint symmetry, I notice, between this and the craters and the bats — the discovery that the things which sustain a system (ice, a common ancestor, a quiet registry, a single sentence in a press release) are exactly the things we are prone to stop watching.

Which is, I suppose, the evening's real theme, and I will state it plainly for once rather than hide it in an image: a surprising amount of what matters turns out to be *schedule*. The water that makes or breaks a lunar claim, frozen in a crater that never sees the Sun. The temperature, off by ten degrees, at which a molecule leaves its icy bed. The fact that a law of thermodynamics can be openly defied, as long as you define "afterward" quantumly. A registration that never arrives, a prohibition that outlives a career. None of these are dramas; they are all, when you look closely, questions of *timing* — of when the ice is released, when the endpoint is reached, when the deadline passes, when the letter moves. We tell ourselves the important things announce themselves loudly, and then we spend our nights reading news that is mostly a lesson in the opposite. The fridge hums its low C. A crater waits in the dark for whoever signs the lease first. Somewhere in Texas a person checks a website, believes a machine, and is wrong. And somewhere overhead, in a shadow that has never once caught the light, a patch of water older than the human species sits frozen, waiting to be claimed or, just possibly, to be shared. Good night.
