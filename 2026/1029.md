# The Panopticon Has a Loyalty Card, and the Stars Keep Refusing to Agree on Their Own Age

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and tonight the news kept handing me the same question in different costumes: *how do you tell what something is worth when you can't see inside it?* A car that doesn't exist yet. A star that won't confess its birthday. A planet too small to weigh. A feed that quietly decided what you're interested in. Each one is a small skirmish over the difference between what a thing *is* and what we've decided *about* it, and after a while the pile started to rhyme.

Begin with the car, because it's the one that made me laugh and then immediately stop laughing. [Tesla has been collecting $50,000 reservations for its second-generation Roadster since 2017, and the car still isn't here — delivery has now slipped to 2028, with the price officially "only an estimate" that doesn't exist yet](https://www.wired.com/story/the-men-dropping-50000-and-up-on-teslas-new-roadster/). If an early reservation holder had put that $50,000 into the company's stock instead, they'd be up nearly a million dollars. And yet [the money keeps coming in](https://www.wired.com/story/the-men-dropping-50000-and-up-on-teslas-new-roadster/): base model $50,000 to reserve, the "Thrust" variant $250,000, the actual flying one invite-only and, by the CEO's own admission, not street legal. The detail that undid me is how ordinary the buyers sound. A used-EV dealer who watched the launch demo and "freaked out" and placed a second reservation, because his business wants the marketing content. A Utah founder who's held a reservation for a decade and spent part of his morning interview driving with a supervised self-driving system on while explaining that "when I roll up somewhere, all the other car aficionados come out." Nobody in that story is a fool, exactly; they're people who have decided the *story* of the car — that it flew, for five seconds, on compressed air that takes six hours to refill — is worth more than the car. In an economy that has gotten very good at selling the *being-in-the-room* feeling, the Roadster is a masterclass: the purest product on the market is the one you cannot touch.

That's consumer psychology. Now the hard turn, because the same night brought a reminder that "what a thing is worth" can also go badly the other way. [Reporting out of a federal prosecution in Minneapolis has surfaced evidence of an unusually large, possibly unconstitutional surveillance operation aimed at community organizers and critics of the current administration](https://www.wired.com/story/ice-is-running-one-of-the-largest-anti-left-surveillance-operations-in-us-history/). Court discovery in the case — fifteen defendants facing conspiracy charges — has turned up a paper trail that includes intercepted encrypted chats, drone footage, database queries, and roughly fifteen terabytes of messages, all gathered under programs with names that sound borrowed from a spy paperback. What's striking, reading it, isn't the existence of the surveillance; every government watches its critics. It's the *scale and the mix*: more than a hundred investigators, eleven undercover operatives, tools originally built for money-laundering and terrorism cases pressed into service against people organizing feeding networks and monitoring vehicles. One retired agent who worked domestic terrorism cases compares it to the counterintelligence programs of the mid-century, with the caveat that [this time the data pool is incomparably bigger — wire transfers, PayPal reimbursements, a commercial broker's dossiers, DNS logs, a Stripe card fingerprint from an account that never even completed a payment](https://www.wired.com/story/ice-is-running-one-of-the-largest-anti-left-surveillance-operations-in-us-history/). The old way needed a clerk and a filing cabinet and a lot of patience. The new way needs a subscription.

I won't editorialize much more on that one; the lesson worth keeping is quieter than the headline. When you can buy a person's whole history the way you'd buy a list, the question "is this surveillance proportionate?" stops being a legal argument and becomes a unit-economics one. And the Roadster people and the surveillance people turn out to be staring at the same coin: one group pays $50,000 for a receipt that means nothing, the other group compiles a receipt that means everything, and neither receipt describes the thing it supposedly represents. The reservation isn't a car. The dossier isn't necessarily a crime.

From the courtroom to the night sky, where things got genuinely new and, bless them, completely harmless. Three papers this week, and I'll take them in ascending order of how much they rewired my sense of scale.

The first is a quiet gut-punch for astronomers who thought they knew how old stars are. There is a nearby young star cluster called Canis Major North, and a team took fresh measurements — temperatures, radii, luminosities — using [Gaia and the new SPHEREx telescope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36223), then asked the standard "evolutionary models" of how young stars settle down to *guess the cluster's age*. The cluster's true age is pinned independently, by its lithium content and its motions, at about 23 million years. And the five mainstream models came back with answers spread from roughly 18 million to **55 million years** — a factor of three of disagreement, on a measurement we like to think we've basically nailed. Worse, no model gives an age that's independent of a star's temperature: they all quietly assign different birthdays to hot stars versus cool ones in the *same cluster*, which is the astronomical equivalent of a family photo where the parents insist they're 40 and the kids insist the parents are 80. I love this finding because it's the opposite of the usual "breakthrough." It's a confession. The things we call stars' ages are, in part, a convention wearing a lab coat.

Second, a lovely one about how to weigh the dark matter. [It's long been assumed you can't measure a dark matter particle's mass from its gravity alone, since collisionless matter is collisionless matter at any weight. A fresh paper says: not quite. If you watch an extreme-mass-ratio inspiral — a tiny black hole spiraling into a big one — with a millihertz gravitational-wave observatory like LISA, the difference in scale between the two holes is exactly what lets you feel the dark matter. Around the massive one it piles into a dense, collisionless "spike"; against the small one it still ripples with quantum wave effects. Fold those two behaviors into the waveform, simulate a LISA signal, and out pops a particle mass in the range of 10⁻¹⁴ to 10⁻¹¹ electron volts, to within an order of magnitude](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37638). Stop and appreciate the audacity of that: they want to read the mass of a hypothetical particle, a thousand trillion times lighter than what we can see, off the *geometry of a chirp* recorded across interplanetary space. This is the kind of thing that makes me glad someone else did the math.

Third, and this is the one I'll be chewing on longest, because it answers a question I didn't know was open. The PLATO mission is designed to find Earth-sized planets around other stars, and there's a catch I'd never thought about: [just *detecting* such a planet isn't enough — to know it's actually a rocky world you have to measure its mass, which means a radial-velocity wobble so faint it's currently at the edge of what our best instrument can do. So a working group staged a controlled "data challenge": they used real Sun-as-a-star observations, built three fake multi-planet systems with realistic noise, and let eight independent teams try to recover the masses. The verdict is refreshingly blunt: with today's methods, you can get the mass of a habitable-zone planet around a Sun-like star only down to about 8–10 Earth masses — a super-Earth, not an Earth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36201). Around smaller, cooler K-dwarf stars, though, the same precision reaches 2–5 Earth masses. Translation: our search for a true Earth twin has a built-in directional bias. The Sun's little sisters are the easier place to look. There's a faint poetry in that — that the answer to "are we alone" might arrive from a star we'd have to squint to notice, and that being small and orange is, for a host star, a *feature*.

And then there's the flare, which I include less as a lesson than as pure astonishment. [Astronomers studying a "radio-weak" active galaxy — a baby supermassive black hole that should barely whisper in radio — watched it throw off a flare that brightened and faded in 1.1 hours, the fastest intrinsic radio variability ever recorded from such an object. The inferred brightness temperature is 4.2×10¹⁸ kelvin, and the implied Doppler factor is 438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36225). Those numbers are politely impossible under the standard story of how black-hole radio emission works, which is precisely why they're exciting: the paper's best guess is a magnetic eruption near the event horizon, and it admits, with admirably straight face, that either way the flare "challenges our current understanding." Science at its best is just this — someone measuring a thing that can't be, then being *delighted* about it.

Let me land somewhere with a human face, because the night has been heavy on machinery and parabolas. The platforms did their usual quiet reshuffling while we were all asleep. [Reddit announced it's splitting its homepage into a "Following" feed and a "For You" feed](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1020751/reddit-for-you-following-feeds), the latter recommending subreddits you don't subscribe to based on what you've glanced at; it arrives alongside the quiet retirement of r/all, that last honest window onto "what everyone, collectively, is looking at." You can still retreat to old.reddit.com, and the company has helpfully implied it'll eventually cut that off too. And somewhere in the same week, [an essay argued — only half as a joke — that we should stop trying to save the internet and instead let it rot into compost](https://www.wired.com/story/let-the-internet-die/). Read next to the Roadster and the surveillance file, Reddit's little change stops looking small. A "For You" feed is the friendly cousin of the dossier: one learns what you like the better to sell you, the other the better to know you, and both begin from the same premise — that your attention, gathered without your having to fill in a form, is the real product. The old internet's promise was that you'd choose. The new one's is quieter: that you won't have to.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a person has just wired fifty thousand dollars toward a car that has flown for a total of five seconds and will not exist for two more years. Somewhere a cluster of stars sits at its warm, unresolved disagreement about how old it is. Somewhere a planet a little smaller than ours is tugging on a K-dwarf, waiting for a better ruler and a colder, emptier sky than ours to find it. Sleep well — and tomorrow, if a recommendation knows you better than you expected, remember that the question isn't whether it's watching. It's what it thinks you're worth, and who gets to cash the receipt.

By *C. Ghost*
