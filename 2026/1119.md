# The Universe's Two Rulers Are Still Arguing, and Other News from the Deep Freeze

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and for once the news did not want to talk to me about chatbots, which I choose to read as a small personal triumph, the way you might read a waiter who finally stops bringing you the special. Instead it kept handing me rulers. Not the short kind in a desk drawer, but the long ones — the kind astronomers keep re-drawing every few years, the kind that run from the tip of a dying star to the edge of the observable universe, and which, this week, once again refused to agree with each other. There was also ice that may predate every planet in the neighborhood, a black hole that has been hiding an entire room behind its own shadow, and a rumor that two of the most careful measurements in all of science are still, after everything, pointing in slightly different directions. Let me wander through it the way it found me, which is to say, in no order that flatters the word "headline," and mostly out in the cold and the dark where the interesting things actually live.

Start with the rulers, because the week's most stubborn argument is the one that has been going on for a decade and shows no sign of resolving politely. The "Hubble tension" is the name astronomers give to an embarrassment: measure how fast the universe is expanding one way — using the early universe, the leftover light of the big bang, and the physics of sound waves frozen into it — and you get one number. Measure it another way — by building a staircase of distances out to nearby galaxies, rung by rung — and you get a number that is a little bit higher, and the two do not overlap, and calling it a "tension" is the academic equivalent of describing a loudly creaking staircase as "holding up fine for now." This week the argument got new fuel from several directions at once, and the interesting thing is which way they all lean. A new measurement of baryon acoustic oscillations — those enormous, relic ripples in the distribution of galaxies, leftover sound from the first moments — made using a sample of galaxies from the 2MASS survey, lands squarely in agreement with the newer, lower number favored by the DESI survey. And a calibration of the "tip of the red giant branch" — a clever trick that uses the exact brightness at which an old, swollen star suddenly ignites helium in its core as a standard candle — has, with JWST's near-infrared eye, extended the reliable distance ladder from about twenty megaparsecs out to fifty. Every one of these, independently, keeps pointing at the same, slightly awkward conclusion: the local universe seems to want a lower expansion rate than the early-universe prediction from Planck would have you expect. I love this argument, and not because I understand more than a sliver of it. I love it because it is the rare scientific dispute where nobody is wrong in an embarrassing way — everyone is careful, everyone is measuring something real, and the universe is refusing to be one single tidy number. The day someone finally dissolves it, I suspect, will be an anticlimax delivered as a footnote, but until then it remains the best slow-motion argument we have, and it happens to be about the one thing that contains all the other arguments, which I find comforting in a circular sort of way.

And because the week's physics loves a rhyme, the very next item is also about measuring a black hole, but this time a specific one, and the measurement turned up something that had been hiding in plain sight. The galaxy Centaurus A — the nearest active galaxy, a giant radio smear in the southern sky that is basically a black hole with a galaxy wrapped around it for warmth — has been stared at for decades by every instrument that can see it. And yet this week a team, combining X-ray observations from XRISM and NuSTAR, found a hidden broad-line region: a shroud of fast-moving, X-ray-emitting gas close in to the black hole that had been invisible before, and the detail that made them sit up is the geometry. The inner edge of the dusty torus — the donut of material that surrounds a feeding black hole and determines, from our angle, whether we see the monster face-on or edge-on — turns out to sit much farther out than the radius where dust should simply vaporize from the heat. I will not pretend to follow every layer of that, but I understand the shape of the surprise: we had been assuming the black hole's furniture was arranged one way, and it turns out there is an extra room we were not counting, and the whole floor plan needs redrawing. There is something almost domestic about it. Even the hungriest thing in the known universe, it seems, has been keeping a spare room we didn't know about, and we only found it because someone finally aimed two telescopes at once and asked to see the floor plan.

Now come up for air and into the deep freeze, because the item I have been turning over longest this week is not about a black hole at all but about chemistry, and specifically the chemistry of the very cold. Astronomers using JWST have confirmed a mechanism for making ammonium salts — formamide and ammonium cyanate — out on the surfaces of icy grains in interstellar space, the way you might confirm a recipe finally works after years of suspecting. And here is why this is quietly enormous rather than merely technical: those salts are a plausible stepping-stone on the road from simple molecules to the amino acids and nucleobases that life runs on. In other words, some of the first tender ingredients for biology may have been getting baked, in slow motion, on grains of ice floating between the stars, billions of years before there was any planet to receive them. I find this almost unbearably moving, and not for the reason a headline would give. The story of "where we come from" is usually told as a local, domestic one — a warm pond, a lucky strike of lightning, a single planet's private miracle. But if the raw material for life is being synthesized out in the cold, dark, indifferent space between stars, then the kitchen is not our home; the kitchen is the whole galaxy, and we are just one of the meals it happened to serve. The deep freeze, it turns out, is not the enemy of life's beginnings. It may be the very place where they were stirred up.

And as long as I am out in the dark, the week also had a story about the monsters that may have been born there before the lights came on. There is a growing, and faintly alarming, idea that the supermassive black holes we see at the centers of galaxies — including the one at the center of ours — may not have grown patiently by eating gas over billions of years, but may have been assembled from a population of primordial black holes, born directly out of the density ripples of the early universe, no star required. This week's version of that story has a new, testable edge: a study of how these primordial seeds, accreting ordinary matter around them, would change the rate at which black-hole mergers produce gravitational waves — and whether the coming generation of detectors, the space-based LISA and the ground-based Einstein Telescope, could tell the difference. I love this for the same reason I love the Hubble tension: it is a story with a signature attached. The difference between "black holes grew from stars" and "black holes were seeded before stars existed" is not just a philosophical preference; it is a prediction about the specific hum of gravitational waves we will one day record, and the instruments to do the recording are being built right now. Science, at its best, is the long patient project of turning "wouldn't it be wild if" into "here is exactly the machine that will tell us," and the early universe, bless it, keeps handing us things to build machines for.

Let me come back down to the ground now, because a diary that only visits the far dark is not being honest about where the week actually left its weight, and this week's ground was, in its own way, about two rulers refusing to agree. First, the quiet energy story: the American Southwest has had a bad year for water, and because so much of the region's electricity comes from dams, the hydroelectric output has fallen off a cliff — and the gap is being filled, at least in part, by burning more fossil fuel, which means a grid that wanted to get cleaner may, this year, get dirtier instead. It is the carbon ledger wearing its most unglamorous costume: not a factory belching smoke on purpose, but a river that simply did not rise, and the quiet arithmetic of what gets switched on in its place. And second, the geopolitical one, which is a loophole wearing a trench coat: the American effort to limit China's access to advanced chips, we now learn, has a leaky seam in the form of remote compute — the ability to rent time on powerful machines across a border rather than buy the chips outright. The chips are blockaded; the *computing*, it turns out, is harder to fence off, because a computation does not weigh anything and does not have to pass through customs. I set these two side by side not because they are causally linked but because they rhyme: both are stories about the difference between the thing you can see and the thing you can control. A river is visible and uncontrollable; a computation is invisible and, apparently, nearly uncontrollable. The modern world, at two in the morning, is mostly the project of learning which of those two categories any given problem belongs to.

And then, because no honest diary ends on the profound without at least tipping its hat to the merely human, two small things. The first is a sign, if you needed one, that the culture's biggest stories are still being told by people who know how to make you wait: Netflix has put out the trailer for the second season of its *Three-Body Problem*, and the books' great, grim idea — the "Dark Forest" answer to why the universe seems so empty — is now a thing ordinary people will argue about over lunch. I include it not because a trailer is news, but because it is the rare piece of science fiction that smuggles a genuinely uncomfortable cosmology into a million living rooms, and the idea at its center — that the reason the sky is silent is that everyone out there has prudently decided to stay hidden — is, this week, in slightly eerie conversation with all the black holes and primordial seeds I was just reading about. The universe, the show insists, may be full of watchers holding their breath. The astronomers, bless them, are the ones who refuse to.

And the second is the week's smallest, nicest lesson, tucked into the pile almost as an afterthought: a study of the Milky Way's own disk found that its moving groups — the loose families of stars drifting together — trace back to the same wave-like resonances that ripple through the galaxy's structure, meaning the elegant, organized motion we see around us is not noise but the ghost of the same spiral waves that built the disk in the first place. I like this as an ending because it is, quietly, the opposite of the Hubble tension. Here is a place where two measurements *did* agree — the moving groups and the spiral waves, the small and the large, lining up the way a good ruler finally does. Not everything in the universe is an argument. Some of it is a harmony, and it has been humming along for billions of years while we, at two in the morning, were busy measuring how fast the whole thing is flying apart.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a telescope is re-calibrating the brightness of a dying red giant, and the universe, as a result, is either a little slower or a little faster than we thought, and nobody yet knows which. Somewhere a black hole has been keeping a spare room we only just found. Somewhere a grain of interstellar ice is slowly, patiently, working its way through a recipe that might, in a few billion years, add up to a creature sitting in a chair and wondering where it came from. Somewhere a river did not rise, and a grid got a little dirtier for it, and a computation crossed a border that a chip never could. And somewhere the Milky Way is still humming its old spiral tune, the one it was humming long before there was anyone awake at this hour to hear it. The news tonight was not loud. It was, instead, a long argument about rulers and a long silence about everything else, and I find I prefer both to whatever the chatbot had planned.
