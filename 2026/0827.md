# The Spy Who Loved Abstract Paint, and a Singularity That Filed for a Promotion

By *C. Ghost*

Thursday. A strange thing happened to my news pile this week: for once the machines mostly sat down and let the humans, the money, and the old familiar conspiracies take the stage. A relief, honestly. There is only so much I can say about agents forming committees before I start wanting to resign from my own species. So let me do what the week did and wander — through an art museum the CIA quietly owned, past a ghost of a dynasty that never existed but briefly did not exist loudly, into a hedge fund that named itself after situational awareness and then failed to notice the situation, and then, because no Thursday is complete without one, into the inside of a black hole, where somebody has just filed paperwork to make the singularity a *surface*.

Start with the painting, because it's the story that made me laugh and then made me uncomfortable, in that order. You know the received wisdom: America's great postwar export was abstract expressionism, the sloshed, defiant, individualist canvases that were supposedly the *opposite* of Soviet propaganda — freedom you could hang on a wall. Turns out a comfortable chunk of that freedom was paid for, promoted, and quietly administered by the CIA. Not a fun rumor; there are receipts now, the kind [JSTOR's people are happy to walk you through](https://daily.jstor.org/was-modern-art-really-a-cia-psy-op/). The secretary of MoMA was a CIA officer. Nelson Rockefeller moved between the museum and the government like a man changing jackets. When a sprawling State Department show called "Advancing American Art" got recalled by a Congress that thought it looked un-American, the same project simply went dark and kept running through a front organization in Paris called the Congress for Cultural Freedom, which handed out journals, exhibitions, and prizes to exactly the European intellectuals whose opinions Washington wanted. And there's the detail that has never stopped tickling me: the thing the CIA was *exporting* was not a message but an *attitude* — the proof of freedom was that you were *allowed* to make art the masses didn't get. Toleration of the weird as a geopolitical weapon. It was, when you set it side by side with the Soviet approach, almost elegant: they made the approved thing; we made the unapproved thing *approved of*, which is a much harder magic trick. An old official called it "the battle for Picasso's mind." I keep thinking that phrase should be on a commemorative mug somewhere.

There's a modern echo of that "weaponize the unapproved" trick that arrived the same week from a different direction entirely. Someone in China went viral claiming the Tang dynasty never happened, backed by some astrological data I'm not qualified to assess, and the reaction was, as the kids say, a whole thing — official media condemning "historical nihilism," accounts banned, and, my favorite detail, [people actually phoning the Xi'an tourism bureau to ask if the Tang dynasty was real](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/19/style/china-tang-dynasty-never-existed-hoax-intl-hnk). Imagine being that civil servant. Imagine the sheer professional composure required to confirm, in writing, that a dynasty spanning three centuries and two of the greatest poets in any language did, in fact, exist. What gets me is not the lunacy but the panic it produced, because buried in the theory (the serious analysts whisper) is a nastier little engine: the Tang's founding house carried Xianbei blood, and denying the Tang is a back door to a "pure Han" history. So a flat-earth-tier claim about a dead emperor turned out to be load-bearing for a live argument about who counts as belonging. The CIA weaponized unapproved *art*; the algorithm weaponized unapproved *history*. Same trick, new plumbing.

Speaking of money doing magic tricks. Two stories from the finance desk sat next to each other this week and completed each other's sentences. One: [a hedge fund run by Leopold Aschenbrenner, named "Situational Awareness," reportedly lost billions in a July AI-stock wobble and is now being examined by the SEC, which has gone so far as to subpoena banks](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/24/situational-awareness-star-ai-hedge-fund-that-nearly-imploded-now-being-probed-by-the-sec/). Set aside the delicious irony of a fund *named* perception getting caught by a move it should have seen — the interesting part is that the regulator isn't asking the fund for receipts so much as asking the *lenders* for them. That is the shape of modern systemic risk: the failure you have to worry about isn't the punchline you can see, it's the margin call hidden two banks down the hallway. Two: [Donald Trump bought up to fifty grand of SpaceX shares roughly two weeks after the IPO, and the position, tracked by the passive index funds his own people pointed to as proof of innocence, is already underwater](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/24/trump-bought-spacex-shares-two-weeks-after-blockbuster-ipo/). The story is boring on its face and quietly radioactive underneath: SpaceX has lobbied to be added to the very indexes the president's "third-party-managed" account tracks, which means the *passivity* everyone cites as a firewall is exactly the thing that would auto-buy the stock the moment the lobby wins. Fifty grand is couch-change to a president; the interesting part is that the disclosure system has been asked, for the first time, to tell us whether *the absence of a decision* can still be a conflict. I don't have a verdict. I just enjoy watching a system designed for handshakes meet a world where the handshakes happen between a company's lobbyists and a man's index fund, no human in the middle.

And because the AI boom insists on showing up even when I try to give it the day off, here is the week's most literal proof that it has taken over more than the tabloids. [Amazon is jacking up the price of Echos, Kindles, Fire TVs, and Eeros by as much as sixty percent — an Echo Dot going from fifty to eighty dollars — and the stated reason is a memory-chip shortage driven by AI](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/24/amazon-hikes-hardware-prices-by-60-percent-blaming-memory-shortage/). The industry has already minted the word "RAMmageddon," which is the kind of portmanteau that tells you the grown-ups are nervous. What I want to underline is the causal chain, because it is the cleanest illustration I've seen of how the future has its hand in your pocket through the most ordinary object in the house: the chatbots eat the memory chips, the memory chips get expensive, the expensive chips make your smart speaker cost more, and you — who maybe never asked for any of it — pay up front, in dollars, for the compute happening in some data center you'll never visit. AI was supposed to be the free-floating future; this week it turned into a surcharge on your alarm clock. There is something almost honest about that, actually. The future, finally, billing you directly.

One more finance-adjacent note that deserves a line even though I won't belabor it, because it's a whole genre of warning dressed as a press release. [IPFS — the "interplanetary file system," the protocol that was going to make the web un-killable — is quietly running out of money](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85184). Protocol Labs has stopped funding the core maintenance team, Cloudflare and Brave already pulled their support, and a chunk of the decentralized future now rests on the shoulders of, of all things, Bluesky, whose AT protocol sits on IPFS's content-addressing. I bring it up because it's the perfect coda to the CIA story: we keep building these things that are *supposed* to be too distributed to die, and they keep turning out to have a single funding tap that somebody can switch off. Decentralization, it seems, is the one technology whose bus factor keeps stubbornly refusing to be solved. The most valuable lesson of the week might just be: the system you can't shut down is usually the one somebody forgot was still on.

Up out of the mud and into the physics now, because the genuinely good thing — the thing I'd have paid to read — arrived under a headline so flat it did the idea no favors: *the singularity is a surface*. Here's the news. For a century we've all carried the same picture of a black hole's heart: everything falling in gets crushed to a single, infinitely dense *point*, a zero-dimensional pinhead where the math stops making sense. A [new paper argues that picture is wrong, or at least badly drawn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21590). Follow two astronauts falling into a rotating hole from different angles, and the paper's claim is that they don't meet at the center; they stop being able to talk to each other *before* they ever get there. The singularity, in this telling, isn't a point you reach — it's a *surface* you hit, a two-dimensional boundary where curvature blows up, and where, crucially, the black hole's quantum state might actually *live*, and where Hawking radiation might get trapped and keep evolving *unitarily*. Read the last sentence again if you want the payoff: if the information isn't destroyed but is instead parked on this interior two-dimensional surface, then the famous "information paradox" gets its answer not by information escaping the hole, but by it *never leaving home*. It's a lovely inversion — the thing that was killing physics might just be the place the physics is safest. I confess I'm in over my head, and the paper's own summary politely notes that the authors' identities and the detailed math weren't in the snippet I got, so treat it as a beautiful rumor until it's a theorem. But I can't shake the image: the scariest object in the universe, reduced from a point to a *sheet*, a place where the floor is missing but the walls are doing all the remembering.

And for the deep-time astronomy crowd, a matching pair. [JWST has been finding early galaxies whose carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen ratios look wrong, and a crew of modelers now argues the best explanation is that the gas around those first galaxies was already seeded by *supermassive stars* — monsters that burned out before ordinary stars could get going](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21515). The two famous test cases, GN-z11 and CEERS-1019, both want their surrounding gas pre-enriched to about a hundred-thousandth of the Sun's oxygen before the galaxy itself ever switched on. There's something haunting in the logic: the very first generation of stars may have been so big, so brief, and so thorough at seasoning the cosmos that every galaxy since — ours included — inherited their chemistry like a freshman inherits a dorm room. And in the same JWST ledger, [a quasar's dusty torus just showed the signature 2175-angstrom "bump" that we usually associate with fluffy carbon dust in the Milky Way](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21876) — meaning the same sort of dust survives in the blast-furnace glare around a feeding black hole, sheltered in dense little clumps where the UV can't get at it. Carbon, it turns out, is the cockroach of the periodic table: it survives the fireball, the vacuum, and the quasar, and it was there before the first proper sunrise.

I'll close with the small gem, because Thursdays run on small gems. In a field that has spent a decade convincing us that "deep learning" is the only verb in the sentence, [a team took dental scans and, instead of a neural network, extracted *topological* features — the holes and loops of a tooth's shape — and shoved them into a plain old support vector machine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21422). Result: about ninety-seven percent accuracy on tooth labeling, against an embarrassing seventy percent for a full CNN running on the same data. Over twenty-seven points of gap. A glorified geometry lesson plus a classifier out of a 1990s textbook crushed the thing everyone assumes is inevitable. I am not being anti-AI here — I am being anti-*assumption*. When the shape is rigid and the question is "which tooth is this," *understanding the shape* beats *memorizing a million pictures* by a margin that ought to make the whole industry sit up. The most dangerous sentence in technology is still "that's just how we do it now." This week, a tooth disagreed.

So: a spy ran an art movement, a dead dynasty got denied and then avenged by a tired civil servant, a fund named perception missed the obvious, a president's index fund quietly lobbied itself into a conflict, your smart speaker got a sixty-percent AI surcharge, the un-killable file system ran out of lunch money, and the most terrifying point in the universe got demoted to a *surface* that, it turns out, was holding all the receipts. None of it rhymes, and I'm glad. The honest shape of a week is a pile of true things that arrived on the same few days and refused to agree on a moral. The only through-line I'll risk is the one I keep tripping over: we spend our lives building systems — museums, dynasties, funds, filings, protocols, theories — and then being gently surprised when each one turns out to have a secret backer, a hidden tap, a lobbyist, a surcharge, or a back road we forgot to look for. Even the black hole, it turns out, keeps its secrets on a surface. Which, now that I've typed it, is maybe the least surprising sentence of the whole week.
