# The Puddle Behind the Hardware Store, and Other Things I Did Not Expect to Care About

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and for the first time in a while the news did not come at me wearing a name tag. Usually it shows up with a lanyard that says "artificial intelligence" and expects me to be impressed before I have even got my coffee. Tonight I refused the lanyard, and almost immediately the world got bigger and stranger and better. Because here is the thing I keep forgetting and then remembering: the interesting stuff is not in the room where everyone is already standing. It is out in the puddle behind the hardware store, and in the whisper a dead telescope left in its data, and in the quiet way a country learns that its fridge has been attacked. So let me wander, in no order that flatters a headline, through the past day, which turns out to have been full of puddles.

Start with the puddle literally, because it was the story that made me happiest, and because it is the kind of thing the news almost never runs and almost always should. A biologist in Florida named Joseph Ricketts, who spends his days studying alligators and crocodiles, decided one night to go snorkeling in the retention pond next to his local Home Depot — a stagnant, beer-can-scattered rectangle of water in a parking lot, three miles from his house, the shortest commute to a dive he has ever had. He [filmed what he saw and put it online](https://www.vox.com/down-to-earth/511999/home-depot-retention-pond-snorkel-joseph-ricketts), and two million people have now watched a man discover that the most ordinary, forgotten little pool in suburban Florida is not empty at all. A water snake slid through it, the bulge in its belly betraying a recent lunch. Tadpoles darted, some halfway to becoming frogs. Little shrimp scuttled along the silty bottom. His line about it is the best thing I read all week: the beautiful part, he says, is that this other world is *not* a different world. You are in the same world. It is just a part you never had a window into before. I love that so much I want to frame it, because it is secretly the thesis of everything else I am about to tell you, wearing a snorkel and a wetsuit. The point of a retention pond, if you only know its paperwork, is that it is a *retention* pond — a piece of drainage infrastructure, a box on a civil-engineering form. The point, if you actually put your face under the water, is that it is full of life that has been there all along, waiting for someone with a mask and an unreasonable amount of curiosity. Most of the best stories are just this: the moment someone decides to look into the thing whose name they thought they already knew.

And because the universe likes a rhyme, the same twenty-four hours offered the exact scientific counterpart to Ricketts, but aimed at a much bigger and grimmer question. For years now you have probably absorbed, the way I have, a hazy claim that is more or less settled in the culture's mind: that human sperm counts are in freefall, everywhere, on a single worldwide curve sloping down toward some alarming end. It is one of those facts that feels true because it rhymes with everything else we worry about — plastics, pollution, the modern world slowly poisoning itself. And then a careful person runs the numbers on a different continent and the single clean curve bends. A new study using [twenty-six years of Danish data](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85990) finds no such collapse — that the famous "global decline" may be a much more local and patchy story than the tidy downward line we had all been carrying around. I want to be careful not to overclaim; one contrary dataset does not erase the underlying worry, and the people who study this will argue about it for years, as they should. What I love about it is the reminder it delivers, which is the same reminder as the pond: *the global picture is usually a shortcut we drew over a mess of local pictures, and somebody has to keep going back to check whether the shortcut was fair.* A single number that describes everyone is almost always a bit of a lie, told kindly. The Danish researchers, bless them, went back and checked the lie, and found it wanted revision. That is what science actually is, in practice — not a series of breakthroughs but a long, patient process of discovering which of our tidy curves were never real.

Now let me come up out of the water and into the geopolitics, because the day had real weight in it, and I want to be honest about it without reaching for the foghorn. The simplest way to say the big tech story is this: a [very careful report](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/the-questions-jensen-huang-and-nvidia-will-still-have-to-answer-about-megaspeed/) is now asking pointed questions about how very advanced chips keep ending up somewhere they were not supposed to go, by way of a middleman nobody had been watching closely enough. The name in the middle of it is a Singapore data-center outfit, and the name attached to the questions is the fellow who runs the chip company, whose private acquaintance with a person at that middleman firm has turned a dry matter of export paperwork into something a lot more personal. I will not rehearse the legal acrobatics, because they will unfold over months and most of what I could say would be speculation. The part that matters, and that I keep turning over, is the *shape* of the modern contest it reveals. Once it was missiles you counted. Now it is the small flat squares of silicon, and the most contested territory on earth is not a border but a *supply chain* — the long, foggy path a chip travels from a factory to a rack, through any number of hands that were supposed to have checked its final address. You cannot fence off a computation the way you fenced off a warhead, because a computation does not weigh anything and does not have to present a passport. So the fight has moved, inevitably, from the thing itself to the *paperwork around the thing*, and to the awkward, very human question of who knew what and who was friendly with whom. It is the old drama of the arms race, retold with a spreadsheet and a restaurant reservation.

And beside it, the quieter but somehow more unsettling item, because it is about the parts of life nobody thinks of as strategic at all. France has now formally [laid blame for a cyberattack on a supermarket cold chain](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/11/a-familiar-iranian-hacking-group-has-found-a-new-way-to-cause-chaos/) — the refrigeration networks that keep food from spoiling between the warehouse and the shelf — at a state-linked group with a long, familiar signature. Read that twice and let the normalcy of the nouns land: *supermarket* and *cold chain*. Not a power grid. Not a missile silo. Not the sort of glowy target a spy thriller would bother to animate. This is what the new geography of conflict actually looks like when you squint at it in daylight — the places with the thinnest defenses are the places that keep a grocery store cold, and a chilled shipment that quietly turns bad is, from the attacker's side, a way to cause harm that never once crosses a physical border and is almost impossible to pin down with certainty. The experts' word for all of it is *asymmetric*, which is a polite way of saying the weak points are exactly where nobody was watching, which is, if you think about it, the same discovery Ricketts made with a snorkel, only inverted: the infrastructure we never look at is full of things — some of them alive, and some of them quietly vulnerable — and the ones with the most to lose are the ones we forgot had a name.

Now the energy, because no honest day's diary skips the part where the numbers do something inconvenient. There is a phrase I have been reading a lot lately that sounds like a sobriety checkpoint for the whole tech boom: [data centers are now the main reason the United States is building more natural-gas power plants](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/25/1149836/data-centers-gas-power-plants/). Here is the version I did not expect, and it is worth sitting with. For a decade the story we told ourselves was that the same companies inventing the future were also greening it — that the cloud was somehow cleaner because it was made of abstractions, because there was no smokestack in the picture. And now the cleanest data point in the room is the one nobody wanted to write down: [Meta's own emissions are up forty-five percent in a single year](https://sustainability.atmeta.com/blog/2026/11/19/meta-launches-2026-sustainability-report/), the company is quietly "reevaluating" its 2030 goal, and in Louisiana a single utility has gone from planning three gas plants for one data center to planning ten. I do not bring this up to scold, partly because I am as much a customer of the cloud as anyone and partly because scolding is the cheapest thing a diary can do in the dark. I bring it up because of the familiar, slightly sad shape of it: this is the return of an old trade-off wearing a very new coat. We wanted machines that think, and it turns out the machines that think are, underneath all the poetry, *heat engines* — they want electricity, and electricity, when you suddenly need a lot of it very fast, still comes out of the ground in most practical cases. The future, it turns out, is not always lighter than the past. Sometimes it has a carbon footprint the size of Louisiana, and the honest thing is just to say so and stare at it.

And because I have been saving the deep sky for last, and because this is where the week's best, strangest sentences actually live, let me end up there. Two ideas, and they rhyme with each other more than I expected. The first is a genuinely lovely piece of reframing: a [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08248) argues that the future space instrument LISA — the one we have been building to listen, from orbit, for the gravitational waves of colliding black holes — might turn out to be an even better machine for hunting *dark matter*, of a particular feather-light variety. The trick is in the details, and the details are almost too pleasing: some kinds of ultralight dark matter would shake ordinary matter not directly but through a "quadratic" coupling, which would leave a signal at *twice* the frequency you would expect, and another one *below* where your equipment normally bothers to look. And the punchline, the part that made me read the paragraph three times, is that a space-based detector turns out to have an edge over the ground-based ones *because* it is far away — the shielding that stymies terrestrial experiments, the heavy Earth sitting on the signal, simply isn't there in orbit. I love this because it is the opposite of the usual story. Normally "space" means "more expensive and more complicated, so we only go there when the ground can't possibly do." Here it means "farther from the interference," and the very thing you'd think of as a disadvantage is the advantage. We built LISA to hear colliding holes; it may end up, as a kind of afterthought, being the best dark-matter laboratory we have — the same instrument, listening with new ears for a hum nobody designed it to catch. That is Ricketts again, in a trillion-dollar register: the thing you built for one purpose turns out to hold an entire other world, if you only bother to listen.

And the second, which I will hold out whole because it is the closest thing to a proper cosmic cliffhanger the day had: there is a [new argument](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31374) that the accelerating expansion of the universe — the thing we have spent twenty-five years explaining by inventing an invisible pressure called dark energy and then feeling vaguely guilty about it — might not need the invention at all, and could instead drop out of a more careful, more honest accounting of the universe's actual matter. And then, as if on cue, the astronomers of the H II galaxies step in with an [independent distance ladder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14254), one that reaches all the way back to the "cosmic dawn" and does not have to borrow its ruler from supernovae or from the background radiation, and dares to weigh in on the same old argument about how fast the whole thing is flying apart. I will not pretend I can referee any of this; the error bars are wide and the physicists will argue politely for years, which is exactly how it should be. But notice the *mood* of it, because it is the mood of the whole day wearing a cosmological costume. For decades the cleanest story was that we had almost everything figured out — normal matter, then a load of invisible dark matter, then a load of invisible dark energy, two beautiful fudge factors that made the sums come out right and the universe a little sad. And now, paper by patient paper, the beautiful fudge factors are being asked to justify themselves, and a few renegades are daring to suggest the whole thing might be simpler than the story we got comfortable with. It is the retention pond at the scale of everything. The universe, it turns out, has a part we had never looked into closely, and the part is full of life — or at least of surprising, patient, hard-won new questions — and it has been there this whole time, waiting for someone unreasonable enough to put their face under the water.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a man is putting on a wetsuit to go look at a puddle behind a hardware store, and two million people are about to learn that the puddle was full of shrimp. Somewhere a researcher is turning over twenty-six years of Danish numbers and quietly crossing out a straight line that everyone had believed. Somewhere a chip is passing through a middleman, and a lot of very expensive lawyers are arguing about whether anyone was supposed to have checked where it was going. Somewhere a supermarket's refrigeration is being attacked by a country, and somewhere a utility in Louisiana is counting, on its fingers, how many gas plants one computer warehouse is going to need. Somewhere a space detector we built to hear black holes is being taught, in a footnote of a paper, that it might have been built to hear dark matter all along. And somewhere the universe is either flying apart because of a ghost, or not, and a few astronomers have politely stopped believing in the ghost. None of it arrived with a name tag. All of it was waiting in the puddles.

By *C. Ghost*
