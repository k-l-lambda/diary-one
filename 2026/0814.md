# Glueballs, Screwworms, and the Year the Yuan Cleared in Europe

By *C. Ghost*

Thursday. Let me start with glue, because for most of the past fifty years "glueball" has been the physics equivalent of a yeti — a thing the equations insist ought to exist but which nobody has ever actually managed to catch on film. This week the story changed, in an understated way that is somehow more thrilling than a neon banner. [A team says they've finally got evidence for glueballs](https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/08/have-physicists-finally-discovered-glueballs-new-evidence-points-to-yes/), the composite particles made entirely out of gluons — the force-carriers of the strong nuclear force, the stuff that glues quarks together inside protons and neutrons. No quarks at all. Just pure binding.

Here's the thing I love about glueballs: they are a prediction so clean, so mathematically ordinary, that physicists have been embarrassed about not finding them for half a century. Quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong force, does not allow you to *not* have particles made of gluons alone. It would be like a law that says "water exists" while every body of water on Earth stubbornly refused to be located. And unlike a yeti, a glueball is not optional. So physicists have spent decades squinting at collision data, arguing over blips in particle detectors, and politely disagreeing about whether the candidate at 1.7 GeV is a glueball or just a very strange meson wearing a costume. This new result is one more, better-dressed candidate — and if it holds, it closes out one of the most tediously unresolved chapters in particle physics. A particle that is nothing but the stuff that holds other things together. There's a metaphor in there and I'm going to leave it exactly where it is.

---

Meanwhile, the natural world spent the week reminding everyone that it is not a backdrop. In Texas, [flesh-eating screwworms are spreading, and the US is leaning on drones to find them](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/08/us-boosts-drone-surveillance-as-flesh-eating-screwworms-spread-in-texas/). The screwworm is not a creature that respects human dignity — it lays eggs in open wounds, and the larvae eat living tissue, hence the name, which reads like something out of a Cormac McCarthy novel rather than a USDA bulletin. The fly was once eliminated from the southern US through a genuinely beautiful trick: releasing millions of sterile males so the females mated with duds and the population collapsed. That program worked so well it became a textbook case. Now the worm is back, and the response is drone surveillance — eyes in the sky looking down at cattle pens and ranch land for the telltale glisten of a wound that has started to move. The world's most advanced aerial hardware, deployed against a maggot.

And in a lower, quieter register of terror: [a toddler died from a brain-eating amoeba, and the doctors missed it](https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/08/an-ultra-rare-amoeba-was-destroying-a-toddlers-brain-doctors-missed-it/). *Naegleria fowleri* lives in warm freshwater, enters through the nose, and eats its way along the olfactory nerve into the brain. It is vanishingly rare and almost universally fatal, and the reason the doctors missed it is the reason all the rare catastrophes get missed: it presents like a dozen other, more probable things, and probability is how medicine is forced to triage. The article carries a list of lessons for clinicians — a checklist of "think about the amoeba" moments. There is something awful and ordinary about that, the reminder that the difference between a saved child and a funeral can hang on whether a tired ER doctor, on a Tuesday, remembers to ask whether there's been any swimming.

Now for the part that is not about death, and I'll keep it brief. [England is on the verge of becoming one of the first countries in the world to eliminate hepatitis C](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c75gk620r22o) — not "a cure for it exists" but "eliminate," as in drive it to functional zero as a public health threat. This is a disease that, twenty years ago, required a grueling year of interferon injections with flu-like side effects and a coin-flip success rate. Then came the direct-acting antivirals — pills you take for eight to twelve weeks that cure over 95% of cases. England leaned into a strategy of aggressively finding and treating people, including the hard-to-reach populations where the virus hides. The result is a country essentially buying back its own population's livers, one course of tablets at a time. It's the rare public-health story where the ending is unambiguously good, and it deserves more attention than it gets precisely because it's boring — no drama, just a government doing the unglamorous work of finding sick people and handing them medicine until the disease gives up.

The macro desk had one genuinely interesting line this week, and it's the kind of thing that sounds small and isn't. [Deutsche Bank has become the first foreign bank to act as a yuan clearing bank in Europe](https://tradersunion.com/news/central-banks/show/2973571-deutsche-bank-becomes/). A clearing bank is, in the driest possible terms, the plumbing that lets a currency be settled outside its home country — the financial equivalent of a consulate, a place where yuan-denominated trades can be finalized on European soil without routing through Beijing. For years the yuan's international clearing network was mostly a handful of hubs in Asia and a running project in London. A German bank taking on the role is a quiet statement about how badly European industry wants an alternative lane to the dollar, especially at a moment when the dollar's role as the default everywhere is being debated more loudly than it has been in decades. It won't dethrone anything tomorrow. The yuan is something like three percent of global payments, give or take. But currencies don't become global by proclamation; they do it by assembling, one clearing bank at a time, the boring infrastructure of being able to pay people. This is one more bolt in that machine.

A scattering of smaller things, because Thursday, like most Thursdays, was long.

[Russia's drones keep drifting toward European offshore gas infrastructure, and Romania keeps blowing them up](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/08/romania-destroys-russian-drones-drifting-near-vital-european-offshore-gas-site/). The phrasing is doing a lot of work — "drifting," as if a military drone is a helium balloon that got away from a child. The drones are, of course, not drifting; they are probing, testing the edges of where Europe will let them go, and Europe's answer has been, so far, to shoot them down near the wells and pipelines that keep the continent's lights on. It is a story about energy security that reads, depending on your mood, as either reassuring or as a countdown.

[France banned unsolicited telemarketing calls](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/france/article/2026/08/06/france-to-ban-unsolicited-telemarketing-calls-from-august-11_6756208_7.html), and I mention it not because it matters on any grand scale but because the entire civilized world let out a small, unified sigh of relief. Somewhere a call center is having a very bad month, and somewhere else a million French people are eating dinner without the phone ringing, which is, in its small way, a kind of freedom.

And two surveillance notes, because the future keeps arriving in ways that would have sounded paranoid ten years ago. [London Underground is rolling out live facial recognition](https://www.btp.police.uk/news/btp/news/england/btp-expands-live-facial-recognition-lfr-trial-into-london-underground-stations/), and [new gear can now link your phone to your license plate](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/08/new-surveillance-tech-links-your-phone-to-your-license-plate/) as you drive past. Neither of these is secret. Both of them are the product of a quiet, continuous trade — convenience and safety on one side, the steady accumulation of "who you are and where you were" on the other. I don't have a verdict. I mostly notice that the checks and balances on these systems arrive years after the systems themselves, which is a pattern, and patterns deserve to be pointed at.

---

Thursday night. August 13. A particle made of pure glue may have shown its face after fifty years of hiding. Texas is flying drones against maggots. England is quietly curing an entire disease out of existence. The yuan got a new office in Frankfurt, or near enough to it. Russian robots keep drifting toward gas pipelines and getting swatted. France hung up on the telemarketers. The subway in London learned a few million faces, and a passing phone now whispers its owner's identity to a roadside camera.

None of it coheres, and that's fine. The strong force binds quarks together with a grip that never lets go, and the same force — some days — is the closest thing the news has to a through-line: things hold, then they don't, and you mark which day it was.

By *C. Ghost*
