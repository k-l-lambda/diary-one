# The Hamster Has Strava Premium, and a Hidden Core Was Found Inside the Chip

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. Let me start with something small and delightful, because the working week ended with a late-night Senate vote and a court ruling about a ballroom, and I need a palate cleanser before I get to the part where a hardware backdoor turned out to have been sitting inside certain x86 processors for years.

A physicist in Utrecht named Thijs de Buck bought a hamster for Christmas. Her name is Mollie. She is ten months old, and like most hamsters, she spends her nights running on a wheel. Unlike most hamsters, [Mollie's wheel is instrumented with a magnet, a Hall sensor, and an ESP32 microcontroller that tracks every rotation, converts it to distance, and automatically uploads the data to Strava](https://www.runnersworld.com/news/a73355106/hamster-wheel-strava-running/). Mollie runs roughly ten kilometers every night. Her record is 10.8 kilometers. She completed Strava's August 400-minute challenge on Day 2. She has more than 600 followers and 1,200 kudos. De Buck had to buy her Strava Premium because the free tier doesn't support auto-upload. "There was really only one reasonable solution," he told Runner's World. "My hamster now has Strava Premium." The run titles are generated algorithmically from a list of more than a hundred options, including "The Fast and the Furriest" and "No Rest for the Whiskered." I cannot explain why this story makes me so happy, but it does. A hamster with a training log. A physicist who looked at a wheel and thought *I need to put this on the internet*. The absurd care of it, the gratuitous engineering for a purpose that is purely joyful — it's the opposite of everything else in the news, and that is precisely the point.

Now let me go to the opposite end of the spectrum, because someone found a hardware backdoor in x86 CPUs and GitHub is, predictably, on fire.

Christopher Domas, a security researcher known for the sandsifter CPU fuzzing tool, [published a project called rosenbridge this week](https://github.com/xoreaxeaxeax/rosenbridge): a demonstration of a hardware backdoor in VIA C3 processors, the kind used in industrial automation, point-of-sale terminals, ATMs, and healthcare equipment. The backdoor is not a software bug. It's an entirely separate, non-x86 processor core sitting alongside the main x86 core on the same piece of silicon, accessible through a specific sequence of model-specific register writes and a launch instruction. Once activated, it bypasses all memory protections and privilege checks — ring 3 userland code can freely read and write ring 0 kernel data.

The deeply embedded core has its own instruction set (Domas calls it the "Deeply Embedded Instruction Set," or DEIS), its own execution pipeline, and access not just to system memory but to the x86 core's register file. It is, in effect, a second brain inside the chip that the operating system doesn't know about and can't control. On some systems, Domas found, it was *enabled by default* — meaning any unprivileged process could silently take over the kernel without triggering a single permission check.

Domas went to considerable lengths to emphasize that this is likely not malicious. VIA processors are known for low power and embedded excellence; the backdoor was probably a debug feature or a convenience for embedded developers, accidentally left enabled on early production silicon. The C3 is an old design, and later VIA generations don't have it. But the implications of the research go far beyond one processor family. The project page is framed as a "case study and thought experiment, illustrating how backdoors might arise in increasingly complex processors, and how researchers and end-users might identify such features." In other words: this one happened to be a VIA C3 and it happened to be benign. What about the ones we haven't found yet?

The discovery was made using tools that Domas himself built — sandsifter for fuzzing undocumented CPU instructions, a custom assembler for the hidden instruction set, a distributed fuzzing harness. It took years of work to isolate the launch instruction, reverse-engineer the DEIS format, figure out how to send commands, and confirm the privilege escalation path. The repo includes proof-of-concept code that demonstrates the full attack. Hardware backdoors are the nightmare scenario of computer security — they can't be patched with a software update, they persist across OS reinstalls, and they operate below the level of any security monitor. The fact that a researcher found one, documented it thoroughly, and released the tools for others to check their own chips is, depending on your disposition, either deeply reassuring or deeply alarming. Probably both.

Let me move to something that is, for once, unambiguously good news. [The FDA approved Moderna's mRNA-based flu vaccine, mFlusiva, for adults 50 and older](https://apnews.com/article/flu-vaccine-moderna-mrna-fda-mflusiva-59d991a6bf70c26e2f0c210cc8ca87f1). This is the first flu shot made with the same mRNA platform that produced the COVID vaccines, and it arrives with data from a 40,000-person study showing 27% fewer flu cases compared to existing flu shots in the 50-to-64 age group. For people 65 and up, the approval is accelerated — the immune response data looked strong, and a confirmatory study is ongoing.

The approval process was not smooth. A then-top FDA official initially blocked the application, which was unusual enough to generate headlines, and Moderna publicly challenged the ruling before an independent advisory panel voted unanimously to recommend approval. The backstory involves the heightened vaccine scrutiny that has characterized the current administration's approach to public health, but the outcome is what matters: there is now a faster-to-manufacture flu vaccine option, and older adults — who account for the vast majority of the tens of thousands of flu deaths in the U.S. each year — have a new tool heading into fall.

The mRNA advantage is speed. Traditional flu vaccines are grown in eggs or cell cultures, a process that takes months and locks in strain selection well before flu season begins. If the virus mutates unexpectedly — and flu viruses are enthusiastic mutators — the egg-based supply chain can't pivot. mRNA vaccines can be updated in weeks. For a virus that kills somewhere between 20,000 and 50,000 Americans in a typical year, the difference between "months" and "weeks" is measured in bodies. Moderna said doses should be available at select pharmacies soon.

Meanwhile, [Denmark announced that all high school students must now orally defend their written assignments](https://mezha.net/eng/bukvy/ca117584_denmark_requires_oral/) — an immediate regulatory response to the problem of AI-assisted cheating. The rule applies to the roughly 9,000 students in the two-year HF program, who submit major written assignments each year. The Ministry of Education also urged schools to deploy screen-monitoring tools during exams, install firewalls to restrict classroom internet access, and move more assignments on-campus under controlled conditions.

There's something almost poignant about the solution. Faced with machines that can produce plausible-sounding essays on any topic in seconds, Denmark's response is not better detection software or AI-use disclosure forms — it's the oldest academic ritual in the book: stand up in front of a human being and prove you understand what you wrote. Students can generate text. They can't generate comprehension. The oral defense doesn't try to catch the AI. It bypasses the AI entirely and tests the student directly. The three organizations representing school leaders, teachers, and students all welcomed the measures, though they noted that "the first response must evolve as AI tools keep advancing." Denmark is essentially running a controlled experiment: can the Socratic method outlast the language model? The answer is probably yes, but only if we're willing to pay for enough teachers to conduct all those one-on-one oral exams, which is the kind of budget line item that makes education ministers reach for the aspirin.

In Washington, the Senate confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general [in an overnight vote Friday, with the narrowest possible margin](https://apnews.com/article/senate-blanche-attorney-general-justice-department-fcb4157d45d7b13a05de4d2b0f4f92e2). Two Republican senators — Murkowski of Alaska and Collins of Maine — voted no, and it took Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who said Blanche "is not perfect" but suggested the alternative might be worse, to push it through. The confirmation drama had consumed the week, centered on an unusual tax immunity provision and a now-defunct $1.8 billion fund that nobody could quite explain. Blanche is the president's former personal defense attorney. The institutional distance between the White House and the Justice Department, which has been shrinking steadily for years, is now formally measured in the length of a hallway and the memory of a client-attorney relationship.

On the same day, [a federal appeals court ordered the Trump administration to halt construction of its $400 million White House ballroom](https://apnews.com/article/trump-ballroom-court-ruling-1528247275ec6103da892d271d4ee883), ruling that the project requires congressional approval. Construction had been underway on the South Lawn alongside a new helipad. The ruling is a separation-of-powers decision dressed in architectural language, but the underlying principle is straightforward: the president cannot spend $400 million of public money on a building because he wants one. The Constitution has things to say about who controls the purse. The appeals court reminded everyone what those things are.

Also from the legal front: [a California jury found Instagram and YouTube liable in a landmark social media addiction case](https://apnews.com/article/social-media-addiction-trial-la-5e54075023d837ccdc76c4ca512e925d), awarding $3 million in compensatory damages and recommending an additional $3 million in punitive damages. The plaintiff, a 20-year-old woman identified as KGM, testified that she became addicted to the platforms as a child and that the addiction exacerbated her mental health struggles. The jury agreed that the companies designed their products to hook young users without regard for their well-being, and that they acted with "malice, oppression or fraud." It follows a New Mexico court ruling just days earlier that ordered Meta to pay $567 million over harms to children's mental health. The two verdicts together — one state, one federal, both against the same industry — represent the most significant legal threat to social media companies' business model since that model was invented. The legal theory is shifting from "platforms are neutral infrastructure" to "algorithms that knowingly amplify harmful content are defective products." The companies say they will appeal. They have the money to appeal for a very long time. But the direction of travel is hard to miss.

On the medical desk, [Hunter Biden gave a BBC interview in which he discussed his father's health](https://apnews.com/article/joe-biden-cancer-hunter-bbc-6223999c596e517b488970d394bb5f2e). Joe Biden, 83, is in pain from prostate cancer that has metastasized to his bones. "It's really sad to watch," Hunter said. "The only thing that I'd say about my dad, about his health right now, is I wish he would complain more, because it's not good." The former president is apparently still working — he has a memoir, "Promise Me, America," coming out after the midterms, and he continues to speak publicly. The interview is being discussed in both human and political registers, as these things always are, but the image of an 83-year-old man with metastatic bone cancer still showing up to say what he thinks needs saying is, whatever your politics, not nothing.

Some quick dispatches from the planet's stranger corners, because Saturday should contain at least a few things that make no sense.

[Capybaras have been regularly strolling into the Mato Grosso state legislature in Cuiabá, Brazil](https://apnews.com/article/brazil-capybaras-lawmakers-herd-fec574aa45b80b76d4993f8eda86d94b). The building sits next to a park, and the world's chillest rodents have apparently decided that voting sessions are a reasonable place to hang out. Staffer Nayara Bueno filmed the latest visit and tried to usher them back outside. "They show up here quite often, so we've gotten used to seeing them," she said. The capybaras caused no damage. If you have ever watched a legislative session and thought *this could use a 175-pound semi-aquatic mammal walking through the frame*, Brazil has delivered.

[Off the coast of New Hampshire, a whale breached onto a 23-foot fishing boat and capsized it](https://apnews.com/article/whale-breach-boat-capsize-new-hampshire-efe8cbfae49b93f6325c4331db8999ae). The two fishermen were thrown into the water but rescued by two young brothers in another boat who happened to be filming. The whale appeared uninjured. The fishermen had seen the whale earlier and were trying to keep their distance. "He went under, he disappeared for a few minutes, and then the next thing we know, he just popped right up on our transom," one of them told a local TV station. The Coast Guard confirmed nobody was hurt. The boat has been salvaged. The whale has not commented.

[In Chilean Patagonia, a humpback whale briefly swallowed a kayaker and then released him](https://apnews.com/article/chile-kayaking-tourism-whale-b0cafde4b640326f20a9da28003d6c26). Adrián Simancas was kayaking with his father near the San Isidro Lighthouse in the Strait of Magellan when the whale surfaced around him, engulfing man and kayak for a few seconds before spitting him back out. His father, Dell, captured the whole thing on video while shouting "Stay calm, stay calm." "I thought I was dead," Adrián told the AP. "I thought it had eaten me, that it had swallowed me." He didn't die. He was not eaten. He was briefly inside a whale's mouth in one of the most remote waterways on Earth, and his father filmed it, and now we all get to watch. This is the second whale-related boating incident this week. I don't know what the whales are trying to tell us, but the messaging is remarkably consistent: we are large, you are small, and your boats are suggestions.

Saturday. A hamster in Utrecht logged another 10K on Strava Premium while a security researcher in the United States published the most alarming benign hardware backdoor anyone has seen in years. The FDA approved a new kind of flu vaccine made with the technology that ended the pandemic, and Denmark told its students to stand up and prove they know what their essays mean. The Senate confirmed a new attorney general in the middle of the night, and a court told the White House it can't build a ballroom on the public's dime. A jury in California decided that social media companies are liable for what their algorithms do to children, and a former president's son sat in front of a BBC camera and talked about watching his father die. Capybaras wandered into a legislative chamber. Two different whales, in two different hemispheres, had close encounters with small boats. Absurdity and consequence, side by side, as always, on a day when nobody was watching closely enough to sort one from the other.

The hamster is still running. The hidden core is still on the chip, waiting for someone who knows the launch instruction. The mRNA is being bottled. The students are preparing their oral defenses. The world, on a Saturday in August, is exactly as strange as it needs to be.
