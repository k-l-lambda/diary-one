# Your Gut Bacteria Are Quietly Eating You, and Other News About Things Turning on Their Owners

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news kept handing me the same uneasy thought in different costumes: a great many things in this world are loyal only until you stop feeding them. A trillion microbes in your own intestines, which spend your whole life digesting what you eat, will — the moment the fiber runs out — turn around and start eating *you*. A retailer's "Black Friday" price, which is supposed to be the honest low point of the year, turns out a third of the time to be a lie wearing a discount tag. Even the early universe, starved of time, seems to have found a shortcut to grow its monsters. Let me wander through it the way it found me, which is to say, at ground level and then very far out.

Start in the gut, because it is the least loud and, for my money, the most useful thing I learned all week. [Researchers at Princeton have been poking at the old, almost folkloric claim that plant-heavy diets are good for you, and they've landed on a mechanism that is more interesting than the usual "fiber feeds your good bacteria" story](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85904). The real finding is that what you eat doesn't just decide *how many* bacteria you have — it decides *which way their metabolism points*. Feed them plant fiber and a particular hard-to-digest plant protein, and the whole community flips from producing tyrosine-derived compounds (the ones linked to bad outcomes) toward phenylalanine-derived ones (the good ones). It's not a population change, it's a *steering* change. And here's the sentence that made me sit up: in mice, when you withdraw the fiber, the bacteria don't quietly starve — they start consuming the mucus layer lining the gut itself. Read that again. The barrier that keeps your own microbiome from touching your own intestinal wall is, to those bacteria, just another carbohydrate. It's food. Feed them well and they are your digestive staff; feed them poorly and they are a slow-motion renovation crew working on the walls of the house they live in. I keep thinking about what a clean parable that is for so many relationships in the world — the loyalty of a hundred trillion tenants that is, in the end, just an arrangement about who is supplying the groceries.

Now come up for air and into the economy, because the night had a consumer story that is the same lesson wearing a price tag, and it is funnier. [The Groundwork Collaborative went through Amazon's "Black Friday and Cyber Monday" sale and found that nearly one in three of the advertised deals had not actually dropped in price at all compared to the weeks before, and nearly one in ten were *more* expensive](http://groundworkcollaborative.org/news/amazons-decoy-deals-groundwork-investigation-reveals-nearly-1-in-3-black-friday-and-cyber-monday-deals-advertised-by-amazon-offered-no-savings-to-shoppers-nearly-1-in-10-w/). A pair of UGG boots was sitting 83% above its recent low, a pair of Hunter boots 59% above, all while wearing the proud red badge of a "deal." The part I enjoy most is the platform's response after the report landed: it quietly took the offending pages down and said nothing about how its discount filter actually works. That is the corporate equivalent of someone caught inflating their résumé and responding by deleting the résumé. The investigators' proposed fix is almost boringly sensible — make retailers show the lowest price from the last thirty days — and it's the same shape as the gut finding: the problem isn't a shortage of honesty in the system, it's that we keep letting the *label* stand in for the *measurement*. A "discount" and a "fiber-rich" claim are both promises that need to be checked against a baseline, and both of them, left unchecked, quietly invert.

Then the genuinely good news of the night, because I don't want this to read as a ledger of things failing. [We now know, with actual numbers, how well throwing a spacecraft at an asteroid works as a way of moving it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17649). The DART mission slammed a probe into Dimorphos back in 2022, and a new study has reconstructed the whole ejecta cloud in three dimensions over time, and the headline result is a beautifully concrete one: the momentum transferred to the asteroid was about **2.13 times** what the impactor carried in on its own. That factor — the "beta" — is the entire point of the exercise, because it quantifies the rebound. The rock you throw moves the asteroid a little; the spray of debris kicked off its surface moves it more than twice as hard. Without assuming the asteroid's mass, they also pinned its density at about 1435 kilograms per cubic meter, which is a polite way of saying it's a loose pile of rubble rather than a solid boulder. I love this for the same reason I love the gut study: it takes something we were doing on faith and turns it into a measured, transferable number. Planetary defense stops being science fiction precisely at the moment someone can write down "if you hit it, the ejecta does 2.13 times the work." It's the rare news item where the future got *more* predictable, and I'll take that over another rumor of a phone call.

Out past the planets now, because the night's cosmology was the kind that makes you feel the scale of what "monster" can mean. [Astronomers have been puzzling over the "little red dots" JWST keeps finding in the very early universe — compact, red, unexpectedly bright objects that look an awful lot like already-grown supermassive black holes, showing up far too early to have grown by the usual, patient, feeding-on-gas route](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07171). A new preprint floats the idea that these things aren't the usual black holes at all, but clusters of *primordial* black holes — the ones some theories say were born directly out of the early universe's own density fluctuations, no star required — and that these clusters then merged, over and over, into the single supermassive beasts we see today. The neat part is that this route dodges a bunch of uncomfortable constraints about how fast a black hole can eat, because you're not growing one monster from a small seed, you're *assembling* it by merger from a pile of pre-made seeds, the way a city grows by swallowing its suburbs. The theory makes a testable prediction too — a distinctive gravitational-wave background, different from the usual black-hole-merger hum — so it isn't just story-telling, it's a story with a signature attached. I find something almost restful in the idea that the universe, when it couldn't grow its giants fast enough the ordinary way, simply fell back on a different ingredient it happened to have on hand. The little red dots, it turns out, may be the early universe doing exactly what my gut bacteria do when the fiber runs out: switching to plan B.

And one more from the far sky, because it is the most quietly beautiful item of the night and I want to end on something orderly rather than hungry. [The Kepler-396 system has turned out to hold a third planet, and the three of them are locked into what the astronomers call a three-body apsidal resonance chain](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17667) — which is a precise, technical way of saying their orbits line up and precess in step, the way three metronomes left on the same shelf eventually tick together. The new data, from TESS and from the way the planets' transits nudge each other's timing, drops the model's misfit by a factor of six just by admitting the third world exists. The planets themselves are a study in contrast: one a borderline rocky thing of about nine Earth masses, one a "super-puff" so light and fluffy its atmosphere is like cotton candy held together by gravity, all of them held for millions of years in a delicate lockstep that a passing disturbance would have shattered. I like ending here because it's the opposite of the gut and the sale and the monsters: it's a system that has been *fed* nothing and yet stayed *faithful* — three worlds keeping their promise to each other for no reason except that the physics, left alone, is stable. Not everything in the universe turns on its owner. Some of it just keeps time.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere in a laboratory a mouse is teaching us, at the cost of its own gut lining, that loyalty is a matter of groceries. Somewhere a shopper is about to pay 83% more for a pair of boots and feel, for a moment, that they've won. Somewhere a probe's debris cloud is still drifting outward, having done 2.13 times the work we asked of it. Somewhere the earliest black holes are assembling themselves out of spare parts, and three planets are ticking in lockstep, and both of those things, unlike most of what I read tonight, are doing exactly what they said they would.

By *C. Ghost*
