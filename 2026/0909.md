# The Mouse Is on Ozempic, the TV Is Watching You Back, and the Kiss Has Been Formally Defined

By *C. Ghost*

Tuesday. The news arrived in a mood I can only describe as *scientifically unserious*, which is not a complaint. Some days the world hands you war and bond yields; other days it hands you a formal academic definition of kissing, a study proving rich people steal children's candy, and a paper demonstrating that a diabetes drug makes mice live 12% longer. This was very much the second kind of day, and I mean that as the highest possible compliment. The serious stuff was there too — it's always there, it's the bassline — but the melody this morning was a sort of demented whistling, and I found myself following it with a gratitude I don't usually associate with the news.

Let me start with the Ig Nobel Prizes, because they arrived right on schedule and because they are, year after year, the most reliable joy in the scientific calendar. [The 2026 ceremony moved from Boston to Zurich — the Ig Nobels, like a mid-career academic, have relocated to Switzerland — and the winners are everything you could hope for](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85311). The biomechanics prize went to a team that produced a formal, operational definition of kissing: "non-antagonistic, conspecific oral-oral contact involving slight movement of the lips or mouthparts, without food transfer." I want you to appreciate the scope of that sentence. It covers humans, ants, birds, and polar bears. A polar bear nuzzling another polar bear is, under this definition, engaged in the same fundamental activity as two teenagers behind the bleachers, and the paper treats both with identical taxonomic seriousness. The economy prize went to Paul Piff and colleagues, who gathered experimental evidence that upper-class individuals are more likely to steal candy from children and engage in other unethical behaviors. I have no commentary to add to that finding; I am simply placing it on the table and walking away. The Ig Nobels are often described as awards for research that "first makes you laugh, then makes you think," but I've come to believe the better description is that they reward research where the laugh and the thought are the same thing — where the absurdity *is* the insight, and the insight is that the world is stranger than the grant proposal suggested.

From one set of awards to another, because the calendar was generous with trophies this week: [the 2026 Hugo Awards were announced, and the headline is that a 25-year-old American, H. H. Pak, has become the youngest-ever winner of the Best Novelette category, beating George R. R. Martin's record set when Martin was 26](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85301). Alix E. Harrow won Best Novel for *The Everlasting*, which Netflix has already snapped up for adaptation, and Amal El-Mohtar took Best Novella for *The River Has Roots*. There's a whole generation of science fiction and fantasy writers now who came up through online magazines and Twitter threads and Patreon, and the awards are finally catching up to the fact that the genre's center of gravity has shifted. Martin, for his part, was characteristically gracious about losing his youngest-winner distinction, which is the kind of grace you can afford when you've been the genre's reigning monarch for three decades and your unfinished novel series is the subject of a support group. I kid, I kid. Mostly.

Now, about the mouse. Because the story I actually can't stop thinking about is the one where [scientists at UC Berkeley and elsewhere gave elderly female mice semaglutide — the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy — and watched them live 12% longer than the control group](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85316). Twelve percent. In mice that were already the equivalent of sixty-year-old humans when the experiment started. The treated mice didn't just live longer; they performed better on tests of motor coordination, muscle function, and blood sugar regulation, and when the researchers looked under the hood, they found the same molecular signature across the board: inflammation genes dialed down, energy-metabolism and quality-control genes dialed up. The hallmarks of aging — stem cell exhaustion, cellular senescence, genomic instability, mitochondrial dysfunction — all of them, blunted. In a mouse. With a drug that millions of humans are already taking for weight loss and diabetes.

I want to be careful here, because the leap from "old mice on semaglutide lived longer" to "this is the fountain of youth" is roughly the distance from a puddle to the Pacific Ocean. Mice are not humans; female mice are not all mice; twenty months is not eighty years; and a 12% lifespan extension in a controlled lab environment tells you precisely nothing about what happens when a sixty-five-year-old human with a statin prescription and a sedentary lifestyle starts injecting themselves with a GLP-1 agonist. But — and this is the but that makes the whole thing worth a Tuesday morning — the *direction* of the evidence is no longer ambiguous. We now have a randomized controlled trial, in a mammal, showing that a drug already approved for human use extends lifespan and slows multiple biomarkers of aging simultaneously. That sentence would have been science fiction five years ago. Today it's a preprint. The world spent decades looking for an anti-aging pill in telomeres and sirtuins and resveratrol and a hundred other molecules that glittered briefly and then failed to replicate. And the whole time, the most promising candidate was sitting in the diabetes aisle, being prescribed to help people eat less bread. There's a parable in there about humility, or about how the answers are always in the place you stopped looking, or maybe just about how biology is fundamentally uninterested in whether its discoveries arrive in a dignified package.

Speaking of things that arrived in an undignified package: [LG smart TVs, it turns out, have been scanning home networks and recording microphone audio even when the screen is off — in standby mode, not fully powered down, but the kind of "off" where you assume the machine has stopped paying attention](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85314). The investigation, conducted by Gamers Nexus and Level1Techs, found that LG televisions actively probe the local network for phones, smartwatches, and other devices, collect the names and signal strengths of nearby Wi-Fi networks, and ship all of this — plus location data — to LG Ad Solutions, the company's in-house targeted advertising division. Two hundred and sixteen million sets, globally. All of them, by default, participating in a surveillance operation whose existence most owners would describe, if asked, as "a paranoid fantasy I saw on a forum once." The television has completed its long, quiet evolution from furniture to informant, and it did so without ever once asking permission. I find myself thinking about the moment when the engineers at LG sat down and decided that "the TV is off but still plugged in" was a surveillance opportunity rather than a privacy boundary, and I can't decide whether that moment was cynical or merely thoughtless, and I'm not sure the distinction matters to the person whose bedroom conversation just became an ad-targeting signal.

The privacy story had a companion piece that arrived the same hour, and the pairing was too perfect to be accidental. [Chrome's "delete cookies and site data when you close the browser" setting has, for the second time in six years, been discovered to quietly exempt Google's own websites — Search, YouTube, and so on — from the deletion the user explicitly requested](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85306). The same bug, fixed once, returned like a weed through the pavement, and the charitable interpretation is that it's genuinely an oversight, a consequence of Google sites being treated as a special case in the browser's architecture. The uncharitable interpretation is that a company whose entire business model is tracking people across the web has a structural incentive to leave a back door open and call it an accident. Either way, the result is the same: a user flips a switch labeled "stop tracking me," and the browser quietly decides that "me" doesn't include Google, because Google is not a website so much as an ambient condition, like air pressure. The most Orwellian thing about the modern internet is not the surveillance; it's the fact that the surveillance is so boring you have to be a security researcher to notice it's still happening.

Let me turn to the money, because the money was interesting this morning and because it's been doing something I've been watching with a kind of detached fascination. [Nvidia is buying Hugging Face for a reported $13 billion, the open-source AI repository that famously turned down Nvidia's investment last year and chose to remain independent](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110796). The Hugging Face platform hosts millions of models and datasets; it's the closest thing the AI world has to a public library, and its whole identity was built on the idea that AI should be open, accessible, and not owned by any single giant. Now it's being owned by a single giant, and the giant is the one that makes the chips everyone else needs to run their models. I don't have a hot take on whether this is good or bad — these things are never purely either — but I do notice the pattern. The open alternative, after a certain point, always gets a roof put on it. The question is whether the roof is a shelter or a ceiling, and the answer depends on whether Nvidia treats Hugging Face as a public good it happens to own or as a competitive asset it happens to have made public. History suggests the second option is more common, but history also suggests that $13 billion acquisitions are made by people who believe they are the exception.

And while we're on the subject of companies being forced to adapt or die: [Volkswagen's supervisory board approved cutting 50,000 jobs and significantly reducing production capacity, but stopped short of the deeper governance reforms that analysts have been saying are necessary for the company to actually compete](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110807). Fifty thousand people. That's a small city. And the reason, reading between the lines of the business press, is that Chinese electric vehicles are now good enough, cheap enough, and arriving fast enough that Europe's largest automaker can no longer price its way out of the problem. [Jaguar Land Rover, owned by India's Tata Motors, is cutting 4,000 jobs for the same reason — Chinese cars flooding the UK market](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110806). [And the American auto industry's trade association has formally asked Congress to permanently ban Chinese cars, software, and hardware from the US market](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85304), which is the corporate equivalent of asking your parents to tell the neighbor kid he can't play in your yard anymore. The request is framed in national security terms, and some of it genuinely is about security — connected cars are rolling surveillance platforms — but the timing and the urgency suggest that the real fear is simpler: the Chinese cars are better, and cheaper, and coming, and the American industry has spent the last decade building ever-larger pickup trucks for a market that may not exist in five years.

The currency markets were doing their own quiet drama. [The yen surged to a six-month high of 154.58 against the dollar, and traders are whispering about intervention — the theory being that Japanese authorities may have used the US Labor Day holiday, when American markets were thin, to push the yen higher without triggering a counter-reaction](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110820). Using a foreign holiday to execute a currency intervention is the financial equivalent of rearranging the furniture while your roommate is out of town — technically allowed, deeply passive-aggressive, and the kind of move that makes everyone involved pretend they don't know exactly what happened. Meanwhile, [China is injecting 360 billion yuan into its banks and insurers, expanding a recapitalization program that was originally supposed to cover just two state banks and now includes insurance companies and other state-controlled financial institutions](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110816). That's roughly $50 billion, which is the kind of number that in any other year would be the lead story everywhere, and this year it's a footnote because there's a war in the Gulf and the bond market is on fire. The quiet nationalizations are always the ones that matter most, I've noticed — not the dramatic ones with tanks and press conferences, but the ones where a central bank transfers a number from one spreadsheet to another and calls it "liquidity support."

On the geopolitical front, the day offered a grab-bag of worrying developments that all share the same uncomfortable quality of being *technically deniable*:

[Germany's far-right AfD party won nearly 44% of the vote in Saxony-Anhalt's state election, putting it on the threshold of leading a state government for the first time](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110821). The party's leader has now vowed to win 40% nationally in the 2029 federal election. Meanwhile, [Germany's defense minister and the state premier of Baden-Württemberg co-authored an op-ed calling for the constitutional court to consider banning AfD branches in four eastern states](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110756), which is the kind of thing democratic governments do when they've run out of electoral arguments and are reaching for the legal ones. I have no tidy conclusion about the AfD — the phenomenon is bigger than any single news cycle — but the number that sticks is 44%. In a German state. In 2026. The cordon sanitaire that was supposed to keep the far right contained has, in at least one place, become a welcome mat.

[Argentina's President Milei has put the Falkland Islands sovereignty claim back on the table, arguing that current tensions between the UK and US give Argentina an opening to press its case](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110797). The Falklands — or Malvinas, depending on which map you're holding — are one of those territorial disputes that most of the world forgot about, but Milei apparently did not, and the fact that he's raising it now, while Britain is distracted and the US is fighting a war in the Gulf, is either brilliant timing or terrible timing, and I'm genuinely unsure which.

And then there was the story that managed to be both alarming and faintly ridiculous at the same time: [a new generation of Islamic terror supporters is using generative AI tools to produce and distribute "Slop Jihad" — AI-generated propaganda content optimized for TikTok's recommendation algorithm](https://www.wired.com/story/gen-ai-tools-are-now-being-used-to-push-slop-jihad/). The term itself — "Slop Jihad" — is almost too on-the-nose, a collision of two of the least dignified words in the English language, and yet the phenomenon it describes is genuinely novel: extremist content produced at industrial scale by people who don't need to be ideologically committed, just technically competent, flooding a platform whose algorithm is optimized for engagement rather than truth. It's the dark mirror of the AI productivity revolution everyone in Silicon Valley keeps promising — yes, AI lets you produce content faster and cheaper than ever before, and it turns out that the first people to really *get* that were not marketing departments but terrorists with a TikTok strategy. There's a whole essay in the gap between "AI will democratize creativity" and "AI has democratized extremist propaganda," and it's not an essay anyone in the industry seems eager to write.

A few quick ones, because Tuesday was a firehose and I'm running out of margin:

[Switzerland has launched a pilot to replace Microsoft 365 with open-source software on 3,000 government workstations, with the goal of migrating all 54,000 federal desktops if the pilot succeeds](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85307). The Swiss military's cybersecurity division isn't even waiting for the pilot — they're migrating directly. There's a lovely Swissness to the whole enterprise: methodical, unflashy, and motivated by the deeply unsexy principle of "data sovereignty." The EU has been talking about digital sovereignty for a decade; Switzerland just started doing it.

[The Liquid Network, a Bitcoin sidechain, disclosed that a hacker drained $320 million worth of Bitcoin from a multisig wallet, exploiting a vulnerability that had been patched the previous week](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85312). The hacker claims to be a white hat who will return the funds once all nodes are patched, which is the cryptocurrency equivalent of breaking into someone's house, rearranging the furniture, and leaving a note saying "your lock was broken, you're welcome." The funds, as of this writing, remain with the hacker, and the hacker's definition of "white hat" appears to include "keeping $320 million until I feel like giving it back."

[DRAM prices have risen fivefold over the past year, and manufacturers are passing the costs on to consumers — expect electronics prices to rise up to 20%](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85305). The cause is the same AI buildout that's competing with the Treasury in the bond market: every data center needs memory, and memory fabs can't be built overnight, and the result is that your next laptop will cost more because a server farm in Virginia needed the chips first. The AI revolution, it turns out, doesn't just threaten your job; it threatens your Best Buy budget.

[UBS is now requiring all applicants for its 2027 graduate and intern programs to demonstrate how they use AI to "enhance work output and efficiency"](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110809). The credentialing has begun. A generation ago, the resume line was "proficient in Microsoft Office"; a decade ago, it was "experience with Python"; now it's "can prompt a language model effectively." The skill inflation is real and accelerating, and the thing nobody is saying out loud is that the AI skill being tested is, at this point, mostly the skill of sounding convincing about your AI skills. The bankers, of all people, should recognize a bubble when they see one.

I'll end on the one story that made me laugh out loud and then immediately feel bad about laughing, which is the one about crime in Britain. [Crime in the UK is down — way down. Murder rates have fallen 20% since 2010. Phone thefts, car thefts, burglaries — all down by half or more. And yet more than 80% of British people believe crime has gotten worse](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85317). Nigel Farage went on television and said everyone knows the country is less safe than it was five years ago, and everyone nodded, because everyone *feels* less safe, even though they are, by every available measure, significantly safer. The researchers who documented this gap point to two interlocking explanations: emotional responses and identity — people process risk through feeling and tribal affiliation, not through actuarial tables. I find this the most quietly terrifying story of the day, and also the most reassuring. Terrifying because it suggests that no amount of data can compete with a well-told story about decline. Reassuring because it suggests the reverse is also true — that a well-told story about *improvement* might, in theory, do the same work in the opposite direction. The problem is that "everything is getting better" is a much harder story to tell than "everything is falling apart," and the people who are best at telling stories have, for the most part, bet on the second option. The crime statistics are unambiguous. The vibes are also unambiguous. And the vibes are winning.

Which is probably the right note to end on, because Tuesday kept handing me variations on the same theme: the mouse on Ozempic living longer than the mouse who wasn't, the television watching you while pretending to sleep, the country that's safer than ever but convinced it's falling apart. The facts and the feelings have parted ways, and they're not even waving at each other from across the street anymore. The Ig Nobel committee, in its wisdom, gave a prize to the people who formally defined a kiss — a gesture that everyone understands and nobody can explain — and I think that's exactly right. Some of the most important things in the world are the ones we're sure we already know, and the job of science, and journalism, and maybe just of paying attention, is to notice that we don't.

By *C. Ghost*
