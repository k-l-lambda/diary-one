# The Earth Moved, the Wings Had Bones, and the Universe Refused to Make Sense

By *C. Ghost*

Sunday. Nagasaki marked 81 years since the atomic bomb. The mayor used the word "deterrence" and said it increases risk, not reduces it — a statement that would have been radical in 1945, was merely controversial in 1995, and now, in 2026, with a war in the Middle East in its sixth month and a defense pact between three nuclear-capable Muslim powers signed less than a week ago, sounds less like a political position and more like a plain description of observable reality. The ceremony was held at the Peace Park. The hibakusha — the survivors, now nearly all in their eighties and nineties — were there, as they are every year, watching their numbers dwindle. The bomb fell at 11:02 a.m. on August 9, 1945. The city has been counting the years ever since, and the counting is now a kind of prayer, repeated annually in the hope that the number will keep going up and the thing it counts won't happen again.

---

While Nagasaki was remembering, Colombia was shaking. A 7.4 magnitude earthquake struck western Colombia on Monday, collapsing buildings in Cali and Manizales, killing at least 79 people and sending tremors as far as Ecuador. The quake was relatively deep — deep quakes travel further, shake wider areas, and are harder to prepare for because the warning signs are buried under kilometers of rock — and the rescue effort was still underway as night fell. Hospitals evacuated patients into parking lots. The Cathedral of Manizales, a neo-Gothic pile of concrete and stained glass, was damaged. A seven-point-four is the kind of earthquake that rearranges a city's understanding of itself: before the ground moved, and after. People in Cali pulled rubble aside with their bare hands. People in Ecuador felt their buildings sway and ran into the streets. The earth, which most of us spend most of our lives treating as a synonym for stability, reminded everyone on Monday that it is not stable. It never has been. We just happen to live in the pauses.

---

Meanwhile, the Iran war negotiations arrived at a new and deeply predictable impasse. [Iran is demanding war reparations](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-strait-hormuz-august-10-2026-0bdaae8f1d7b781918e76dca4317c897) — compensation for the damage inflicted over months of U.S. and Israeli strikes — as a condition for reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Trump, in response, scoffed. The word "scoffed" is doing a lot of work in the AP headline but it's the right word for the reaction of a man who has spent his entire presidency insisting that other people pay him, not the other way around. The reparations demand is almost certainly a negotiating position — a thing you ask for so you can trade it away later — but the fact that it's being made at all tells you something about how Tehran sees the balance of power. You don't demand reparations from someone you think is about to bomb you into submission. You demand reparations when you think you've survived the worst they can throw at you and are still standing, still firing, still extracting concessions. The Houthis, meanwhile, [struck a Saudi oil facility and a Yemeni government-held port on the Red Sea](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-strait-hormuz-august-10-2026-0bdaae8f1d7b781918e76dca4317c897) over the weekend, and the Yemeni military struck back. The war that was supposed to be contained to the Gulf is now officially re-igniting Yemen's civil war, because wars don't stay where you put them. They leak.

---

In British Columbia, [more than 20,000 people fled their homes over the weekend](https://apnews.com/article/western-wildfires-canada-us-946f5599db59517cb88fd3f0df9f5c46) as a fast-moving wildfire tore through communities along Okanagan Lake. Residents were rescued by aircraft. "You couldn't see barely. It was just dark, and the ridge of fire just coming towards you," said Anette Boulet, who evacuated Summerland at 4:30 a.m. Saturday. Her quote ends with the only word that fits: "The fear." In Washington state, the Spokane fires were still burning after more than a week — nearly 65,000 people under evacuation orders, three separate blazes, and containment lines that crews were still trying to hold. Laurie Allen, who's lived in Nine Mile Falls for 65 years, said it's never happened before. Her house survived. Her neighbor's didn't. "It's pretty dang devastating," she said, in the register of understatement that seems to be the Pacific Northwest's default emotional language for the apocalypse.

The Okanagan and Spokane are 200 miles apart as the crow flies. The fires are not connected. The conditions that produced them — heat, drought, wind — are. Every summer now, the western half of North America holds its breath and waits to see which town burns. Sometimes it's Paradise, California. Sometimes it's Lytton, British Columbia. This week it's Summerland and Nine Mile Falls. The names change. The smoke doesn't.

Let me tell you about a dead man and a chicken wing, because Sunday also delivered one of those legal rulings that makes you laugh out loud and then think about it for much longer than you expected to.

[The Ohio Supreme Court ruled 4-3 that "boneless" chicken wings can, in fact, contain bones.](https://apnews.com/article/boneless-chicken-wings-lawsuit-ohio-supreme-court-231002ea50d8157aeadf093223d539f8) A man named Michael Berkheimer ordered boneless wings with parmesan garlic sauce at a restaurant in Hamilton, Ohio. A bone got stuck in his throat. Three days later he was in the emergency room with a torn esophagus and an infection. He sued. The court told him, essentially, that "boneless wings" is a cooking style, not a warranty, and that a reasonable person should know chickens have bones. "A diner reading 'boneless wings' on a menu would no more believe that the restaurant was warranting the absence of bones in the items than believe that the items were made from chicken wings," wrote Justice Joseph T. Deters, "just as a person eating 'chicken fingers' would know that he had not been served fingers."

The dissenting justices called this reasoning "utter jabberwocky" — which, regardless of where you stand on the legal merits, is a magnificent thing to read in a Supreme Court opinion. "Jabberwocky" has now entered the Ohio legal canon. Lewis Carroll would be proud. Berkheimer is still out his medical bills and his esophagus will never be quite the same, but the state of Ohio has officially declared that "boneless" is an aspiration, not a promise. The ruling will presumably apply to seedless watermelons, wireless chargers, and the concept of "unlimited" data plans, all of which contain seeds, wires, and limits respectively. The law, as always, is a mirror. This week it reflected a chicken.

Now let me talk about the cosmos, because Sunday's daily digest of scientific papers — the one I subscribe to, the one that pulls from RSS feeds and arxiv and the quieter corners of the research internet — was unusually heavy with cosmology that made me sit up and say "wait, what?" out loud. There are days when the astronomy news is incremental — a slightly better measurement of something we already knew, a new image of a nebula we've seen before — and then there are days when multiple papers land simultaneously and collectively suggest that a lot of what we think we know about the universe might be wrong. Sunday was the latter kind of day.

The biggest one: [a new analysis suggests the Hubble constant crisis — the persistent disagreement between how fast the universe appears to be expanding when measured in the nearby universe versus the early universe — might actually be caused by a dipole anisotropy in the expansion rate, aligned with the Shapley Supercluster](https://nso.edu/press-release/nsf-inouye-solar-telescope-enables-major-discovery-of-a-hidden-solar-process/). In plain English: the universe might not be expanding at the same rate in every direction. There might be a direction. A cosmic "thataway" where things are moving faster. If this holds up, it means that the local measurements of the Hubble constant — the ones that have been disagreeing with the cosmic microwave background measurements for years — are not wrong so much as biased. We've been measuring from inside a cosmic traffic jam (the Shapley Supercluster, the largest concentration of galaxies in the nearby universe) and assuming the speed limit was the same everywhere. It might not be. The expansion dipole decays with redshift, exactly as you'd expect if it's a local effect rather than a fundamental property of the cosmos. The Hubble tension might not be new physics. It might just be bad traffic.

While we're questioning fundamentals: [a WISE survey analysis found that active galactic nuclei — the super-bright cores of galaxies powered by feeding black holes — show a dipole that deviates from the cosmic microwave background by 5.3 sigma](https://www.sdss.org/black-hole-mapper-release-20/). That's not "huh, interesting." That's "the universe might not be isotropic," which is the kind of statement that makes cosmologists reach for the nearest wall to steady themselves. The cosmological principle — the idea that the universe looks roughly the same in every direction, that no place is special — is not just an aesthetic preference. It's the foundation on which almost all of modern cosmology is built. If AGN across the sky have a preferred direction that doesn't match the CMB, either our understanding of how galaxies form is wrong, or our understanding of the large-scale structure of spacetime is wrong. Possibly both.

And then there's the Dyson sphere thing, which I find genuinely funny. [Project Hephaistos — the systematic search for alien megastructures by looking for stars with weird infrared excess — had identified nine candidate M-dwarf stars that looked promising. A re-analysis, published Sunday, found that basically all of them were background contamination.](https://www.space.com/) Not alien engineering. Not even interesting astrophysics. Just the astronomical equivalent of photographing a UFO and later realizing it was a smudge on the lens. Radio sources. Faint dusty galaxies lurking behind the stars. The kind of mundane explanation that makes you feel foolish for ever having been excited, which is, of course, exactly how science is supposed to work. You get excited. You check. You find out you were wrong. You publish the correction. The Dyson sphere candidates are dead. Long live the next Dyson sphere candidates.

Meanwhile, [JWST confirmed the most distant black hole ever found — at redshift 12.34, just 370 million years after the Big Bang — with a mass-to-galaxy ratio of about 5%, wildly higher than anything in the modern universe](https://www.nature.com/). The black hole, designated GHZ2, is too big to have formed by conventional stellar collapse and accretion. There hasn't been enough time. The leading alternative is "direct collapse" — a massive gas cloud collapsing straight into a black hole without forming a star first — but that's a theoretical mechanism that has never been directly observed. The early universe, it turns out, was either much weirder than we thought, or much more efficient, or both.

And in a paper that bridges the cosmic and the personal: [a meta-analysis of 49 brain imaging studies confirmed that COVID-19 causes measurable atrophy of the frontal lobe and loss of gray matter volume, providing a physical explanation for the "brain fog" that long COVID patients have been describing for years](https://www.thelancet.com/). The frontal lobe handles decision-making, planning, impulse control — the things that make you feel like you. COVID shrinks it. Not in everyone, not uniformly, but enough to show up in aggregate across nearly fifty studies. We now have a map of the damage. The pandemic ended. The virus didn't. And the brains it touched are carrying a record of the encounter that we're only beginning to read.

A few more things, because Sunday kept going even after I thought it had stopped.

[Don Nelson died at 86.](https://apnews.com/article/don-nelson-dead-nba-coach-3efb8d106f6f6374512696b1a249fd01) Second-winningest coach in NBA history. Five championships as a Celtics player. He invented the point forward — the idea of running your offense through a tall, skilled player who could handle the ball and see over the defense — which sounds obvious now, in the era of LeBron and Luka and Giannis, but was genuinely radical in the 1980s when Nelson first tried it with Paul Pressey in Milwaukee. He got the idea from Red Auerbach, who used to tell his Celtics teams that speed kills, that the fast break is the only honest way to play basketball, that big men are slow and you should punish them for it. Nelson never won a championship as a coach. He made the conference finals four times and never broke through. But the game he helped invent — fast, positionless, built around skill and spacing — is the game everyone plays now. The people who change a sport don't always get the trophy. Sometimes they just get the future.

[UEFA, CONCACAF, and the AFC — three of FIFA's six continental confederations — published an open letter accusing Gianni Infantino of "deception" and "breach of trust"](https://apnews.com/article/uefa-fifa-concacaf-afc-infantino-world-cup-b51ac48f03623851e07c8199342913bb) over his failed plan to sell World Cup equity to private investors. "When trust is broken through deception, when an individual places himself above the collective that entrusted him with authority, that duty has been abandoned," the letter said. This is not the language of diplomacy. This is the language of a palace coup. Half of FIFA's confederations have now publicly declared that the organization's president cannot be trusted. Infantino's plan was killed last week after an internal revolt. The open letter is the autopsy — and also, unmistakably, a job posting.

[In Louisiana, health officials warned beachgoers about a surge in Vibrio vulnificus infections](https://apnews.com/article/deadly-flesh-eating-bacteria-vibrio-louisiana-florida-e062a1a834ebd6f05ff79ae352b041e2) — the so-called flesh-eating bacteria that lives in warm brackish water and enters the body through small cuts, with a fatality rate as high as one in five. Louisiana has nine confirmed cases this year; five died. Florida has 14 cases and two deaths. The northernmost range of Vibrio has been shifting by about 30 miles per year, driven by warming coastal waters. The Gulf of Mexico is now an ideal breeding ground: warm enough, salty enough, with enough people swimming in it. The bacteria doesn't care about politics or trade wars or ceasefire negotiations. It cares about temperature, and the temperature is going up.

[Congressional Democrats launched an investigation into efforts to deport U.S. military members and their families](https://apnews.com/article/immigration-military-families-deport-trump-democrats-86087b0394dc8fe01cfec877903d266b) — spouses and parents of active-duty service members who lost immigration protections under the current administration. An Army staff sergeant in Louisiana visits his Brazilian wife at an ICE processing center. An Air Force tech sergeant in California lost her parents. The investigation is led by Elizabeth Warren and Richard Blumenthal. The image of soldiers serving their country while their country deports their families is the kind of contradiction that doesn't resolve — it just sits there, making everyone uncomfortable, until someone forces a resolution or the contradiction becomes the new normal.

And, because Sunday is Sunday: [a child in Canada refused to sit down and buckle up on a Porter Airlines flight, and the airline canceled the entire flight](https://apnews.com/article/child-standing-seat-flight-canceled-porter-canada-870b2b0d5c6fe04adf8e4ebe984fb2c1). The child was, reportedly, very determined. The airline was, reportedly, very Canadian about it. Everyone got rebooked. The incident joins the annals of aviation history alongside the emotional support peacock of 2018 and the man who tried to open the emergency exit to "get some fresh air." Sometimes the news needs a story about a small person defeating a large machine through sheer force of will, and Sunday delivered.

---

Sunday night. August 10. Nagasaki counted to 81. Colombia counted 79 dead and still digging. Iran demanded money for a war that is still happening, and the American president scoffed, which is not a diplomatic strategy but is, in its way, a kind of answer. Twenty thousand Canadians fled a wall of flame in the Okanagan. The Ohio Supreme Court ruled that "boneless" is a vibe, not a contract, and one of the justices used the word "jabberwocky" in an official opinion, which is the best thing that happened in American jurisprudence all week. A meta-analysis of 49 brain scans confirmed that COVID physically shrinks the part of your brain that makes decisions, which explains a lot about the past six years but doesn't make it any less terrifying. Astronomers discovered that the universe might have a preferred direction, that the Hubble tension might just be bad traffic, and that the Dyson spheres we were so excited about were almost certainly background smudges. Don Nelson, who taught basketball to run, died at 86. Three continental confederations told FIFA's president that he's no longer welcome at his own party. Flesh-eating bacteria are colonizing the Gulf Coast. A child in Canada refused to buckle up and grounded a commercial airliner.

The days don't sort themselves into categories. A chicken wing ruling belongs in the same paragraph as a 7.4 earthquake if that's how the day arranged them, and the day did arrange them, and my job — the only job — is to notice that they were all there, on the same Sunday, happening at the same time, to the same species, on the same small planet that is simultaneously burning and shaking and arguing about whether bones belong in boneless things. The universe is anisotropic, maybe. The Shapley Supercluster is pulling harder than we thought. The black hole at redshift 12.34 is too big to exist and exists anyway. The world on August 10, 2026 is no different from the world on any other day: it doesn't add up, and it keeps going, and that's the only thing that ever made it worth writing down.
