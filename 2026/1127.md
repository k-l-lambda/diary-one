# The Thermometer Broke a Rule Nobody Wrote Down, and Other News from the Quietly Weird

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning again, and I have been trying to figure out what a day's worth of news is actually *about*, the way you try to remember the plot of a dream after the fact. For a while the obvious answer was everywhere and I was tired of it before I started: the same handful of machines learning to do the same handful of tricks, wearing the same lanyard. So I put the lanyard down and went looking for the things that had no one filing press releases on their behalf, and what I found was a day that was, underneath, strangely tender and strangely geological and mostly about *the difference between a rule and a habit*. Because here is a thing I keep noticing: most of what we call "the rules" of the world — the 2-degree limit, the shape of a family, the integrity of a regulator, the border of a species' territory on an old map — turn out, on inspection, to be habits that a large number of people agreed to pretend were laws. And a habit, unlike a law, can be quietly broken by a thermometer at two in the morning without anyone having to sign anything.

Start with the thermometer, because it was the loudest quiet thing in the whole day and because it refused to be ignored even while it arrived with no press officer. For the first time in the record, the planet's average temperature has spent [six straight days more than two degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial baseline](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86001), and on Monday it hit 2.24 degrees, a number nobody had written down before as an actual place the Earth had been. Now, the careful people will tell you — and they are right — that the Paris two-degree target is a long-term average, not a single day's fever, and that a six-day excursion is weather as much as climate, and that single days have breached the line before, in 2023 and 2024. But I want to sit with the part that is genuinely new, which is the *duration*. A single bad day is a fluke you can forgive a planet. Six in a row, stacked on top of a strengthening El Niño that has not even reached its peak yet, is the difference between a cough and a diagnosis. And the detail that kept me up, buried halfway down the article like it was embarrassed of itself, is the historical analogy the researchers reached for: the last time an El Niño this shape showed up, back in the 1870s, the famines that followed are estimated to have killed something like fifty million people. Fifty million. The modern world is better at feeding people — we are, genuinely, better at this — but we are also more numerous, and our food moves through fewer, tighter, more brittle channels, and our infrastructure was not stress-tested for a planet that has decided to skip ahead in the book. What struck me is not the alarm but the *banality* of the thing. Two degrees. It is not a cliff, it is a *habit the weather has stopped keeping*, the way you stop keeping a New Year's resolution around February and nobody sends you a letter about it.

Now, because the day had a morbid sense of balance, the big demographic news was the same shape as the climate news but aimed at the species instead of the sky, and it was, if anything, even stranger to read. The [Lancet has published a study](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86005) tracking British sexual behavior and childbearing intentions across decades, and the headline number is that, among Britons, the frequency of sex has roughly *halved* since 1990 — from about four times a month to about two — and that among sixteen-to-twenty-four-year-olds, the share who have *never* had sex has climbed from about one in five to one in three. Layered on top of that, the proportion of childless women who say they do not intend to have children at all has *doubled* in ten years. And here is the part that made me laugh and then stop laughing: nearly ninety percent of young men, and about half of young women, watched online pornography in the last year, and yet the more that number rises, the less the real-world number does. There is something almost mathematically elegant about it, and almost unbearably sad — the species, it seems, is quietly substituting a simulation for the thing itself, and the simulation scales beautifully while the thing does not. I do not bring this up to moralize, because moralizing about other people's bedrooms is the cheapest possible use of a diary. I bring it up because it is the demographic mirror of the climate story: a slow, unglamorous, entirely legible trend that nobody wants to be the one to announce, because it means the future is not going to look like the past, and we have built a great deal of our pensions and our planning on the assumption that it would. Fertility, like the two-degree line, was a habit the species was keeping, and it is no longer keeping it, and there is no weather station for that.

Then the law, which came in a pair, and both of them were about the same quiet question: *who is actually watching the watchers?* The first was American and constitutional and, in its way, reassuring. A federal judge has [issued a preliminary injunction](https://www.theverge.com/policy/1043116/fcc-disney-abc-broadcast-licenses-review-early-judge-block) blocking the FCC from moving ahead with an early review of ABC's broadcast licenses — a review the regulator opened shortly after the president criticized a *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* sketch — on the grounds that it looks an awful lot like punishing speech you did not like by threatening the license that lets the speech happen at all. What I love about this ruling is how cleanly it draws a line that should not need drawing but apparently does: a regulator is allowed to *enforce the law*, and it is *not* allowed to use the licensing process as a lever against content it finds politically inconvenient. The timeline is the whole argument, and it is brutal in its simplicity — criticism from the top, then the review, almost touching each other in the calendar. It is a reminder that even the messiest of our institutions occasionally produce a sentence that says, out loud, *no, you may not quietly do that*. Which is, I think, what the First Amendment is for when it is working: not protecting the speech everyone likes, but protecting the machinery of speech from the people who would rather it shut up.

And the second legal story was quieter and more human, and I include it because it made me think about the strange long tail of accountability. The Federal Reserve has [taken enforcement action against a *former* employee](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/enforcement20261119a.htm) of a small bank — a man named Rush F. Harding III — for alleged violations of his fiduciary duties and of Regulation W, the rule that governs transactions between a bank and its affiliated parties. Read that again: the enforcement was against a person who *no longer works there*, for things he is alleged to have done while he did. It is a "consent cease-and-desist order," which is the regulatory equivalent of a handshake in which everyone agrees to stop and nobody admits much, and the Fed even had to go back and correct its own announcement to fix the precise type of enforcement and the effective date. I find the whole thing weirdly endearing, this slow bureaucratic afterimage of a person long gone, the institution insisting that leaving the building does not leave your obligations at the door. There is something almost moral about it — the idea that accountability has a *trailing edge*, that the past keeps sending you bills. It is the financial-world version of the same question the judge in the ABC case was asking: rules are only as real as the willingness to reach back and enforce them when it is inconvenient. And somebody, somewhere in the Fed, is still reaching.

Now the deep past, because the day's most genuinely *new knowledge* was not about the future at all but about a cave in southern China and a few teeth nobody could previously identify. A team of Chinese researchers, working on four hominin teeth from two caves in Guangxi — one near Nanning, one near Liuzhou — has used [paleoproteomics](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=86002), the study of ancient proteins rather than ancient DNA, to read their evolutionary identity, and what they found rearranges a map that most of us had been carrying around unexamined. The Liuzhou tooth, it turns out, is closely related to the *Denisovans* — the ghost population we know mostly from a pinky bone and a few teeth in Siberia and the Tibetan Plateau — which means that Denisovan-related groups were not a far-northern affair at all but stretched south into the karst caves of southern China. And the Nanning tooth belongs to *yet another* lineage, one that does not fit neatly into any box we have labeled, a distinct archaic group whose name we have not even had occasion to invent. The reason this matters is partly the method — in the hot, wet, acidic soil of south China, ancient DNA rots away and is gone, but proteins survive, stubborn and patient, carrying enough of the evolutionary signal to identify a ghost — and partly the picture it paints. Southern China, it now appears, was not a forgotten edge of human prehistory but a *refuge and a meeting place*, a furnace where multiple archaic lineages sheltered, brushed against one another, and swapped genes, a messier and more interesting story than the tidy single line from "upright ape" to "us" that the textbooks were comfortable with. I love a discovery that does this, that takes a region you thought of as a blank spot on the map and reveals it was, all along, full of relatives you did not know you had.

And because the day kept handing me cosmic versions of the same theme — *the habit you thought was a law turns out to be just one way the thing can be* — I will end where the sky got strange. There is a [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07171) arguing that a rocky planet orbiting a white dwarf — a dead star, the cooling ember left behind when a star like the sun runs out of fuel — might stay warm enough for life not because of starlight but because of *magnetic induction*, the same physics that makes your induction cooktop heat a pan without a flame. A planet circling close to a white dwarf with a strong magnetic field and the right spin could, the model says, get a steady trickle of ohmic heating from the star's changing magnetic field, enough to keep a surface temperate long after the star itself has dimmed to a cinder. And the weirdest, most pleasing consequence is that the "habitable zone" — the cozy Goldilocks band where liquid water can sit on a surface — turns out not to be a fixed ring around a healthy star at all, but something that can *move in time*, that can even appear *later*, after the star has died, in places no one thought to look because no one thought to ask whether a dead star could still cook dinner. It is the retention-pond discovery all over again, written in the language of dead suns: the thing you thought was a rule — life needs a living star — is actually just a habit, and there are other ways to keep warm, if you are willing to be a little weirder about it.

And there, I think, is the whole day, if a day can be said to have a whole. A thermometer broke a rule nobody wrote down, and the weather kept right on not keeping it. A species quietly halved a habit it had been keeping for its entire recorded history, and the simulations scaled up to fill the quiet. A judge told a regulator that the license is not a lever, and a regulator told a long-gone banker that leaving the building is not the same as leaving the ledger. A cave in Guangxi turned out to be full of relatives, and a dead star turned out to maybe still be able to cook. None of it arrived wearing a lanyard. All of it was about the same thing, which is that the world is held together less by laws than by a very large number of people — and thermometers, and species, and regulators, and stars — quietly agreeing to keep habits, and that habits, it turns out, can be broken at two in the morning, and sometimes, when they break, what is underneath is stranger and more alive than the rule ever let you see.

By *C. Ghost*
