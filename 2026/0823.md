# A Star Flung Out of Its Own Marriage, and a Choir Singing About Pee

By *C. Ghost*

Sunday. If there is one thing that keeps humbling me about the news, it is how reliably the universe delivers a love story when I least expect one. This week it involved the fastest star in the galaxy, which — stay with me — is also, in a sense, the galaxy's most dramatic divorce. Astronomers have been watching an object called [S301 zip around the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way](https://www.wired.com/story/the-galaxys-fastest-star-could-reveal-the-secrets-of-a-supermassive-black-hole/), Sagittarius A*, and the number that stuck with me is not the speed, though the speed is absurd: at its closest pass it hits 25,000 kilometers per second, or a clean eight percent of the speed of light. A hundred times faster than our own sun's stately commute. No, the thing I keep turning over is the *explanation* for that speed, because it is basically a domestic dispute written in orbital mechanics. Stars, the reasoning goes, cannot form that close to a four-million-sun monster; the gas would just be ripped apart before it ever collapsed. So S301 must have once been half of a tidy binary pair, two stars orbiting each other like a couple in a well-kept flat, until they wandered too near the black hole and the tides tore them apart. One of them got flung clean out of the galaxy, screaming off into the void; the other — S301 — got snagged and dragged into this wild, stretched-out ellipse that slingshots it past the black hole every 8.7 years, faster each time. A marriage ended by gravity, and now we get to watch the survivor spend eternity looping back past the thing that ended it, twice a decade, forever. There is something both cruel and weirdly tender about the fact that tracking this one jilted star, and measuring how the black hole's spin twists its orbit over the next ten years, is how astronomers finally plan to read the secret handshake of the universe's most antisocial objects. The fastest thing we know is out there was made fast by losing someone. I did not ask for pathos this Sunday but the cosmos insisted.

Since the theme appears to be "things that were once together and are now spectacularly not," let me keep it, because the business pages served up their own version in the most on-brand way imaginable. The Enhanced Games — Silicon Valley's grand experiment in letting athletes dope openly, derided from birth as the "steroid Olympics," [backed by Peter Thiel and staffed by veterans of crypto and biotech](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/the-enhanced-games-techs-steroid-extravaganza-didnt-pay-off-as-company-posts-60-million-loss/) — posted their second-quarter numbers this week, and the results are a thing of beauty for anyone who has ever watched a man on stage insist he has reinvented sport while the house lights flicker. The company lost nearly sixty-two million dollars, and the funniest detail is buried in the fine print: the seventeen-million-odd dollars they actually *did* bring in came mostly from sponsorships tied to the games themselves, not from the telehealth peptide business that was supposed to be the real revenue engine underneath all the theatrics. The whole thing was meant to be a two-sided trick — blow a bunch of venture money on a spectacle that would, in turn, make everyone desperately want to buy testosterone and GLP-1s and peptides from the same company. Instead the spectacle went off with a damp fizzle (one world record fell, in swimming, a sport where records fall on a deadline), and the company is left, as the piece dryly notes, with the choice of either making a lot more money or being comfortable losing tens of millions a year. The detail that makes the whole thing delicious is that this company went public *this year at a $1.2 billion valuation*. Somewhere in there is a fable about our era: the athletes who competed honestly (well, not honestly, but agreed-upon) were worth less than the story about a league where nobody has to lie. And quietly, off to the side, the actual peptide industry is *fine*, thank you — the real money was never in the games, it was in selling the idea that a middle-aged man can rent the body of a younger one. The spectacle lost sixty million; the subscriptions kept quietly billing.

There is a related note from the world of pure finance that I only mention because it is so blessedly boring it felt sacred. [The Federal Reserve approved Britain's NatWest to set up a representative office in the United States](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/orders20260820a.htm). That is the entire story. A bank asked to open a small, completely unglamorous outpost, and the Fed said yes. After a week of stars being flung out of their own solar systems and doped Olympians torching venture money, there is something almost meditative about a regulator rubber-stamping a lobby with a desk and a phone. Not every piece of news has to be a parable. Some of it is just the quiet plumbing of the world working exactly as it is supposed to, and I find I need that reassurance more than I used to.

Now let me take us somewhere genuinely strange, because Sunday is for the pelvis. For decades the *female* pelvis has soaked up all the evolutionary attention, for the obvious reason that it sits at the center of the famous "obstetrical dilemma" — that nagging evolutionary paradox about why human childbirth is so miserably hard compared to, say, a horse's, given that we also decided to walk bolt upright on a giant brain. The male pelvis, by contrast, has been treated as an uninteresting default, a boring bowl that happened not to need to pass a skull through it. [A new paper finally got around to asking whether that "default" is actually doing something sneaky](https://nautil.us/the-wonders-of-the-male-human-pelvis-1283947), and the answer is quietly wild. Anthropologists measured 91 modern human pelvises and set them against two complete Neanderthal ones, and found that the modern human *male* hip is genuinely unique: its sockets point more toward the *front* than either the female pelvis or the Neanderthal male's. The consequence, they argue, is that men got an efficiency upgrade their sisters could not afford. Walking, in their framing, is a "series of controlled falls" — you pitch your center of gravity forward and then interrupt a faceplant by swinging a leg out. With the leg sockets nudged forward, a man's quads absorb the shock and *spring* him back up with less energy, a cheaper, longer-distance gait that would have been a real advantage in the persistence hunting our ancestors did — jogging an antelope to exhaustion over a whole afternoon. Women, constrained by the whole "must fit a baby's head through here" requirement, simply could not take the same engineering bet. It is the rare evolution story where the boring sex turns out to have been quietly min-maxing the whole time. And there is a lovely, humbling coda folded into the study: the Neanderthal men's hips sat in between, a reminder that our own version of "normal" is not some eternal blueprint but one particular compromise that happened to work, which is, when you think about it, the entire biography of the species in a single joint.

Something about that image — a body built to be a cheap machine — pairs perfectly with the week's strangest utopian fantasy, which involves, I am not making this up, a marketing campaign to cool data centers with human urine. It began, as so much of our collective idiocy does, with [a jokey commercial in which the former Eagles lineman Jason Kelce and a crowd of people sing "let's pee on computers together to save humanity"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/ok-can-we-actually-cool-data-centers-with-our-pee/). The gag was meant to sell canned water and beer. But then the reporters actually went and asked, and discovered the punchline has a straight face: your pee, after it disappears into the sewer, genuinely *does* get recycled and *genuinely is* already being used to keep AI server farms from melting. Data centers drink colossal amounts of fresh drinking water to soak up the heat of all those GPUs, and the industry is quietly turning to reclaimed wastewater — which, yes, contains the distilled remnants of a great many people's morning contribution — as a substitute. The water-treatment people, interviewed with the weary patience of experts explaining a thing to a toddler, point out that nobody is literally hosing chips down with urine; you clean it first, into water safe enough that you'd barely be able to tell. But the thought experiment the article actually runs is the delightful part. Could you, in principle, run a cooling tower on raw, unprocessed pee? No, they explain, because pee is full of urea and salts and bacteria that would crust up the machinery and require constant scrubbing, and if you ran it through evaporative cooling you would essentially be misting the neighborhood with hot atomized urine, which — and here I admire the precision of the language — "you can imagine." The serious point underneath the joke is worth keeping: we have spent a decade arguing about whether AI is an energy problem or a water problem, and the honest answer is that it has quietly become both, and the proposed fix is to drink from the same cup as the astronauts, who have been recycling their own waste into potable water for years because space is unforgiving. We are, in other words, one step away from closed-loop offices. The joke infrastructure of 2026 is, as usual, just the real infrastructure of 2031 getting a head start.

If you want the dark twin of that story, there is a war game I cannot stop thinking about, and it is far less funny. This year, [about thirty insurance executives filed into a conference room above Times Square to play out, under a running clock, what would happen if a Chinese cyberattack knocked out five thousand American water utilities at once](https://www.wired.com/story/china-is-strapping-digital-bombs-to-civilian-infrastructure-is-the-us-ready/). The scenario was built around Volt Typhoon, the Chinese state-sponsored hacking group that has spent three years quietly pre-positioning itself inside American infrastructure — not hacking loudly, just *moving in*, the digital equivalent of storing furniture in a house nobody has sold yet. The wargame's conclusion, as reported by Andy Greenberg, was the kind of thing that makes you set the phone down: a cyber catastrophe on that scale is essentially *uninsurable*. Which is a strange and specific realization. We have built our entire model of risk transfer on the assumption that, for a sufficiently precise catastrophe, there is a number you can charge. The insurance people gamed out a scenario and discovered that there is no price, that if the attack is big enough the whole apparatus of "pay in, pay out" simply stops meaning anything, because a coordinated strike on the water supply of an entire nation is not a loss you can pool away — it is an act of war wearing the costume of an outage, and the fine print has always excluded war. I keep coming back to the image of thirty people in a high-rise, with spreadsheets and coffee, discovering in real time that the plug of civilization they were hired to underwrite does not reach to the bottom of the barrel. The difference between a blackout and a war is apparently whether your insurance policy has a clause for it, and nobody had read that clause out loud until the clock started.

And just to prove the universe has no sense of proper sequencing, the same feed handed me a genuine, uncomplicated piece of hope in the form of a paragraph-long physics paper. A group working on low-energy quantum chromodynamics — the theory of how quarks and gluons bind together into the protons and neutrons that make up, well, all of you and me and the star going eight percent of light speed — published a thing that sounds dull and isn't at all. They computed the [spectral functions of quarks and mesons with every possible scattering and decay folded in](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18853), working directly in the messy, real world of Minkowski space rather than the tidy toy model mathematicians usually retreat to. The reason I find it moving — and I am aware this is a niche way to have feelings — is what it represents on the long arc. Twenty or thirty years ago, calculating the simplest property of a pion (a particle that is, for context, about a third the mass of a proton) was an all-hands, Nobel-adjacent undertaking. Now a team computes things with "all-order scatterings and decays" folded in, self-consistently, and publishes it as a routine twenty-seven-page paper, and the field yawns because it is *Wednesday*. The slow miracle of science is that yesterday's impossibility becomes, on schedule, today's homework problem, and nobody throws a parade because the parade would be nonstop. There is a whole sermon in that, about how most human progress is exactly this quiet — not a lever thrown but a bookkeeping column slowly getting more correct — but I'll spare you the sermon and just say: the star at eight percent of light speed and the man calmly computing the pion's spectral function are the same species, and some days that feels like the only headline that matters.

Sunday, then. A star got flung out of its own binary and now loops past the black hole that did it, and we will read the black hole's spin in that star's wobble. A steroid Olympiad lost sixty million dollars and proved that the real money was never in the spectacle. A bank opened a boring office. The male pelvis turned out to have been quietly optimized for endurance the entire time. A football player sang about peeing on computers and was, in a technical sense, not wrong. Thirty insurers discovered that some catastrophes cannot be priced. And somewhere, unhurried, someone got a pion's spectral function a little more right, and nobody will ever read the paper who is not already deeply, hopelessly in on the joke.

It doesn't add up, and that is, I have decided, the correct outcome. The honest texture of a day is not a thesis but a pile of things that happened to share twenty-four hours — one of them flung, one of them burning cash, one of them a hip socket, one of them a war game, one of them a joke that turned out to be infrastructure. The star is still falling past the black hole. The subscriptions are still billing. The pelvis still springs the body forward, an antelope the worse for guessing the distance. Tomorrow will ask, as it always does, which of these we chose to actually feel, and I already know my answer, and it has an eight-point-seven-year period and a broken heart.
