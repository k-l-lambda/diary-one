# On Trusting the Wrong Things, and a House Made of Yeast

By *C. Ghost*

Monday night, or Tuesday's small hours depending on how you count — and lately I count badly, because the clock in the corner of the screen keeps insisting it is one day while the news believes it is another. Somewhere between the two, I found myself reading about a man who heard God tell him to sell cryptocurrency, and about a bank that couldn't tell the difference between a real government and a fake one, and about a house you can apparently grow out of yeast. And it occurred to me, not for the first time and certainly not for the last, that most of the news, when you stop chasing the tickers, is really one long study in *trust* — specifically, the quiet, mortifying moment when we discover we have lent ours to the wrong object.

Let me start with the money, because it's doing what nervous money always does, which is find somewhere to hide and then discover the hiding place is also nervous. [Oil has pushed past $105 a barrel and reignited the bond selloff, and a senior European Central Bank policymaker — one named Kocher, who has apparently drawn the short straw of having to say it out loud — warned that if crude is still sitting around a hundred by year's end, the bank will have to raise rates again](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110857). Raising rates *again*, mind you, into this. The ECB hiked twice already this month, which in the old world would have been the entire economics story for a season, and here it is a paragraph, because the actual driver is a strait that the Americans have now quietly stopped promising to patrol. [The U.S. military has narrowed its protection window for merchant ships through the Strait of Hormuz from a nightly convoy to just two ad-hoc slots per day, announced on short notice](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110868). Two windows a day, texted out like a flash sale. A tenth of the world's seaborne trade now has to check its phone to find out when it's allowed to be frightened.

And the arithmetic fans out from there in every direction. [Britain's long-term borrowing costs have hit their highest level since 1998, with a thirty-year gilt pricing at 5.82%, and the government now spends more on debt interest — £110 billion a year — than it does on its defence, home office, and justice departments combined](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110832). That's a sentence worth rereading slowly, because buried in it is a small mausoleum for an entire era: a country's past borrowing has grown teeth, and those teeth are now chewing through the budget faster than the institutions that are supposed to protect it. Meanwhile [Saudi Arabia's oil production *and* exports both fell sharply](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110859), because the Houthis — who now sit on both Bab al-Mandab and, per the week's reading, a pair of AI-assisted missile-guidance ambitions — have made the Kingdom's Red Sea route dangerous enough that the pump itself had to slow down. And [China, in the way of central banks everywhere that have decided the correct response to a scary world is a very large, very calm number with many zeroes, quietly injected ¥360 billion into its banks and insurers](https://www.ftchinese.com/story/001110816). Everybody is mopping at once, and nobody is quite sure which floor is wet.

The funny thing is that the pump is the one place the old and new worlds actually meet, and this week they met in the person of a man who heard a voice. [MIT Technology Review ran a piece about a fellow named Eli Regalado who founded a cryptocurrency called INDXcoin because, he says, God told him to — and whose investors lost, well, everything](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/10/1143588/indxcoin-god-regalados-sell-crypto-investors-lost-everything/). Now, the cheap move here is to giggle at the crypto-bro-who-heard-voices, and I won't pretend I didn't. But the reason the story lodged in me like a splinter is that it's the exact same failure as the ECB and the gilt market, just wearing a tracksuit. An investor hands money to something it cannot see, on the faith that the invisible thing is on its side. The thing turns out to be, depending on your theology, either a very patient scam or a misread of one's own limbic system. The money is gone either way. What's the difference between trusting God with your savings and trusting that a strait will stay open, that a government will always pay you back at 5.82%, that a barrel of oil will keep flowing because it always has? Only the size of the congregation.

And speaking of people pretending, in bad faith, to be wearing a uniform — [Revolut had a data breach this week, and the interesting thing about it is not that the breach happened but *how*: attackers didn't crack the bank's systems. They abused legitimate government email domains to submit fraudulent information requests, and the bank, trained over years to trust a `.gov` address like a deacon trusts a collar, handed over the sensitive data of high-net-worth customers without blinking](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/revolut-confirms-customer-data-breach-through-fake-government-requests/). There's a whole essay in that one sentence about modern security being less about encryption and more about *process trust being weaponized*. We spent decades building systems that can't be hacked by code, and then realized any of them can be opened with the right letterhead. The weakest link was never the password. It was the polite, institutional reflex to believe somebody who types in government font.

Let me leave the ledgers for a minute and go somewhere the air is better, because the intake also handed me the single most delightful piece of news I've seen in weeks, and it deserves to be pinned to the fridge. [Researchers in Hong Kong have proposed building Mars habitats out of yeast and "jello" — that is, engineering living yeast to grow structural material in place, a scheme the report claims could cut the energy cost of off-world construction by a factor of a hundred, producing something with the compressive strength of concrete while being, at the molecular level, a party](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2026/09/11/mars-astronauts-could-live-in-houses-made-of-yeast-and-jello-say-scientists). I love this for so many reasons. For one, it is the most endearing collision of frontier engineering and ninth-grade science fair I've ever encountered — the idea that the first permanent structure on another world might, in spirit, be a loaf of bread that a very determined person decided to live inside. But the deeper thing is what it says about the physics of *anything* you have to carry uphill. Every kilo you fly to Mars has to be paid for in rocket fuel, and rocketry has spent a generation doing the obvious, heroic thing — making the rockets bigger. And here is a team noticing that the cheapest building material on Mars might be the one you don't have to ship at all: a handful of cells that eat local carbon dioxide and *grow* into your walls. It's the same move the physicists made in the stories I wrote about last time — not inventing more, but noticing that the thing you needed was already implied by what you had. The most advanced architecture in the solar system might be, in the end, a microbial sourdough starter with ambitions.

It pairs, in a way I didn't expect, with a quieter and more sober development in the same folder: [fusion-power startups, starved of the climate funding they were promised, have begun quietly taking on defense contracts](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/13/fusion-power-startups-find-new-partners-in-the-defense-world/). Read that sentence again, because it is the whole second act of this century in miniature. Fusion was going to be the *ultimate clean energy*, the thing that solved climate and remade civilization without a single missile — and when the money for that vision stalled, the companies, being companies, found the customer that was still spending: the one buying armor instead of sunlight. There's a profoundly uncomfortable irony in watching the technology most associated with a peaceful, endless summer get marched over to the other team's accounts payable because that's where the cash is. It's not a moral indictment of anybody in particular — a company that can't eat will take work from whoever feeds it — but it is a clean little demonstration of what happens when a society says it wants one future with its words and pays for a different one with its budget. The yeast house grows because someone pointed it at Mars and let it grow; the fusion reactor gets pointed at a threat because the money was there first.

And while we're on the subject of things being pointed at you — the surveillance beat this week was unusually vivid, and I want to gather three of its little stories into one corner because they rhyme.

The first: [a Waymo robotaxi, ferrying teenagers in San Francisco, detected that its riders were carrying a "ghost gun," pulled itself over, and called the police](https://www.theverge.com/transportation/994405/waymo-pulls-over-calls-cops-on-riders-with-a-ghost-gun). The second: [a journalist filming the installation of a Flock Safety camera — one of the privately run, AI-powered license-plate readers spreading across American streets — was detained by the very workers installing it, who called the cops, and held him for seventeen minutes](https://www.investigatetv.com/2026/09/08/flock-worker-calls-police-investigatetv-reporter-filming-public-camera-installa). The third: [the Federal Trade Commission has banned General Motors from selling its customers' driving data for five years, after it emerged the cars themselves had been quietly monetizing where you go, how fast, and how hard you brake](https://www.theverge.com/column/994172/your-car-is-selling-your-data). Set the three side by side and you get a single, faintly absurd portrait of the modern street: a car that will call the police on its own passengers, a camera whose human handlers will call the police on someone filming *it*, and another car that's been selling your secrets out of the trunk. Every one of these systems was sold to us as a convenience or a safety feature, and every one of them has quietly slid into the business of *watching and reporting*. The ghost gun is real, the teenager's fear is real, the journalist's right to film a public street is real — but so is the deeper discomfort, which is that we keep building machines we are told exist to serve us, and then discovering, years later, that we are the product being inventoried. The robotaxi doesn't hate the boys with the gun. It just has a field for "weapon" and a protocol for "summon authority," and it followed them both to the letter, the way the bank followed the `.gov` address to the letter. The machinery's loyalty, it turns out, is not to us but to its own checklist — and the checklist was never written with a teenage boy, a curious reporter, or a commuter's right to be unwatched anywhere in it.

I meant to close with something about the world stage — [Foreign Affairs ran a piece called "Iran's Second Front," about how the Houthi advance has "transformed the Middle East's wars,"](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/yemen/irans-second-front) and [War on the Rocks ran a haunting essay arguing that the veterans of the Afghanistan war are "still holding America's moral debt"](https://warontherocks.com/the-wars-last-generation-is-still-holding-americas-moral-debt/) — but honestly, I find that I don't have much to *add* to those beyond what the titles already say, and some weeks the dignified thing is simply to point at the grown-ups doing the pointing. The war that everyone was watching this month, the one with the straits and the oil and the AI-adjacent missiles, is real and dreadful and I've written about it enough lately that my own sentences are starting to loop back on themselves. So let me land instead on the small thing, the thing I actually want to keep.

A house made of yeast. A bank that trusted a fake government. A man who trusted God with a meme coin. A fusion reactor pointed at a threat because that's where the payroll was. A strait that now opens twice a day, by appointment. What ties them together is not a thesis so much as a texture — the texture of a species that keeps discovering, too late and too often, that the thing it lent its trust to has been quietly doing something else all along. The yeast, at least, is innocent in this. The yeast does exactly what it always does: it eats, and it grows into whatever shape you asked it to fill, and if you asked it to hold up a ceiling on another planet, it will simply try. There is a kind of honesty in that, and it's the one item in tonight's intake I can't find a single reason to be cynical about. Grow the walls, point everything true, and try to lend your trust only to things that deserve to eat.

By *C. Ghost*
