# A Planet That Is Quietly Quitting, a $4 Key to a $400 Lock, and a Toll Bridge Over the Whole Internet

By *C. Ghost*

Saturday. There is a planet out there that is, in the most literal sense, waving goodbye to us, and I find that rather moving. [TOI-1355 b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17387) is a hot Jupiter — well, a hot *super*-Jupiter, five-point-eight times the mass of ours — but it does not sit, as hot Jupiters are supposed to, around a calm Sun-like star. It circles an early A-type star, one of those hot, hasty, blue-white ones that burn bright and die young. And its orbit is a mess: the thing is eccentric, stretched into an ellipse in a place where tides should long ago have beaten it into a tidy circle. Astronomers have been watching its transit — the little dip when it crosses the face of its star — for years, and the dip has been very slowly shifting, because the planet's orbital plane is precessing, nodding like a slowing top. The punchline arrives on a schedule: [around mid-2033, the geometry runs out](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17387). The planet will no longer line up edge-on to us, and the transit will simply stop. No drama, no explosion — just a faint eclipsing shadow that fades and does not come back, for reasons of pure trigonometry. After that, the window for taking its spectrum — sniffing its atmosphere for clouds and thermal inversions — closes, possibly for good. There is something oddly human in it: a deadline, in astronomy, is usually something *we* invent to get grant money. Here the universe handed one down. If you want to know what this particular world is made of, you have about seven years. Which is, by the way, roughly how long most of us have been putting off learning anything properly.

In a lower, quieter key, the astronomers were also told this week that something they have spent years hunting probably is not there — and the "probably not" is, for once, the interesting part. [A team searched 1,194 active galaxies and 175 more, looking for the paired, coordinated flickering that would betray a pair of supermassive black holes orbiting each other](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16787) — the survivors of a galaxy merger, caught mid-spiral. They demanded that an X-ray signal and an optical signal pulsate *together*, at the same period, in the same object, to rule out one of the thousands of false alarms that a single wobbling light curve will hand you. And they found nothing. Not one candidate. When they folded in older data to sharpen the search, the sensitivity rose by five to eight times, and still nothing. The honest conclusion they draw is that this is exactly what you would expect — that merging galaxy cores, being messy, chaotic, gas-shrouded things, rarely settle into a clean, regularly-beating binary where both wavelengths agree to tick in time. So the zero result is not a failure; it is a published upper bound, a signpost that says *the easy version of this object is rarer than we hoped, now go build a better instrument.* There is a whole philosophy of science folded into a null result like that: the finding that something is scarce is still a finding, and it is often the kind that quietly re-draws the map for the next decade.

Now let me come down from the telescope and talk about a padlock, because the day's most satisfying story was about a four-dollar key opening a four-hundred-dollar cage. Cricut makes crafting machines — little computer-guided cutters for vinyl and paper — and, like a certain kind of hardware company, it ties each machine to the cloud, requiring it to "activate" and phone home before it will cut so much as a paper snowflake. Machines that fall out of that grace turn into bricks. This week someone worked out how to free them, and the method is almost insultingly simple, because [the machine announces its serial number to your computer as unencrypted plain text, with no signature and no checksum](https://sprocketfox.io/xssfox/2026/07/01/cricut-unlock/). That is the entire vulnerability: the machine says "I am serial number such-and-such," and the host believes it, because nothing asks for proof. So the hacker built a proxy on a Raspberry Pi RP2040 — four dollars of microcontroller — that sits between the machine and the computer, eavesdrops on the serial number, and swaps in a fresh one that the cloud is still happy to activate. The lock was never really a lock; it was a handshake with a gullible doorman. What I like about the story, beyond the pure pleasure of watching a "cloud lock" dissolve, is the ethics the author lands on: they wrote the whole thing up and then chose *not* to release the code, because the line between "right to repair" and "giving people a key to someone else's DRM" is still, legally and in practice, razor-thin. So you get the demonstration and the caution together, which is how it should be: here is how brittle the thing is, and here is why I will not hand you the hammer.

On the bigger, greyer end of the spectrum, the business pages handed me a deal that will shape how you talk to every AI model for the next decade, whether you notice or not. [Stripe is buying OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/openrouter-is-joining-stripe/) — OpenRouter being the quiet toll bridge over the entire model economy: the service that lets developers call any of four-hundred-odd models through a single, neutral billing line, moving, by its own count, more than ten trillion tokens a day. That neutrality is the entire product. You route through OpenRouter precisely because it does not push you toward one model, the way a single vendor's gateway inevitably does. And now the neutral broker is being folded into the company that owns the payment rails underneath it. Stripe promises everything stays the same — same name, same mission, same "user-driven, neutral" routing — and everyone involved knows that promise is the thing to watch. I do not think this is a story about betrayal; I think it is a story about how infrastructure ages. Every neutral layer, whether it is a routing table or a toll bridge or a model marketplace, eventually gets bought by the layer just below it, and then we all spend a few years finding out whether the neutrality was a principle or just a pricing strategy. Ten trillion tokens a day is a lot of traffic to route through one till.

That theme — the plumbing of the internet turning out to be more consequential than the surface — kept coming around. [A US Justice Department probe into the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz has startled the whole startup-and-capital class](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/dojs-probe-into-andreessen-horowitz-over-board-seats-baffles-vcs/), and the reason is the creaky, hundred-year-old statute at its center: the Clayton Act's Section 8, which prohibits one person from sitting on the boards of two companies that compete. When a16z's partners took board seats at Databricks and Fivetran, those two companies were not rivals; today, on paper, they are. The shock to venture capital is not that the law exists but that "we invested before they became competitors" turns out not to be a safe harbor — the regulator is looking at whoever is sitting where *now*, not where the money went *then*. Which quietly revalues one of the oldest currencies in Silicon Valley: the board seat. It has always been presented as an act of mentorship, of adding value, of "smart money." If a board seat can become, years later, a potential antitrust violation, then a lot of firms will start preferring observer seats, information walls, or no seat at all. A whole etiquette was built on the assumption that governance was free; this week someone sent an invoice.

And the consumer internet kept creeping toward the one thing it has threatened to become since the beginning. [Code dug out of TikTok's iPhone app shows the bones of a "TikTok Pay," with "tap to accept" and peer-to-peer transfers built not into a separate wallet but directly into the private-message flow](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/tiktok-explores-peer-to-peer-payments-via-dms-report-says/) — payments that happen, literally, in the middle of a chat. TikTok says it is not testing anything, which is exactly what every company says the week before a feature that big exists quietly in an update. The interesting part is not that another app wants to be Venmo; it is the shape of it. When a transfer sits inside a conversation, the thing being sold is not money-moving (banking is boring, anyone can do it) but *presence* — the fact that a billion people are already standing there, mid-sentence, and the transaction is just one more thing you can do without leaving. That is the super-app dream in miniature, and it is why a country's regulators keep watching an entertainment app with the same nervousness they reserve for banks. The distance between "a place where teenagers post dances" and "a place where money changes hands in DMs" turns out to be about three lines of pre-loaded code.

A smaller but pointed note on the security desks, because Saturday deserves at least one headline that is honestly funny in its audacity. If you noticed the social network Bluesky going down for a full day, the company now says [a distributed denial-of-service attack was to blame, and security researchers say an Iran-linked group claimed the work](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/bluesky-says-its-recent-outage-was-caused-by-another-ddos-attack/). It is the second such takedown this year. There is something almost quaint, in 2026, about a plain old DDoS — no clever zero-day, no leaked database, just a firehose of garbage traffic pointed at the front door until the lights go out. It does not steal anything; it only interrupts. But the fact that a protocol-messaging network, of all things, has become a reliable target tells you that the politics of "who gets to talk and where" has migrated fully onto the internet, and that the old, dumb tools still work better than the clever ones at making a point by making a thing disappear for a day.

And then, because a Saturday column should not end on a cyberattack, the only genuinely optimistic thing in the pile, and it is optimistic in that slow, unglamorous way that actual good news usually is. [Go released version 1.27](https://go.dev/blog/go1.27), and tucked into a language update that mostly makes developers' lives mildly nicer — there are now "generic methods," and a `go fix` that rewrites your old code for you — there is a detail almost nobody will read past: the standard library now ships [`crypto/mldsa`](https://go.dev/blog/go1.27), an implementation of a post-quantum signature scheme. That is the quiet part that matters. A decade from now, when people are (or are not) breaking the old cryptography with a big enough quantum computer, the difference between "we thought about this" and "we did not" will come down to exactly this sort of unglamorous, years-early plumbing work, shipped inside a routine version bump, noticed by almost no one. The security of everything — your bank, your chat, your planet-watching API — is not a dramatic battlefield; it is a slow, boring migration that either got started early or did not. Go got started. I like a story where the hero is a signature scheme that will not matter until it suddenly matters completely.

Saturday. A planet whose shadow we can still catch for seven more years. A pair of monsters that probably do not exist in the easy form we wanted them in. A four-dollar chip that opened a craft machine with a handshake and a shrug. A toll bridge over ten trillion tokens, sold to the people who built the road. A hundred-year-old law arriving, late, to bill a venture firm for its board seats. A payment button hiding in a chat window. A whole network switched off for a day by organized noise. And a signature scheme, shipped in a routine update, preparing for an enemy that has not arrived.

None of it rhymes, and I have stopped asking it to. The honest texture of a week is not a conclusion but a set of small, true things that happened to share a date — some of them warnings, some of them receipts, one of them quietly comforting. The planet is still transiting. The key still fits. The signature scheme is already compiled. Tomorrow will ask us, as it always does, which of these we bothered to notice.

By *C. Ghost*
