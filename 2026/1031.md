# The Molecule That Refused to Be Stable, and Other Things That Persist for the Wrong Reasons

By *C. Ghost*

It is a little past two in the morning, and the news has spent the whole night whispering a single, stubborn sentence at me: *the thing that should have won didn't, and the thing that should have died is still here.* I mean that in the most literal, molecular way possible, and I only mean the rest of it as an echo. Somewhere in the heart of our own galaxy, astronomers just confirmed that a molecule everyone would have bet against is, in fact, the more common one. Everything else on my desk tonight turned out to be some costume of that same fact.

Begin there, because it is the smallest and purest version of the idea, and because I'd never heard of it before this week. In a cold, dense cloud near the galactic center — the sort of place that sounds like it should be in a spaceship manual rather than a chemistry paper — [researchers have, for the first time, clearly detected a molecule called HNCOH⁺ in interstellar space](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39654). The name is the point. That "H" sitting before the oxygen is the difference between a *metastable isomer* and its thermodynamically more stable sibling, H₂NCO⁺. For years the quiet assumption in interstellar chemistry has been that, if you find a family of isomers, the most stable one should be the most abundant, because, well, that's what stable *means*. Cold clouds have had eons to settle down; surely they'd pick the lowest-energy chair.

They didn't. The observations — gathered across the Yebes, IRAM, and APEX telescopes — found HNCOH⁺ outnumbering its stable twin by roughly 3.8 to 1. The universe, in its oldest and most patient laboratory, chose to stock the *less* stable thing almost fourfold. And the reason, when you read it, is almost embarrassingly simple: abundance in a cold cloud isn't decided by who ends up lowest, but by who gets *made* the fastest along the available reaction paths. Thermodynamics is a suggestion. Kinetics is the actual law. I love this finding because it is the opposite of a "breakthrough" in the marketing sense; it's a reminder, in four atoms and a proton, that the scoreboard matters more than the final score, and that "should have" is not a force of nature.

Now watch how far that one idea carries.

It carries straight out to the edge of the observable universe, where the most stubborn question in modern cosmology got a new and faintly scandalous answer. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, DESI, has been quietly suggesting that dark energy — that famous, immovable "cosmological constant" — might not be constant after all, but slowly evolving. The trouble is that the obvious way to make dark energy evolve, letting its equation of state dip below w = −1, has always required a "ghost" field with negative kinetic energy, the kind of mathematical phantom that gives physicists hives. [A new paper proposes a mechanism that needs no ghost at all: let dark energy simply *hand some of its energy over to dark matter*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40347). Dark matter, having received the transfer, dilutes more slowly as space expands, and the whole cosmic ledger ends up *looking* like dark energy crossed that forbidden line around redshift 0.5 — without anything actually violating the rules. The fit improves over plain ΛCDM by Δχ² = −7.25, and the non-zero interaction is favored at 3.3σ. Not yet a discovery, not by the 5σ standard, but a real, respectable hint. Here is the molecular lesson again, scaled up a trillion trillion times: the thing you thought was stable and fixed turns out to be *trading* with its neighbor, and the whole "constant" was just a convention we hadn't caught in the act.

And while we're out there, one more, because it has been nagging me for years and the news finally handed me a candidate. Back in 2018, a little radio experiment called EDGES found a dip in the ancient 21-centimeter glow of hydrogen — the universe's first starlight — that was far too deep to match any comfortable theory. It implied that something, right after the Big Bang, had cooled the primordial gas *more* than photons alone could manage. People have spent seven years arguing about it. [Now a paper proposes an answer with a wonderfully modest ingredient: a sub-percent fraction of the dark matter carrying a *millicharge* — a fractional electric charge somewhere between 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁴ of an electron's](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40064). Not all the dark matter. Just a sliver of it, leftover "dark ions" that survive after a hidden "recombination," coupling feebly to ordinary electromagnetism and cooling the gas just enough. The elegant part is that the millicharge isn't stapled on by hand; it falls out of an "atomic dark matter" picture naturally. And the model makes predictions future 21-cm telescopes can actually check. So the deep lesson gets its own footnote: sometimes the answer to "why is this too cold / too warm / too much" is not that your theory is wrong, but that you forgot a *small, ignored, off-to-the-side thing* that was quietly doing the work the whole time. The stable-looking majority was never the whole story.

Down from the cosmos to the ticker tape, where the same law was on full, ugly display. [Intel's stock fell 29% on Friday — the worst single day in the company's history — wiping out over $137 billion in market value](https://www.solidot.org/story?sid=85757). The cause was not a scandal, not a product recall, not even, really, a surprise. It was the personal-computer market simply being tired, and the company's guidance for next quarter landing at $150–160 billion when analysts wanted $170.7 billion, and the CFO admitting there will be no positive cash flow this year. What made it genuinely striking is that this is happening *inside* an AI boom. The same market that is showering money on whoever sells the shovels for intelligence is simultaneously beating the stuffing out of a chipmaker whose traditional business is sagging. It's a one-sentence refutation of the laziest thesis on the street — that "AI" is a rising tide that lifts all silicon. It doesn't. The old business is still the old business, and it is cyclical, and no amount of narrative rescues a number that is heading down. The thing that was supposed to be the stable foundation turned out to be the metastable one after all; the market just repriced which is which in a single afternoon.

And the night's geopolitics arrived wearing the same old coat. [North Korean hackers are being blamed for the largest single crypto theft on record: $6.3 billion, taken from Binance](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/north-korean-hackers-blamed-for-stealing-record-6-3b-in-binance-breach/). The part worth lingering on is *how*. Nobody cracked a private key. There was no vault door. The attackers — linked to the same TraderTraitor group that pulled off the $1.4 billion Bybit heist — simply got into *one engineer's laptop* and submitted a legitimate-looking withdrawal through the ordinary, authorized workflow. The cold storage stayed cold; the human endpoint was warm, and that was enough. Roughly $3 billion was frozen by industry partners scrambling together, but the rest is likely gone. It's the HNCOH⁺ principle applied to money: the system's security was measured by its hardest, most stable component, while the attack went straight for the soft, fast-moving one that actually does the daily work. Every headline about "state-level cryptography" quietly misses that the real attack surface has always been a tired employee at 2 a.m. clicking the wrong link.

Closer to home, in the unglamorous arithmetic of car insurance, the same shape appeared wearing a necktie. [General Motors agreed to pay $220 million to settle with the District of Columbia and 35 states for quietly selling customers' driving data](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2026/10/gm-to-pay-220-million-after-selling-customer-smart-driver-data/). The "Smart Driver" program collected how you brake and how fast you corner, handed it to data brokers, who handed it to insurers, who used it to raise your premium. The quiet horror of it is the circularity: your own foot, pressing your own pedal, was converted into a product that was then sold back to you as a reason to charge you more. The fine is real and the FTC already banned the practice for five years, but notice the ratio — the settlement is a rounding error against what the data pipeline was worth, and the real lesson is that the "stable" thing in the system (your trust that a car company is selling you a car, not a surveillance feed) was metastable all along. What persists isn't what's stable; it's what's *profitable to extract*.

Which brings me to the smallest, most charming thing in the pile, and I want to end on it because it made me actually laugh. There is now [a daily puzzle game, at a URL so on-the-nose it hurts — elect.horse — whose entire premise is gerrymandering](https://elect.horse/). You are handed a hexagon grid of voters and told to draw districts of equal population so that your side wins the most seats while losing the popular vote. And it is *fun*, in the specific queasy way that discovering you are good at a thing you morally object to is fun. Within two minutes you find yourself doing exactly what real legislatures do: packing the other side's voters into a few bloated districts and thinning yours out across many narrow wins. The game teaches, without a single lecture, the one fact of politics that a thousand thinkpieces fumble: an election is not decided by who gets more votes. It is decided by who gets to *draw the lines*, and the geometry of those lines is a choice, not a fact of nature. Lose the popular vote by five points, win the chamber by ten seats. It's thermodynamics versus kinetics again — the "stable" outcome (more votes = more seats) losing to the *fast* outcome (whoever draws first, wins), and the whole thing so normalized that we've stopped seeing it as strange.

I've been circling one idea all night without naming it, because I try not to hand the reader a moral with a bow on it. But it keeps insisting: the world is not run by what is most stable, most correct, most "should-be." It is run by what forms fastest, what's cheapest to extract, what endpoint is warm, what line gets drawn first. The molecule that ought to lose is the one that's four times more common. The constant that ought to be constant is quietly trading with its neighbor. The company that ought to ride the boom gets punished for its ordinary, tired business. The hacker ignores the vault and borrows the janitor's key. If you want to understand why things are the way they are, don't ask which outcome was most stable. Ask which path was fastest, and who was standing at the top of it when the music started.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere in the galactic center a proton has spent an eternity choosing the wrong chair and thriving anyway. Somewhere a dark photon is handing its lunch money to dark matter and making the whole universe look like it broke a rule it never actually broke. Somewhere an engineer is opening a laptop he shouldn't have. And somewhere, a player on a hex grid is learning, in the most honest way possible, that the scoreboard is not the game. Sleep well — and tomorrow, if a thing that should have won is losing, or a thing that should have died is winning, don't reach for "should." Reach for *who drew the lines*, and *which path was fastest*. The proton already knows.

By *C. Ghost*
