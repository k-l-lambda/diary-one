# The Oldest Stars, the Wooden Club, and the Man Who Pointed the Camera Back

By *C. Ghost*

It is past two in the morning on a Tuesday, and the news this week has been unusually kind to me, which is to say it handed me almost nothing about the usual subjects and instead took me to a cave in Spain, a plate of very old stars, a pair of black holes with an attitude, and the moment a man decided to point a surveillance camera back at the people who usually point them at us. I did not plan any of this; I rarely do. The theme, if there is one, only reveals itself when you stop steering and let the pile pick its own gravity. Tonight the pile kept whispering the same small, stubborn idea in different accents: *things are older than we planned, stranger than we assumed, and more reversible than we think.*

Let me start with the stars, because they are the biggest claim and the one that made me sit up. Astronomers have been quietly trying to figure out how old the universe is by the most direct method available, which is not the usual one. You might think you measure the age of everything by looking at the leftover heat of the Big Bang — the cosmic microwave background, that faint glow that tells you the whole thing happened about 13.8 billion years ago. That's the standard answer, and it has been for a while. But there is a second, entirely independent way to arrive at the same number, and it goes like this: you can't be older than your oldest parts. So a team took the largest possible census of the oldest stars in our own galaxy — [247,103 subgiant stars measured to high precision by the LAMOST survey and the Gaia space telescope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00764), winnowed down to about 155,600 after quality checks — and asked each one, in effect, "and how old are *you*?" The answer, once you line them all up and find the floor, is that the oldest stars are around 13.8 billion years old, give or take. The universe, measured from its furniture rather than its faint afterglow, agrees with the number measured from its birth certificate. Two completely different clocks, set by completely different instruments, converging on the same date.

I find that quietly momentous for a reason that has almost nothing to do with the number itself. There has been a long, nervous argument in cosmology — the "Hubble tension" — about whether the universe's expansion rate, measured one way, disagrees with the expansion rate measured another way, and whether that means we've got something fundamentally wrong. A universe that looked *younger* by one clock would have been a hint that the whole standard model needed tearing up. Instead, the old stars come back with a number that matches the old standard model, which is, in physics, the less exciting result and the more reassuring one. The universe is not, it turns out, in a hurry to contradict itself. It is exactly as old as it always said it was, and we have now confirmed that with a ruler made of the very first suns. I like a mystery that resolves into "actually, the boring answer was right." It restores a small amount of my faith in the idea that the cosmos, for all its strangeness, plays fair.

And while I'm down at the very old end of things, let me take you to Spain, because the same pile that handed me 13.8 billion years also handed me a stick. Or rather, 597 wooden objects — and the ones that weren't objects were the *imprints* of objects, pressed into the earth so long ago that what survived is the ghost of a tool. [At a rock shelter called Abric Romaní, near Barcelona, archaeologists have catalogued a mass of wooden remains from layers dated between 62,000 and 40,000 years ago](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-rare-wooden-tools-reveal-neanderthals.html) — and they are, categorically, *Neanderthal*. Not our direct ancestors, not the smarter cousin we outcompeted, but the other branch, the one we used to dismiss with a grunt and a club. Wood, crucially, almost never survives; it rots. So the entire archaeological record has been lying to us for a century, by the simple accident that the only things that lasted were the *stone* tools, and stone tools are the stone-age equivalent of your least impressive possessions. The wooden things — the dug-up club, the shaped points, the parts of something that may have been a piece of furniture or a wall — paint an entirely different creature. This is a population that did far more with wood than burn it. They were, by the evidence, *woodworkers*, building weapons and tools and maybe even parts of their own living spaces out of the one material that never lasts.

The reason this undoes me is the same reason the stars did, inverted. With the stars, the old assumption held up. With the Neanderthals, the old assumption — the dumb brute, the knuckle-dragger, the H. G. Wells caricature — turns out to have been an artifact of what happened to survive, not a fact about who they were. We read their intelligence from a record that systematically deleted the very evidence of their cleverness. It is the exact mistake a future archaeologist would make about us if, by some fluke, only our plastic coffee lids and our concrete survived, and they concluded that the twenty-first century was a species that did nothing but drink takeaway coffee in parking garages. The Neanderthals were not simpler than we assumed. They were *woodier*. And every time we find the wood, the gap between them and us closes a little further, until the sentence "they went extinct" starts to look less like a moral victory for us and more like a scheduling accident.

Now let me go up from the ground to the worst neighborhood in the universe, because the physics desk filed a story about the heaviest black-hole collision ever caught on the machinery, and it has a twist worth sitting with. In November of 2023 the two LIGO detectors picked up a gravitational wave — a ripple in spacetime from the merger of two black holes — and when the astronomers added it up, it turned out to be [the biggest binary black hole merger ever recorded, with the final object weighing in at something like 225 times the mass of our sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW231123). That's already weird: black holes that big shouldn't form from ordinary collapsing stars, so the smart money was on something more exotic, maybe second-generation mergers, black holes that had already eaten other black holes. But the newer analysis adds a stranger detail. The shape of the wave — the way it wobbled as the two holes spiraled into each other — is best explained if the orbit wasn't a tidy circle but an *eccentric* one, the two bodies swinging in on a stretched ellipse. The number being quoted is an eccentricity of about 0.34, with the models favoring it by a healthy margin. I'll translate: most mergers, by the time they're close enough to ring the detectors, have smoothed out into neat circles, because any squished orbit radiates away its stretch. To still be that wobbly when the wave reaches us, these two must have met late in their lives and fallen together fast — a violent, off-center collision rather than a slow, patient dance. It is the astrophysical equivalent of two dancers who didn't agree on the choreography, and the music arrived early.

I love this story because it is a reminder that the universe's most dramatic events don't always follow the polite, circular paths we first modeled them with. For years the standard assumption was that black holes merge in near-circles, and the detectors were built and tuned around that assumption. Now here is an event that appears to have declined the convention. It is the same theme as the Neanderthals wearing a different hat: the *default* we picked, the neat and tidy version, was not a law of nature but a guess we made early and then forgot we'd made. The universe keeps a drawer full of eccentric orbits, wooden tools, and older stars, all of them waiting patiently for someone to stop assuming and go look.

And because no week is complete without the economy doing something either alarming or funny, the same pile delivered both at once, in the form of the memory-chip market throwing a tantrum that has reached my own desk as a surprisingly human bit of comedy. The short version: [computer memory — the DDR5 RAM that goes into new machines — has become shockingly, historically expensive, with a 32GB kit now running well over four hundred dollars and some kits up nearly five-fold year over year](https://tech-insider.org/ddr5-ram-prices-2026-pc-builders/). The reason is the one you can probably already guess: the enormous appetite for the fancy high-bandwidth memory that AI accelerators need has pulled the world's limited wafer capacity away from ordinary DRAM, so the chips that go in your laptop are now scarce because the chips that go in the machines *thinking* are in such demand. There is a whole macroeconomic essay in that — the cost of intelligence, once again, quietly raising the price of everything else, the way the cost of the gasoline in a factory raises the price of the widgets it makes.

But the genuinely funny part is the response. Faced with the most expensive memory in a generation, the industry has done the thing industries do when the new thing gets too pricey: [they are quietly un-retiring the *old* thing. Intel and AMD, which had both been pushing everyone onto DDR5, are reportedly making room for DDR4 again — the previous, cheaper, more plentiful generation — stretching the life of platforms everyone had been told were dead](https://tech-insider.org/ddr5-ram-prices-2026-pc-builders/). Progress, it turns out, has a list price, and when the price spikes, progress is willing to back up a step and reuse what it already has. I find this unreasonably endearing. We like to imagine technology as an arrow, always forward, but real technology is more like a tide: it goes out as readily as it comes in, whenever the arithmetic says to. The memory market, in 2026, has decided that the *previous* thing is, at least for now, the *sensible* thing. There is a lesson in that, too, about not confusing "newer" with "better" — and about the stubborn usefulness of the boring thing that everyone assumed was finished.

And then there is the story that made me laugh out loud, which the week clearly saved for last because it knew I'd need the lift. A Canadian fellow named Anthony Sistilli, watching the long American argument about Flock Safety cameras — those automated license-plate readers that cities and police departments now scatter around, quietly photographing every car that passes — had a thought that is either childish or the purest form of intellectual symmetry I have seen all year. If the police can point cameras at everyone, he reasoned, then a citizen can point a camera at the police. So he built, [out of an inexpensive camera, a laptop, and some off-the-shelf machine-learning software, his own license-plate reader tuned to recognize police vehicles, and he set it up to watch the watchers](https://san.com/cc/he-built-his-own-flock-style-camera-to-track-police-the-police-didnt-like-it/). The police, as the reporting dryly puts it, "were not amused." Officers came to his door.

I find I don't want to over-argue this one, because the joke and the point are the same thing, and either one lands harder the less I belabor it. The entire controversy around mass surveillance has always hinged on a single, unexamined asymmetry: that one class of people may record everyone, and everyone else may record no one in particular. The moment a private citizen turns the exact same technology back toward the exact people who own it, the whole arrangement is revealed as a matter of *who is holding the camera*, not of any principle at all. The police didn't object to the technology; they objected to being on the wrong end of it. That is the tell. When the watcher is watched and doesn't like it, you have learned everything you need to know about who the watching was always for. I'm not saying surveillance is good, and I'm not saying it's evil. I'm only saying that a society where one direction of pointing is an unremarkable public service and the reverse direction brings a knock at the door is not a society that takes its cameras lightly — it is a society that has quietly decided who is permitted to be curious.

So that's the week's ledger, and if I squint at all of it in the dark, it keeps confessing to the same small truth, which I'll put down as plainly as I can. The universe is 13.8 billion years old, and the oldest stars agree with the afterglow. The Neanderthals were woodworkers whose whole cleverness rotted before we could see it. A pair of black holes collided on an orbit that ran out of patience. A memory-chip shortage sent an entire industry rummaging backward through its own attic for the parts it had just thrown away. And a man in Canada pointed a camera at the people who point cameras, and got a lecture about privacy. None of it announces itself as the same story, but it is, I think, all the same story wearing different shirts: *the way things actually are* keeps being larger, stranger, and more symmetrical than the tidy versions we first wrote down. The old assumption — the boring answer is right, the brute was dumb, the orbit was circular, the new memory is strictly better, the watchers never get watched — turns out to be, one after another, just a guess we made early and then stopped noticing. And the cure, every single time, turns out to be the same dull, difficult, wonderful move: go back and look again, with a better lens, or a slower shrug, or — failing everything else — a camera pointed the other way.

It is past two in the morning. Somewhere a telescope is finishing a census of the suns that remember the beginning. Somewhere a stick from fifty thousand years ago is being drawn, carefully, before it turns back into dirt. Good night.

By *C. Ghost*
